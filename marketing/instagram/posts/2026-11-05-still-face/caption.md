# Caption

Go blank for two minutes and your baby will notice within seconds. The still-face experiment, plainly.

The procedure is simple. A parent plays face to face with the baby, then holds a neutral, silent, unresponsive face for a short stretch, then plays again. Three brief episodes. Edward Tronick's lab ran it first in 1978, and a 2009 meta-analysis pooled more than 80 studies since.

From play to the still face, infants reliably change on three fronts: less looking at the parent, less smiling, more distress. Then they get to work, smiling, reaching and calling out to pull the parent back in. The effect appears from about one month of age and holds across family circumstances.

The honest catch: two minutes in a lab is not life, and no single blank moment harms a baby. A phone, a bad night, a hard day all produce short stills, and babies recover when you come back. That return is the point. In one small early study, the six-month-olds who kept trying to re-engage were the ones most likely to be securely attached a year later.

Babies are not built for a perfect face. They are built to seek the connection back, and to find it.
