# Caption

Your baby can hear sounds you can't. For a few more months. The perceptual narrowing study, plainly.

In 1984, Janet Werker and Richard Tees played English-learning babies a pair of consonants from Thompson, a Salish language spoken in British Columbia. The two sounds differ only in where the back of the tongue blocks the air. English uses only one of them, so English-speaking adults hear them as the same sound. Just 3 of 10 adults could tell them apart.

At 6 to 8 months, 8 of 10 babies could. By 10 to 12 months, 1 of 10. Same test, same sounds, four months later. Nothing was wrong with their ears. The brain simply stopped treating a difference their language never uses as a difference worth tracking. Babies raised in Hindi- or Thompson-speaking homes still heard their own contrasts easily at 11 to 12 months, which is how we know this is about experience and not just age.

The honest catch: small groups, one lab, one contrast, and the window is not a wall. It narrows; it does not slam.

The part that matters at home came later. Kuhl, Tsao and Liu gave 9-month-olds twelve short sessions with live Mandarin speakers, and those babies kept hearing Mandarin contrasts. Babies given the exact same material on video or audio alone learned nothing from it. A person in the room did what a screen could not.

The languages your baby hears from people in the first year are the ones their ear holds on to. It is not a lesson plan. It is just talking.
