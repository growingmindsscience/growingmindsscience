# Caption

It is not how many words your child hears. It is how many they exchange. Here is the study, plainly.

At MIT, 36 families gave their four- to six-year-olds a two-ounce recorder to wear through two full weekend days. It logged every adult word and every back-and-forth exchange, defined simply: one person speaks, the other replies within five seconds. Then each child listened to stories in an MRI scanner.

Word count mattered less than expected. The number of conversational turns predicted verbal skill more strongly than the number of adult words heard (r = .51 against r = .36), and only turns predicted activation in Broca's area, the core language region, while the children listened. That held after family income, parental education, IQ and the sheer amount of speech were accounted for. Every 11 extra turns an hour went with a one-point bump in verbal score, whatever the family earned.

The honest catch: 36 children, measured once, is a correlation. What makes it worth acting on is the follow-up. The same team ran a randomized trial in which families coached into more turn-taking saw their children's language gains follow, and an independent study of 122 infants links turns to vocabulary growth from 9 to 24 months.

So the cheapest language intervention there is: ask, then wait. Build on whatever comes back.
