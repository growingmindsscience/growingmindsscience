# Caption

"Baby talk" gets mocked a lot. It also survived the biggest stress test in infant research. Here it is, plainly.

In ManyBabies 1, 67 labs across four continents ran the same experiment on 2,329 babies aged 3 to 15 months. Every lab used identical audio: mothers recorded talking to their own infant, and the same mothers talking to an adult. Trials alternated, and each lab timed how long the baby kept listening.

Babies listened longer to the infant-directed recordings. The effect was reliable, and at d = 0.35 it was roughly half the size the older published literature had reported (d = 0.67). It was stronger in older babies, stronger when the accent matched the baby's own, and stronger with the head-turn method. When the old meta-analysis was corrected and expanded, it landed on d = 0.35 too: two routes to the same number.

The honest catch: the measure has almost no test-retest reliability (r = 0.09), so this is a group finding, not something you can run on your own child. And a follow-up found no link between a baby's speech preference and their vocabulary at 18 or 24 months.

What infant-directed speech is: higher pitch, wider pitch swings, shorter phrases. If you have ever talked to a baby, you already do all three without having been taught. The takeaway is not "do baby talk or else". It is that the way most adults already shift their voice around infants is genuinely more interesting to babies than adult speech, and that a field willing to re-test its own classics at this scale is one worth listening to.
