# Caption

Fed, changed, rocked, and still crying at 6 weeks. Here is how much newborn crying is normal, and how common colic really is.

Wolke and colleagues (2017) pooled 28 diary studies of 8,690 babies. Fussing and crying averaged about 2 hours a day in the first 6 weeks, as parents recorded it, and about 68 minutes at 10 to 12 weeks. Colic affected 17 to 25% of babies in the first 6 weeks and 0.6% by 10 to 12 weeks.

It is not a verdict on your care. Persistent crying at 6 weeks usually happens even with sensitive, affectionate mothers (St James-Roberts, 1998). And most heavy criers at 5 to 6 weeks were settled at night by 12 weeks (St James-Roberts & Peachey, 2011).

The catch: these are parent-diary numbers. A small recorder study of 20 babies measured far less crying.

Babies this young have few ways to calm themselves. Looking away is their main one, and much of the rest they borrow from you. Try dim lights, walking while you hold them, and trading off with another adult. At your edge, lay your baby on their back in the crib, step out, breathe, and go back when you are steady.

Worth asking your pediatrician about: crying not easing by about 3 months, or anything that feels wrong to you. If crying comes with a fever, poor feeding or vomiting, or sounds unlike your baby's usual cry, call rather than wait.
