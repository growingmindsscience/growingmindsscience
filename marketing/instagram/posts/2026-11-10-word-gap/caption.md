# Caption

"Poorer children hear 30 million fewer words by age three." You have probably seen the number. Here is what happened when researchers checked it.

It comes from one 1995 study. Hart and Risley recorded 42 families for one hour a month and extrapolated to a 30-million-word difference by age three between the richest and poorest homes. It became a headline, then a policy, then a quiet source of guilt for a lot of parents.

When others looked again, it did not hold up as stated. A 2019 study that recorded whole days, and counted everyone in the home rather than only the mother, found no such gap. A 2022 meta-analysis found a difference that is real but far smaller, and that moves depending on what you count.

The more useful finding came from a different direction. In a 2018 study of 36 children, the number of back-and-forth exchanges predicted language skill better than the number of words heard, and only the exchanges predicted activity in the brain's language areas. A later randomized trial backed the direction.

So the correction is freeing. You do not need to monologue all day. Answer your baby's sounds and gestures as turns, then wait for the next one.
