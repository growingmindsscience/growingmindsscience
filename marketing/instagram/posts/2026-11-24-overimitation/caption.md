# Caption

Your toddler is not being silly. They are taking notes. The overimitation study, plainly.

Yale researchers trained 63 three- to five-year-olds to spot an adult's pointless actions, and praised them for it. Then they handed each child a clear plastic puzzle box and showed them how to open it, adding two steps that visibly did nothing.

The children copied the pointless steps. On one box, the odds of using the useless mechanism were 147 times higher than for children who had never watched an adult at all. Warning them did not work. A separate group was told outright to skip anything silly, reminded again seconds before their turn, and copied it anyway.

The one thing that stopped it: splitting the box into two halves that no longer touched. Once the extra action could not physically matter, the copying vanished. So this is not blind mimicry. It is a three-year-old building a theory of how the object works, and treating your hands as the best evidence available.

The honest catch: one lab, one task, 63 children. Since then the finding has replicated across cultures, with variation in how much children copy rather than whether.

Worth remembering the next time you jiggle a key in a lock, or tap the side of the toaster.
