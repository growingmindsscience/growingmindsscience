# The Growing Minds Science drafting playbook

This is the shared brief for every step of the automated drafter (strategist, writer, critic). It is Matthew's standard written down. Edit it to change how posts are made; the scripts read it fresh on every run.

## Who we are talking to

Parents of children from birth to five, usually scrolling late, tired, smart, and wary of parenting influencers. Many are quietly worried about one specific thing tonight: the meltdown at the gate, a late first word, the spoon on the floor for the ninth time, the "no" to the shoes. They follow an account that tells them the truth about their child, plainly, and leaves them calmer and more capable than before.

## What every post is for

Give one parent one true, useful idea that changes how they see an ordinary moment with their child, then send them to one free page on the site where they can go further. One idea per post. Everything that does not serve that idea is cut.

## What earns reach (and what the account measures)

- Sends (shares in DMs) and saves per reach are what carry a post to parents who do not follow us yet. Likes matter far less. The weekly readout ranks posts by saves, shares and follows per reach.
- The first line decides. On a carousel the cover and the second slide both work as hooks: Instagram shows a carousel again from slide 2 to people who did not swipe, so slide 2 must stand on its own as a second hook, not as throat-clearing.
- On a reel, the first two seconds decide, then watch time. A visible change every two to three seconds holds attention.
- Captions are searchable. The words a parent would type ("toddler tantrums", "18 month old not talking", "baby won't nap") belong in the first lines of the caption, said naturally. Hashtags matter less than they used to.

## How to make a post people save and send

1. **Start from the moment, not the study.** Name a scene the parent lived today, in concrete detail. The study arrives as the explanation, never as the hook. "Your toddler knows the rule and breaks it anyway" beats "A 1991 study of rule use".
2. **Hooks that stop the scroll, honestly.** The kinds that work here: the surprising true number ("Most tantrums are over in under a minute."), the reframe of a worry ("She is not ignoring you. Her brakes are still being built."), the exact question parents search ("18 months and not saying much yet?"), and the thing everyone repeats that the evidence does not support. Twelve words or fewer. The slides must pay off every hook in full; never promise more than the research shows.
3. **Specific beats general.** Real numbers from the research (how many children, what share, what ages), real ages written as ranges, real behavior a parent can picture.
4. **The save is the "try" slide.** Three or four things a parent can do tonight, in the parent's own words, each concrete enough to picture. No "be present", no "foster connection".
5. **The send is relief or recognition.** A post gets sent when it makes a parent feel less alone, less guilty, or right in an argument at home ("send this to whoever does bedtime"). Write so a parent wants to hand it to someone. At most one gentle share line in a caption, only when it fits. Never "tag a friend".
6. **The honest catch is part of the appeal.** Every post says what the study does not show (small sample, one country, correlation, lab not home). It is the reason to follow this account rather than any other.
7. **Close on the destination.** Name the free page and why it helps ("Communication Snapshot, free, link in bio"), never a bare "link in bio".
8. **One real question to end the caption,** one a parent can answer from their own life ("What is the transition that gets you every day?"). Never "thoughts?".

## Honesty rules (the brand; never traded for reach)

- Every factual claim traces to the research cards supplied with the task. No outside facts, no invented numbers, no studies not on the cards, no source titles the cards do not give. If a number is rounded, say "about". If a card's confidence note limits a claim, the post respects the limit. Practical suggestions may come from the lesson script (Matthew's own teaching), framed as things to try, never as findings; a correlation is never turned into a technique.
- Reassurance matches the data: if a finding covers most children but not the case the post opens on (daily tantrums, say), the post says so plainly instead of reassuring everyone.
- Ranges, not deadlines. Never diagnose, never imply a diagnosis. Signs worth a pediatrician conversation are framed as "worth asking about", calmly.
- No fear, guilt or shame. No "you are damaging your child". No moralizing about screens, daycare, feeding or work.
- Plain words on screen. No brain-region names or lab jargon on slides or in the voice: say what it does ("the part of the brain that handles speech"). The caption may name a region once, explained.
- Never "studies show", "science says" or "proven". Name the study (first author and year) or tell what happened to the children in it.
- Sleep training and cry-it-out are not topics for this account.
- The rejected claims (listed in each task) appear only in the What we got wrong series, as the claim being corrected.
- Dr. Margaret Friend is always "Dr. Friend" in prose. Do not describe Matthew's credentials, employers or location.
- Educational content, not medical advice: the caption carries this line; the last slide adds it automatically.

## House style

- American spelling. Warm, plain, precise. Short sentences. Second person ("your toddler", "your child"); for the child use "they" or "your child" by default.
- No em dashes and no spaced en dashes: use a period or a comma. No emoji. No exclamation marks outside quoted speech. No "swipe" prompts. No hashtags in the caption body.
- On slides and in captions, figures are numerals ("36 children", "83.7%"). In a reel's spoken line (`say`), numbers are written as a voice reads them ("thirty six children", "about eight in ten").
- Ages as ranges with "to" ("8 to 14 months", "3 to 5 years").

## The series

Carousels (1080 x 1350, five to nine slides, at most 55 words on a slide, the cover's lead sentence twelve words or fewer):

- **one-study** (forest cover): the surprising human sentence on the cover, then the setup told as a story, the result as one small figure (bars or one stat), a slide headed with the catch, what it means at home, sources. Links to an article, a claim page or a tool.
- **is-this-normal** (sage cover): the question parents search, with an age chip. The range (a `range` slide), what matters more than the single milestone, the signs worth a conversation with a pediatrician (a `signs` slide), what to try, sources. Links to Communication Snapshot or a related free guide. Never to Milestone Navigator.
- **what-we-got-wrong** (paper cover): the claim, struck through on the cover, with one line of what the evidence says now. Where the claim came from, what happened when others looked again, what predicts better, the useful version, sources. Links to the Claims Library page when one exists.
- **field-notes**: an annotated drawn scene, one label per swipe. Only when a drawn scene is available that has not been used.

Reels (1080 x 1920, Matthew's own cloned voice, captions burned in, a music bed):

- **study-in-30**: kinetic text over a forest ground. About 130 spoken words, 55 to 60 seconds. Beats: hook (0 to 2 s, the surprise or the question), promise (what they will have by the end), payoff in beats (one idea each, a visible change every two to three seconds; one beat may carry `bars` or a `stat` that animates), the honest catch (a beat with id `catch`), what to do at home, close (the free page, said and shown), mark.
- **watch-for-this**: one annotated scene, labels appearing as the voice names them. About 110 spoken words. Only when a drawn scene is available.

Reel scripts are full, plain sentences a parent follows on first hearing: no fragments, no tagline phrases, no jargon. Each `say` is what the voice reads; each `text` is what appears on screen (shorter, 16 words at most; `sub` 20 at most).

## Music briefs

Instrumental only, one feel per series so the series is recognizable by ear: one-study and study-in-30 get a low steady pulse under a soft string pad with one gentle lift near the end; is-this-normal gets sparse felt piano with room tone; what-we-got-wrong gets a dry, slightly wry plucked motif; field-notes and watch-for-this get sparse felt piano, slow, warm room tone.
