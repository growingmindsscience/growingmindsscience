# 09 · The marshmallow test does not predict what you were told it predicts

- **Suggested post date:** Friday, 2026-08-07
- **Type:** Myth correction
- **Source:** Watts, Duncan & Quan, *Psychological Science*, 29, 1159-1177 (2018)
- **Status:** draft, not published

---

A four-year-old sits with a marshmallow and is told they can have a second one if they wait. Some wait. Some do not. The waiters, the story goes, grow up with better grades, better health, better lives.

It is one of the most repeated findings in psychology, and it has been used to sell a lot of things to parents.

In 2018, Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan ran a conceptual replication with a much larger and more socioeconomically diverse sample than the original studies, and controlled for family background and early cognitive ability.

The association did not vanish, but it shrank a great deal. Much of what delay of gratification appeared to predict was carried by the circumstances the child came from rather than by the willpower the task was thought to measure.

Which makes sense once you say it plainly. Whether waiting is a good bet depends on whether promises in your environment have historically been kept. A child who has learned that a second marshmallow reliably arrives is making a different calculation than a child who has learned otherwise. That is information about the world, not a character flaw.

I bring this up because the marshmallow story has a moral built into it, and the moral points at the child. Weak willpower, poor self-control, a deficit to be trained out at four years old.

The replication points somewhere else. If you want a child to get better at waiting, the most useful thing in your control is being predictable. Not drilling the waiting.

Watts, Duncan & Quan, *Psychological Science*, 29, 1159-1177 (2018).
