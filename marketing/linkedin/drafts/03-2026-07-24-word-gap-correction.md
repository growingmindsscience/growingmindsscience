# 03 · The "30 million word gap" is not a settled finding

- **Suggested post date:** Friday, 2026-07-24
- **Type:** Myth correction
- **Sources:** Hart & Risley (1995); Sperry, Sperry & Miller, *Child Development*, 90(4), 1303-1318 (2019)
- **Status:** draft, not published

---

You have almost certainly met this number. By age three, children in lower-income homes are said to have heard 30 million fewer words than children in professional families. It has shaped curricula, public health campaigns, and a great deal of guilt.

It came from Betty Hart and Todd Risley's 1995 work, which followed 42 families. Forty-two. The 30 million figure was an extrapolation from monthly hour-long recordings in a small sample, projected out across four years.

In 2019, Douglas Sperry, Linda Sperry and Peggy Miller looked again across a wider set of communities, and did not find the gap. Part of the reason is what gets counted as language. The original method centred speech directed at the child by a primary caregiver in the home. In communities where children spend their days inside multigenerational talk, or amid the speech of many adults and older kids, a great deal of real language exposure is invisible to that method.

So the honest position today is that the size, and possibly the existence, of a 30 million word gap is contested.

I am not raising this to score a point about an old study. I raise it because of what the number does to parents. It converts a relationship into a quota, and then tells a specific set of families that they are already behind by an amount they can never audit.

What does hold up is narrower and more useful: responsive back-and-forth with a child matters. That is not a word count. It is a loop, and it is available to everyone.
