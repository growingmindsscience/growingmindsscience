# 12 · Most toddler meltdowns are transitions, not defiance

- **Suggested post date:** Friday, 2026-08-14
- **Type:** Practical activity
- **Related site page:** /tools/routines-transitions-night-waking
- **Status:** draft, not published

---

If you logged every hard moment in a week with a young child and sorted them, a striking number would fall in the same place. Not the middle of an activity. The seam between two of them.

Leaving the park. Turning off the show. Coming to the table. Getting in the car seat. Bath to pyjamas. Awake to asleep.

That is not a coincidence and it is not personal. Transitions ask for three things at once from a brain that is still assembling all three: stop what you are absorbed in, tolerate the feeling of it ending, and start something you did not choose.

Adults do this badly too. We just have more practice and better cover stories.

A few things that reduce the friction, none of which are new:

**Warn against events, not clock time.** "Two more turns on the slide" is legible to a four-year-old. "Five more minutes" is not.

**Keep the sequence identical.** The predictability is the intervention. When the order never changes, the child's body starts making the transition before you ask.

**Name the feeling and hold the limit at the same time.** "You wish we could stay. It is hard to leave. We are going now." All three, in that order. The first two are not concessions.

**Give the leaving a shape.** Wave at the slide. Say bye to the ducks. A ritual gives the ending an edge, and an ending with an edge is easier to accept than one that just happens to you.

None of this makes transitions painless. It makes them shorter, which on a weekday is most of what you need.
