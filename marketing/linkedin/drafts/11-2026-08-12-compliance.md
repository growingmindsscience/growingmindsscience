# 11 · Two kinds of "listening," and only one of them lasts

- **Suggested post date:** Wednesday, 2026-08-12
- **Type:** Research explainer
- **Source:** Grazyna Kochanska and colleagues, longitudinal research on early compliance
- **Status:** draft, not published

---

Grazyna Kochanska's longitudinal work with families, followed from infancy into the preschool years, draws a distinction I think about often.

**Situational compliance** is a child doing what was asked because an adult is present and insisting. It is real behavior. It depends entirely on the adult staying in the room.

**Committed compliance** is a child following a rule willingly, with something like ownership of it. It holds up when nobody is watching, because the child is not responding to pressure, they are responding to something they now hold themselves.

The finding that surprises people: children with warmer, more autonomy-supportive parents in the toddler years showed *more* committed compliance later, not less.

That runs against a strong intuition. If you let a toddler push back, exercise preferences, say no, surely you get a child who does not listen. The longitudinal picture suggests the opposite. Allowing autonomy in the toddler years, inside limits, is associated with children who internalize rules rather than merely submit to them.

Which reframes the exhausting part of toddlerhood. The pushing back is not damage being done to your authority. Under warm and consistent conditions, it looks more like the process by which rules stop being external.

Worth saying: this is correlational longitudinal work with families, not a randomized trial in parenting style, so read it as a well-supported pattern rather than a proven lever.

Still. On a hard afternoon, "this is how it becomes theirs" is a more accurate story than "I am losing control here."
