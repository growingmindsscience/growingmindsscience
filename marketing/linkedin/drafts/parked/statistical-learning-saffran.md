# 01 · Two minutes of speech, and an 8-month-old finds the words

- **Suggested post date:** Monday, 2026-07-20
- **Type:** Research explainer
- **Source:** Saffran, Aslin & Newport, *Science*, 274, 1926-1928 (1996)
- **Status:** draft, not published

---

Nobody puts spaces between spoken words. We hear them because we already know the language.

So how does a baby, who knows none of it, find where one word ends and the next begins?

In 1996, Jenny Saffran, Richard Aslin and Elissa Newport played 8-month-olds two minutes of a made-up language. No pauses, no rhythm, no intonation. Just a flat stream of syllables running together, produced by a speech synthesizer so there were no vocal cues at all.

The only structure in it was statistical. Inside a word, one syllable reliably predicted the next. Across a word boundary, that prediction was much weaker.

After two minutes, the babies listened longer to syllable strings that crossed a boundary than to the "words." They had noticed the difference. Two minutes of exposure to a language nobody had ever spoken, and they had already started carving it up.

What I find worth sitting with is what this is not. It is not a lesson. Nobody taught, nobody drilled, nobody quizzed. The babies were tracking probabilities in ordinary sound, which is something their brains do whether or not anyone intends it.

The practical version is quieter than most parenting advice: your child is learning from the speech that is already happening around them. Not the speech aimed at them in a teaching voice. Just speech.

That is a lower bar than it usually gets sold as.
