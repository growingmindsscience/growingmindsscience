# 04 · Twelve sessions with a live person. The same material on video did nothing.

- **Suggested post date:** Monday, 2026-07-27
- **Type:** Research explainer
- **Sources:** Werker & Tees, *Infant Behavior and Development*, 7, 49-63 (1984); Kuhl, Tsao & Liu, *PNAS*, 100(15), 9096-9101 (2003)
- **Status:** draft, not published

---

Babies start out able to hear speech sound distinctions that adults around them have stopped hearing.

Janet Werker and Richard Tees showed this in 1984 with a pair of consonants from Thompson, a Salish language of British Columbia. English uses only one of them, and English-speaking adults hear them as the same sound. At 6 to 8 months, most English-learning babies could tell them apart. By 10 to 12 months, most could not. Nothing was wrong with their ears. The brain had stopped tracking a difference their language never used.

Then in 2003, Patricia Kuhl, Feng-Ming Tsao and Huei-Mei Liu ran the part I keep coming back to. They gave 9-month-old English-learning babies twelve short sessions of Mandarin. One group got live Mandarin speakers in the room. Another group got the same material, same speakers, delivered on video or audio only.

The live group held onto the Mandarin contrasts. The video and audio groups learned nothing measurable from it.

Same content. Same amount of exposure. A person in the room did something a screen did not.

The mechanism is still debated, and this is one study on one age group with one language pair, so I would not stretch it into a general law about screens. But it is a clean demonstration that for early speech learning, exposure and interaction are not the same input.

The practical version, and it is genuinely small: the languages a baby hears from people in the first year are the ones their ear holds on to. It does not need a curriculum. It is just talking, in the room.

Werker & Tees (1984). Kuhl, Tsao & Liu, *PNAS*, 100(15), 9096-9101 (2003).
