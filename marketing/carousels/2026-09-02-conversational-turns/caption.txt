It is not how many words your child hears. It is how many they exchange.

At MIT, researchers gave 36 families a two-ounce recorder that rode in the child's shirt pocket through two full weekend days, logging every adult word and every back-and-forth exchange. A conversational turn was defined simply: an adult speaks and the child replies, or the reverse, within five seconds. Then each child, aged four to six, listened to stories in an MRI scanner.

Word count mattered less than expected. The number of conversational turns predicted children's verbal skill more strongly than the sheer number of adult words heard, and only turns predicted activation in Broca's area, the brain's core language region, during story listening. That held after controlling for family income, parental education, IQ, and the raw quantity of speech. Broca's activation in turn explained nearly half of the link between conversation and verbal skill. Every 11 extra turns per hour went with a one-point bump in verbal score, whatever the family's income.

The finding has held up. The same team found more turns went with stronger white-matter language tracts, then ran a randomized trial in which families coached into more turn-taking saw their children's language gains follow. An independent longitudinal study of 122 infants links turns to vocabulary growth from 9 to 24 months, controlling for words heard.

So the cheapest language intervention in existence is a pause. Ask, then wait for the answer. Build on whatever comes back, even if it is one word about a truck. The serve matters less than the return.
