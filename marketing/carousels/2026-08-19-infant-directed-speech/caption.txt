"Baby talk" gets mocked a lot. It also turns out to be one of the few findings in infant research that survives a serious stress test.

In ManyBabies 1, 67 labs across four continents ran the same experiment on 2,329 babies aged 3 to 15 months. Every lab used identical audio: mothers recorded talking to their own infant, and the same mothers talking to an adult. Trials alternated, and each lab timed how long the baby kept listening.

Babies listened longer to the infant-directed recordings. The effect was reliable — but at d = 0.35, roughly half the size the older published literature had reported (d = 0.67). It was stronger in older babies, stronger when the accent matched the baby's own, and stronger with the head-turn method.

What is infant-directed speech, exactly? Higher pitch, wider pitch swings, shorter utterances. If you have ever talked to a baby, you already do all three without having been taught.

Three caveats worth keeping. When the older meta-analysis was corrected and expanded, it also landed on d = 0.35 — two independent routes to the same number, which is reassuring. But the measure has almost no test-retest reliability (r = 0.09), so this is a group finding, not something you can run on your own child. And a follow-up found no link between a baby's speech preference and their vocabulary at 18 or 24 months.

The takeaway is not "do baby talk or else." It is that the way most adults already shift their voice around infants is genuinely more interesting to babies than ordinary adult speech — and that a field willing to re-test its own classics at this scale is one worth listening to.
