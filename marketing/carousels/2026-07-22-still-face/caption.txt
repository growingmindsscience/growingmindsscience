When a parent suddenly stops responding — holding a blank, silent, unresponsive face — babies notice within seconds. That reaction is the "still-face" paradigm, one of the most reliable findings in developmental science.

The procedure is simple. A parent plays face-to-face with the baby, then holds a neutral, unresponsive face for a short stretch, then resumes. Three brief episodes, back to back.

From play to the still face, infants reliably change on three fronts: less gaze, less positive affect, and more negative affect. Then they get to work trying to pull the parent back in — smiling, gesturing, and calling out to restart the connection.

That effort matters. In one early study, 6-month-olds who kept trying to re-engage a still-faced parent were far more likely to be securely attached a year later — 12 of 13, versus 0 of 4 who gave up.

A two-minute still face in a lab is not the same as everyday life, and no single blank moment harms a baby. The point is gentler: responsive back-and-forth is something infants are wired to seek, and to repair. The effect shows up from about one month (not in newborns) and holds across gender and family risk.
