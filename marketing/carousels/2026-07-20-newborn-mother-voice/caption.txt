Your newborn already knows your voice.

In a 1980 study published in Science, DeCasper and Fifer tested 10 babies under three days old. Each baby sucked on a special pacifier wired so that the rhythm of its sucking switched on either its mother's voice or a stranger's — both reading the same Dr. Seuss story.

The babies figured it out. Eight of the ten changed the timing of their sucking to hear their own mother, and worked to keep her voice playing (sign test, p = .02). With at most 12 hours of contact since birth, they already preferred her.

Why so fast? A baby has been hearing your muffled voice through the last trimester in the womb. By birth it is already the most familiar sound in the world — which is why everyday talking, reading aloud, and singing matter more than they seem to.

Later work backs this up: newborns prefer a story their mother read aloud before birth (DeCasper & Spence, 1986), and fetuses respond to their mother's voice with measurable heart-rate changes (Kisilevsky et al., 2003).

Source: DeCasper, A. J. & Fifer, W. P. (1980). Of Human Bonding: Newborns Prefer Their Mothers' Voices. Science, 208, 1174–1176.
