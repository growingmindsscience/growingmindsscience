Your baby can hear sounds you can't. For a few more months.

In 1984, Janet Werker and Richard Tees played English-learning babies a pair of consonants from Thompson, a Salish language spoken in British Columbia. The two sounds differ only in where the back of the tongue blocks the air: one at the velum, one behind it. English uses only one of them, so English-speaking adults hear them as the same sound. Just 3 of 10 adults could tell them apart.

At 6 to 8 months, 8 of 10 babies could.

By 10 to 12 months, 1 of 10 could. Same test, same sounds, four months later. Nothing was wrong with their ears. The brain simply stopped treating a difference their language never uses as a difference worth tracking. The babies in the study being raised in Hindi- or Thompson-speaking homes still heard their own contrasts easily at 11 to 12 months, which is how we know this is about experience and not just age.

But the window isn't a wall. Kuhl, Tsao and Liu later showed that 9-month-olds who spent 12 short sessions with live Mandarin speakers kept hearing Mandarin contrasts, while babies given the exact same material on video or audio alone learned nothing from it. A person in the room did what a screen couldn't.

The practical version: the languages your baby hears in the first year are the ones their ear holds on to. It isn't a lesson plan. It's just talking.

Werker & Tees, Infant Behavior and Development, 7, 49-63 (1984). Kuhl, Tsao & Liu, PNAS, 100(15), 9096-9101 (2003).
