What's typical, by stage

A calm look at sleep development across the first year: what is typical, what varies, and what the research can and cannot tell us.

Tools · Infant · 0–12 mo · Sleep development

What this guide helps with

  • Separating developmental sleep changes from sleep-safety decisions.
  • Knowing what evidence supports, and where exact schedules become guesswork.
  • Deciding when a pattern deserves a pediatrician conversation.

The core idea

Infant sleep is developmental. It changes as feeding, circadian rhythms, motor skills, separation awareness, and family routines change. The research can give useful ranges and safety rules. It cannot tell you that every healthy baby should follow one exact schedule.

Safety comes first

For the first year, sleep advice should never override safe sleep: back to sleep, a firm flat surface, no soft bedding, and your baby’s own sleep surface, not your bed.

Ranges are more honest than targets

For 4-12 months, the major sleep-duration benchmark is 12-16 total hours per 24 hours, including naps. Newborn sleep is even more variable.

Night waking is data, not a verdict

Waking can reflect hunger, development, illness, discomfort, sleep associations, schedule fit, or temperament. The useful question is what pattern is changing.

Stage guide

Choose your baby’s closest stage. Each bar shows where that stage sits in the first year. Use this as orientation, not a prescription.

Fragmented sleep is expected.

0-2 months

Newborns do not yet have mature circadian rhythms. Sleep is spread across day and night, and feeding needs usually drive the pattern.

Typical patternMany short sleep periods across 24 hours, with frequent waking for feeding, comfort, and regulation.
What helpsProtect safe sleep, use daylight and ordinary household cues during the day, keep nights low-stimulation, and follow medical feeding guidance.
What not to overreadA newborn who wakes often is not failing to self-soothe. The nervous system is immature, and sleep is still organizing.

Rhythm starts to emerge, but it is fragile.

2-4 months

Some babies begin giving longer stretches, especially at night, while others remain highly variable. Growth, feeding, reflux, temperament, and family routines can all matter.

Typical patternSleep may still happen in several blocks, but day-night differences often become clearer.
What helpsA simple bedtime rhythm, consistent light cues, and a safe sleep space can support organization without forcing a rigid schedule. If you swaddle, stop as soon as your baby shows signs of trying to roll.
What not to overreadSome babies wake more for a while around this age, as the body clock switches on and sleep reorganizes. The “four-month regression” is a popular name, not a research finding, and it usually settles. Expect a zigzag, not a staircase.

Total sleep range becomes more useful.

4-6 months

From 4 months onward, the AASM-supported benchmark is 12-16 total hours per 24 hours, including naps. Some babies begin more predictable nap and bedtime patterns here.

Typical patternMany babies still wake at night, while some begin longer stretches. Naps may be inconsistent before they consolidate.
What helpsLook at the whole 24-hour rhythm: wake time, naps, bedtime, feeding, and how the baby gets help returning to sleep.
What not to overreadThe 12-16 hour range is a total sleep-duration guide, not a promise that nights will be uninterrupted.

Development can show up at night.

6-9 months

Rolling, sitting, crawling practice, new foods, illness, teething discomfort, and separation awareness can all disturb sleep. More skills can mean more night activity for a while.

Typical patternSome babies move toward two or three naps and longer night stretches. Others still need help overnight.
What helpsKeep the response predictable and boring at night, give motor practice during the day, and keep the sleep surface safe as mobility increases.
What not to overreadA baby who was sleeping longer and starts waking again has not lost all progress. Sleep often changes around new skills.

More pattern does not mean no waking.

9-12 months

By the end of the first year, many babies have more predictable sleep, but normal variation remains wide. Travel, illness, separation, feeding changes, and nap transitions can still disrupt nights.

Typical patternMany babies are moving toward two naps and a steadier bedtime rhythm, with total sleep still commonly understood within the 12-16 hour range.
What helpsA repeatable wind-down, enough daytime sleep, and a response plan caregivers can use consistently.
What not to overreadOne rough week is not a diagnosis. Look for persistent patterns, daytime functioning, growth, feeding, breathing, and family strain.

Safe sleep, every sleep

Every stage answer above rests on these. If sleep feels impossible, make the sleep space safe first, then ask whether a medical or feeding issue sits underneath the waking.

  • On the back, for every sleepNaps and nights, with every caregiver, until your baby’s first birthday.
  • A firm, flat surface made for infantsA crib, bassinet, or play yard with a firm, flat, non-inclined mattress and a fitted sheet.
  • Nothing else in the sleep spaceNo pillows, loose blankets, soft bedding, positioners, or weighted sleep products in or near it.
  • Share a room, not a bedKeep your baby on their own infant sleep surface in your room, ideally for at least the first 6 months. The AAP does not recommend bed-sharing.
  • Never on a sofa or armchairNever sleep with your baby on a sofa, couch, or armchair. If you might doze off during a night feed, feed in an adult bed with pillows and covers moved away, and move your baby back to their own sleep space when you wake.
  • Swaddle on the back, and stop at rollingIf you swaddle, always place your baby on their back, and stop as soon as they show signs of trying to roll. That is usually around 3 to 4 months, and can be earlier.
  • Car seats and swings are not bedsCar seats, strollers, swings, infant carriers, and slings are not for routine sleep. If your baby falls asleep in one, move them to a firm, flat sleep surface as soon as you safely can.

When to get help

Most night waking is ordinary. These are the times not to troubleshoot alone, most urgent first.

  • Call 911 now

    If your baby is struggling to breathe, stops breathing, turns blue or grey, goes limp, or is very hard to wake. Outside the US, call your local emergency number.

    911 Emergency

  • Call your pediatrician right away

    For a fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months, even if your baby seems otherwise well.

  • Call your pediatrician promptly

    For breathing that worries you, poor weight gain, feeding difficulty, unusual lethargy, or persistent vomiting.

  • If you are running on empty

    If you are so tired you might fall asleep holding your baby, put them on their back in their own sleep space first. If you feel you might hurt your child, put them somewhere safe, like a crib, and step away for a few minutes. In the US, call or text 988 any time.

What the research can and cannot tell us

Can tell us

  • Safe sleep practices reduce risk and should anchor every plan.
  • Infant sleep changes rapidly across the first year.
  • There is wide normal variation in sleep duration, waking, and nap patterns.
  • For 4-12 months, 12-16 total hours per 24 hours is the main health-oriented duration range.
  • Persistent sleep difficulty can affect the whole family and may deserve support.

Cannot tell us

  • That every baby should sleep through the night by a specific month.
  • That one nap schedule works for all infants.
  • That night waking always means a parent has created a bad habit.
  • That a sleep plan is appropriate when feeding, growth, breathing, or medical concerns are present.
  • That convenience products are safe just because they are marketed for sleep.

When you need the short version

One-minute summary

  • Infant sleep changes quickly in the first year.
  • Newborn sleep is fragmented; day-night rhythm takes time.
  • From 4-12 months, 12-16 total hours per 24 hours is the main sleep-duration benchmark.
  • Night waking can be normal, especially during feeding needs, illness, and developmental change.
  • Safe sleep rules matter more than any schedule: on the back, on a firm, flat surface of their own, never on a sofa.
  • Call 911 for trouble breathing or a baby who is very hard to wake, and your pediatrician right away for any fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months.
