Beyond “less is better”

A minute count on its own is a blunt tool. For babies and young toddlers the guidelines still set a ceiling, and inside it the research points to better questions: what the content is, the context it’s used in, the child it’s for, and what it crowds out. This is a quick way to think it through without the guilt.

The core idea

For years the main message was “less is better,” and a single number of minutes carried all the weight. For babies and young toddlers that number still matters most. Past it, the American Academy of Pediatrics now puts far more emphasis on content quality, watching together, and what screens crowd out than on a blanket time cap. A video call with a grandparent and an autoplay feed at bedtime are not the same exposure, even at the same minute count.

Four things to weigh

Content: what is on the screen

Slow, age-appropriate, made-for-children content sits in a different category from fast-paced apps, open platforms, and autoplay. Even good content lands better when an adult helps connect it to the real world.

Context: how it’s being used

Solo at bedtime is different from shared on the couch. Background TV that no one is watching is different again. The same show can help or just fill time depending on what’s happening around it.

The child: age and individual

Under about two and a half, children learn markedly less from screens than from a live person. This is the “video deficit.” Older children, and the same content with a responsive adult, change the math.

What it tends to displace

The clearest mechanism we have is displacement: screens become more of a concern when they repeatedly replace sleep, conversation, movement, and unstructured play. Guard those, and a little screen time carries less weight.

What the guidelines still say

“Less is better” hasn’t gone away for the youngest children. These are the age lines the major guidelines draw. Inside them, the questions on the rest of this page tell you more than the clock does.

  • Before about 18 months

    Avoid screens other than video chat. That was the American Academy of Pediatrics’ advice for years, and its 2026 policy statement still notes that babies this young have trouble carrying what they see on a screen into real life.

  • Before age 2

    The World Health Organization (2019) does not recommend sedentary screen time, such as watching TV or videos.

  • Ages 2 to 4

    No more than an hour a day of sedentary screen time, and less is better (World Health Organization, 2019). The AAP’s 2016 statement likewise suggested about an hour a day of high-quality programs for ages 2 to 5, watched together where possible.

Is this screen moment helping, or just filling time?

Pick the moment closest to yours. None of these is a verdict on your parenting. Each one gives you a calmer way to read it, plus one small thing you can adjust.

Background TV is the underrated one.

Mostly filling space

A TV on that no one is really watching is associated with less back-and-forth talk and more disrupted play. In studies by Marie Evans Schmidt, Heather Kirkorian, and colleagues, toddlers played in shorter bursts and parents talked less with an adult show on, even though the children barely looked at it. Each effect was modest, but a TV that’s on for hours a day gives it a lot of time to add up.

One adjustment: turn it off when no one is actively watching. The quiet usually fills back up with talk on its own.

This is the friendliest way to watch.

Watching with you

When an adult watches alongside and talks about what’s happening, screens shift toward what researchers call joint media engagement. The video deficit shrinks, because the screen now sits inside a real conversation. You’re the bridge between what’s on screen and the world.

One adjustment: keep narrating in plain turns. “What do you think happens next?” does more than the show does alone.

A reset in a hard moment is allowed.

A tool, used on purpose

Handing over a tablet to help a flooded child settle is a reasonable tool, not a failure. It only becomes worth a second look if it’s the main way big feelings get resolved most days, because then it can crowd out practice with frustration. One hard afternoon is not a pattern.

For a baby under about 18 months, reach for holding, rocking, or a change of scene instead; that is the age when the guidelines above advise against screens other than video chat.

One adjustment: notice what happens afterward. If transitions off the device are rough, give a clear ending up front next time.

A live call works differently from a video.

In a category of its own

Bidirectional video calls work differently from passive video. When a grandparent responds in real time to what your child does and says, the exchange resembles a real conversation, the contingent, back-and-forth quality that early learning is built on. The AAP treats live video chat as a reasonable exception even for the youngest children.

One adjustment: nothing needed. Maybe hand the child something to show the screen, so the call stays a two-way thing.

The timing is the part to look at.

Working against the night

A fast-paced app close to bedtime tends to make winding down harder: the content is exciting and the device is hard to put away. The concern here is less the app itself than what it's displacing, which is sleep. Protecting the wind-down is one of the higher-value moves you can make.

One adjustment: move the device out of the bedtime hour, and trade it for something slower if a screen is still needed.

A better question than “how much?”

When a moment feels off, these three questions tell you more than the minute count does. They also give you something to adjust besides guilt.

1

What is it displacing?

Is this screen time replacing sleep, talk, movement, or play? Displacement is the clearest mechanism the research has. Protect those first.

2

Is it solo or shared?

Co-viewing isn’t required every second. But a nearby adult who talks about what’s happening turns a passive show into something closer to a conversation.

3

Does it fit this child, today?

Age matters most under two and a half. So does how your own child handles the transition off. That data from your actual kid beats any general rule.

Two ways to frame it

More helpful framing

  • Ask what, when, why, and with whom, not only how many minutes.
  • Turn off background TV when no one is watching.
  • Watch with the child and talk when you can.
  • Treat live video calls as mostly connection: stay nearby and help the back-and-forth.
  • Protect sleep, meals, movement, and play as the real anchors.

Less helpful framing

  • Counting minutes as if every minute is the same exposure.
  • Treating any screen as proof of bad parenting.
  • Trusting “educational” as a label without watching along.
  • Leaving a TV on as constant background noise.
  • Letting a hard season become a referendum on your whole approach.

When you need the short version

One-minute summary

  • For babies and young toddlers, the guidelines still set a ceiling. Inside it, content, context, and the child matter more than the minute count.
  • Under about two and a half, children learn far less from screens than from a live person.
  • Background TV is its own issue: it cuts into talk and play even when the child isn’t watching.
  • Watching together shrinks the gap and turns a screen into a conversation.
  • Live video calls work differently from video, because a responsive person keeps some back-and-forth.
  • Guard what screens displace, sleep, talk, movement, and play, more than the minutes.
A note on support

Most of this research is correlational, and heavy screen use often travels with other stressors, so we can’t cleanly separate the screen from everything around it. This tool is educational and can’t assess your individual child. If screens feel like they’re displacing sleep, eating, or relationships in a way you can’t shift, or you have concerns about development, it’s worth talking with your pediatrician or an early childhood specialist.

Sources and further reading
