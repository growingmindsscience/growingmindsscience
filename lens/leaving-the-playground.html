Leaving the playground

A Developmental Lens · 18 months to 4 years · Regulation, autonomy, transitions

An ordinary moment every parent of a toddler knows, read through the Developmental Lens: what we see, what may be developing, what that changes, and what to notice next time.

What are we actually seeing?

It is time to leave the playground. You give the two-minute warning, then say “time to go”. Your two-and-a-half-year-old runs back to the slide. When you reach for a hand, they drop to the ground and cry. Ten minutes ago, everything was fine. Nothing about the playground changed. The plan did.

What is observable

  • A happy child, absorbed in play.
  • An announcement from an adult.
  • A run in the opposite direction.
  • A body that goes to the floor.
  • Crying that is loud and fast.

What is not observable

Intent. From the outside, nobody can see whether this is “defiance”, “manipulation” or “being tired”. We can see the sequence, and the sequence is the clue.

Most common from about 18 months to 3 years and still common at 4, with wide variation between children and between days.

What might be developing beneath the surface?

Four processes can be read in this one moment. None is a flaw. Each is a skill still being built.

  • Switching rules is hard at this age.

    In a classic card-sorting game, most three-year-olds keep sorting by the first rule even after they are told the new one. By four or five, most children switch easily. Leaving the playground asks for a similar switch, from “play” to “go”, while the first plan is still running, and in a moment that matters far more to a child than a card game.

    Well established in laboratory tasks. Zelazo, 2006; Zelazo and colleagues, 2003.

  • The brakes are the last part to arrive.

    Inhibitory control, the ability to hold back or stop an action, relies partly on prefrontal brain systems that grow fastest in the preschool years and keep developing long after. Strong feelings and fast reactions are there from early on. The ability to hold them back comes later.

    Well established. Diamond, 2013.

  • Autonomy is the work of this age.

    Between roughly 18 months and 3 years, a child is discovering that they are a separate person with preferences of their own. “No” is part of becoming a person with a will of one’s own, and it often shows up right when an adult’s plan takes over.

    Supported; the size of specific effects varies. Brownell and colleagues, 2007; Erikson, 1950.

  • Once overwhelmed, reasoning does not land.

    When a child is overwhelmed, the thinking parts of the brain are much harder to reach, so words and reasons tend not to land until they have calmed down. Young children borrow a calm adult to get there.

    Supported. The two-zone picture of the brain is a conceptual model, not a scan. Diamond, 2013; Growing Minds toddler class, Module 4.

  • You are inside the system too.

    Being late, being watched by other parents, being tired: your own state shapes how you respond, and research on co-regulation suggests that how calmly you respond matters, not only what you say.

    Supported. Waters and colleagues, 2014; see “When your own nervous system is the variable”.

What can this understanding change?

None of this makes leaving easy. It changes what you are responding to.

  1. Say what is next before the end. “Two more slides, then we go home for lunch.” A plan said out loud gives your child something to hold on to. It may not stop the protest, because leaving something fun is hard even with notice, but the next step is no longer a surprise.

    Regular daily routines are linked with better self-regulation in young children. In small studies, a warning on its own often did not prevent protest, especially when leaving something fun.

  2. Give the last move a shape. One small choice inside a firm limit: “Slide or swing for the last one?” Your child gets a say in how you leave, not whether.

    Some support. Offering choices was one part of a wider autonomy-supportive style linked with more willing cooperation in toddlers; choices on their own are less tested. Laurin and Joussemet, 2017; Andreadakis and colleagues, 2019.

  3. Carry the plan for them. Narrate the next step while you walk. The working memory that holds “home, lunch, nap” is yours for now.

  4. If it tips into a meltdown, regulate first and teach later. Fewer words, a lower voice, a steady body, staying close. The explanation can wait for the car.

    Co-regulation is well supported in general; the specific steps are practical guidance rather than separately tested. Potegal and colleagues, 2003; Morris and colleagues, 2007.

  5. Expect it to be hard sometimes anyway. A smooth exit on Tuesday and a floor exit on Wednesday is ordinary at this age, not proof the method failed. Sleep, hunger and a long day all change how much a young child can manage.

What might you notice next time?

  • What came before the hard moment: the warning, the walk to the gate, or the car seat?
  • Which part of the transition was hardest, and was it the same part as last time?
  • Did advance notice change anything, for your child or for you?
  • What helped you stay steady, and what made it harder?

These are observations, not scores. The point is curiosity about one child, not a verdict.

A conceptual reading of a familiar moment. It describes processes, not what any particular child is thinking.

The labels on the photograph, as text
  • Observe: A happy child: absorbed in play; An announcement: “Time to go.”; A hand on the slide: the run back; Not observable: intent. We see the sequence, not the reason.
  • Understand: Switching rules: “play” to “go” is hard at three; The brakes arrive last: holding back an action develops slowly; Autonomy: “no” is part of becoming a person; Your own state: late, watched, tired: it shapes the return; Conceptual model: capacities still being built, not a diagnosis.
  • Respond: The hand held low: close, steady, not grabbing; Say what is next: “two more slides, then lunch”.
  • Reflect: Next time: notice what came just before.
