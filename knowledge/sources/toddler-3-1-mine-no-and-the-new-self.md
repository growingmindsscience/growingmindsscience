## Overview

This is Lesson 3.1 of the Growing Minds class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 3 (Autonomy and No). It covers: When toddlers first recognize themselves, what mine and no really mean, how often toddlers go along with what we ask, and why a child who pushes back is usually a child who is developing well.

A twenty-month-old is standing in front of the hallway mirror with a smear of blueberry on her nose. She looks, frowns, and reaches up to rub her own nose. That afternoon she grips a toy truck and announces mine, and at bath time she plants her feet and says no.

Those three moments tend to arrive in the same stretch of the second year. In this lesson, we'll look at when toddlers first recognize themselves, and at what mine and no really mean. We'll also look at how often toddlers go along with us, which is more often than it feels.

## The Mirror Test

Researchers have a simple way to ask whether a child knows herself. An adult quietly dabs a spot of color on the child's nose, and then the child is placed in front of a mirror.

A child who doesn't yet recognize herself treats the reflection as someone else. In a classic study of eighty-eight children, babies from about six to twelve months greeted the mirror like a friendly playmate.

A child who does recognize herself looks in the mirror and then touches her own nose. That small reach tells us she understands that the child in the glass is her.

## When It Arrives

So when does that reach appear? One study saw ninety-eight babies every three months through the second year. Recognizing themselves in the mirror emerged between eighteen and twenty-one months, the same stretch in which pretend play appeared in Module 1.

In the classic study, about two thirds of the children between twenty and twenty-four months recognized themselves. That leaves a third who did not yet.

So for most of these children it arrived late in the second year, and for some it came later still.

## A Window, Not a Deadline

The mirror test is a window, and it should not be treated as a deadline. When researchers tried it in other parts of the world, the results changed.

One study followed two hundred and seventy-six toddlers in Germany, India, and Cameroon. Recognition grew with age everywhere. It was more common in the city families, where mothers put more weight on a child acting independently, and less common in the rural families.

In another study, children in Kenya rarely reached for the mark at all, even well past two. The researchers questioned whether the test can be read as a universal marker of a sense of self.

## What Arrives With It

Around the same time, other signs of a self show up. Children start to use words like me, my, and I. In a study of sixty-six children, the ones who recognized themselves in the mirror used more of these words, and their pretend play was more advanced.

A new feeling appears as well. In two small studies, toddlers were put in situations meant to bring out either fear or embarrassment. Embarrassment went with recognizing themselves in the mirror, and wariness did not.

Embarrassment needs a self to be embarrassed about. So a toddler who suddenly turns shy when everyone looks at her may be showing a new sense of self.

## A Self Among Other Selves

Knowing yourself also seems to open a door to other people. In a set of experiments with a hundred and twenty-six children, a playmate became upset. Almost all the children who recognized themselves in the mirror showed concern and tried to help. The children who did not yet recognize themselves looked puzzled or carried on.

A more recent study, which followed seventy-two toddlers month by month, was more cautious. Self-recognition came before comforting someone in distress, but it did not seem to cause it directly.

Either way, the toddler who has just discovered me is also beginning to notice you.

## Mine

To study mine, researchers watched sixty-six British toddlers at home with children they knew, on two visits six months apart. Words like mine and my appeared between eighteen and twenty-four months.

Toddlers who said mine were also more likely to say yours. And although saying mine went with more grabbing and pushing at first, the toddlers who used these words at the first visit were more likely to share with their friends six months later.

So mine is not the opposite of sharing. It is the first half of an idea whose second half is yours.

## Mine Is Knowledge

What looks like selfishness is partly knowledge. In one study, toddlers between eighteen and twenty-eight months already showed a basic understanding of which things were theirs and which were someone else's.

In another, two-year-olds were given toys and told the toys were theirs. When they played with a friend, the owners were more likely to keep hold of their toys and to say mine. But they also pointed out which toys were their friend's, said yours, and handed those toys back.

A toddler who shouts mine is applying a rule about who owns what. She applies it loudly, and mostly in her own favor, but it is a rule she can also apply to other people's things.

## Mine Comes Before Sharing

All of this matters for sharing. In one study, toddlers of eighteen and twenty-four months had toys or snacks, while an adult playmate had none and made her wish for some more and more obvious.

The two-year-olds shared often, and without much prompting. The eighteen-month-olds shared when they had several chances and the adult spelled out what she wanted. At both ages, the toddlers who understood ownership better shared more, whatever their age or language.

So understanding what is mine seems to come before real sharing. Giving up something of their own was the hardest kind of helping at both eighteen and thirty months, and the Preschool class follows sharing from three to five.

## Then Comes No

The other famous word is no, and it is one of the first words many children say. Studies of early speech find that early nos tend to be refusals. Using no to say that something isn't true comes later, most often between twenty and twenty-five months in one study.

Think about what a no requires. A child has to notice that what she wants is different from what you want, and then tell you. A baby can cry or turn away, but it takes a child with a sense of me to refuse.

It may be why no and mine tend to arrive in the same season as the reach toward the nose in the mirror.

## What Refusing Tells You

It is natural to read a toddler's no as a sign that something has gone wrong. One study of one-year-olds and young two-year-olds found something close to the opposite.

The toddlers who resisted their mothers most actively tended to have mothers who were more supportive, who gave them more say, and who had fewer symptoms of depression. These toddlers also started more friendly exchanges with their mothers.

As the children got older, willing cooperation went up, and so did active resistance. Where mothers were struggling with depression, that rise in resistance was missing. The findings fit the idea that pushing back at this age often reflects a child's wish to have a hand in events, and does not point to poor parenting or a troubled relationship.

## How Refusing Grows Up

Refusing also changes shape with age. A study that followed children from the toddler years into preschool found that conflicts between mothers and children peaked at around two and a half.

The findings suggest that toddlers rely mostly on plain refusal, and that preschoolers rely more and more on negotiating.

A separate study watched how these standoffs ended for children from fifteen months to four years. With older children, mothers succeeded right away more often, compromises were more common, and mothers had to enforce things less.

## How Often They Say Yes

With all this refusing, how often do toddlers go along? It is more often than it feels at six in the evening.

When researchers watched ninety children of twenty-seven months at home with their mothers, they described the children as generally obedient. In day care, children of two, three, and four went along with eighty-four percent of requests on average.

Refusing is just as typical. When parents of forty children without behavior problems recorded refusals at home for two weeks, thirty-seven of the children refused often enough to meet a diagnostic checklist's bar for a symptom.

Much also depends on the moment. In a lab study of toddlers in five situations, cooperation swung widely from one to the next. So there is no standard number of nos per day, because the same child can be easy in one moment and immovable in the next.

## Two Kinds of Yes

Not every yes is the same. The study from Module 1 that compared do requests with don't requests also separated two kinds of cooperation. In one, the child goes along without much heart in it. In the other, the child takes up the request eagerly, as if it were her own idea.

Only the wholehearted kind was linked to following the rule when the child was alone. In a related study, wholehearted cooperation was most common in pairs who shared a lot of enjoyment with each other.

So the road to a child who follows rules on her own seems to run through the relationship more than through winning each standoff. The next lesson turns that into practice.

## Counting Clashes Misses the Point

Some toddlers argue far more than others, and their parents often worry about what that says. A study of sixty-four mothers and toddlers looked at this question.

How often a pair clashed was related to the child's temperament, including how negatively the child reacted to things and how active and impulsive the child was. It was not related to how secure the bond between mother and child was.

What the secure pairs had was a different kind of clash, with more explaining, more compromise, and more disputes that actually got settled. So a high count of nos is tied to temperament, which the Infant class covered. The relationship shows in how the clashes go.

## What This Means at Home

What does this change at home? Start by hearing mine and no as news about development. They are not a verdict on your parenting, and they are not a plan to defeat you.

With mine, say out loud who owns what. This is your truck, and that one is Sam's. When she has to hand something back, name that too, because it is his. You are teaching the second half of the idea.

With no, decide which ones you can simply accept. Letting a no stand when it costs nothing is not giving in. For the limits that matter, you will need warmth and a plan, and that is the next lesson.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

A self arrives in the second year. In the studies we covered, most toddlers recognized themselves by about two, and me, mine, and no arrive in the same period.

Mine comes before real sharing. Toddlers who understand what is theirs also learn yours, and they tend to share more.

A no is not a failure. Active refusing at this age goes along with supportive parenting, and young children still go along with most requests.

## Next: Choices and Warm Limits

So the child who rubs the blueberry off her own nose, guards her truck, and refuses the bath is a child busy becoming someone. Your job is not to stop that, and it is not to step aside either.

## Common myths

Myth: "Mine is selfishness, and it has to be trained out."
What the research shows: Toddlers who said mine also said yours and shared more six months later (Hay, 2006); ownership understanding went with more sharing (Brownell et al., 2013).

Myth: "A defiant toddler means something is wrong at home."
What the research shows: Active resistance was associated with supportive mothering and rose with age alongside willing compliance (Dix et al., 2007); how often pairs clash tracks temperament, not attachment security (Laible et al., 2008).
