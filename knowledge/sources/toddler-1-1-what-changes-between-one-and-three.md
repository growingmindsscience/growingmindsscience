## Overview

This is Lesson 1.1 of the From Little Onward class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 1 (The Toddler Brain, Briefly). It covers: What grows fastest in these two years, what lags behind, why the brain's wiring explains the gap, why a toddler can know a rule and still not follow it, and a better question to ask when it happens.

Picture a two-year-old standing over the dog's water bowl. She looks straight at her father, shakes her head, and tells herself no. Then she plunges both hands into the water anyway.

Welcome to the Toddler Years class. In this first lesson, we'll look at what grows fastest between one and three, and at what lags behind. We'll see why a toddler can know a rule and still not follow it, and we'll finish with a question that makes these years easier.

## A Brain Still Growing Fast

If you took the Infant class, you heard that the brain about doubles in size during the first year. Growth slows after that, but it certainly doesn't stop.

In one study that scanned children from birth to age two, the brain grew by another fifteen percent in the second year.

A second study followed seventy-two children with scans soon after birth, at one, and at two. In the second year, the areas for basic sensing grew more slowly, while areas toward the front and top of the brain grew faster. The brain uses those areas for planning, attention, and control, and they still have a long way to go.

## A Body on the Move

The change you can see most easily is movement. As the Infant class covered, most children take their first steps alone somewhere between about eight and eighteen months.

Once they start, the practice is enormous. Researchers who watched toddlers between twelve and nineteen months at play counted an average of about twenty-four hundred steps and seventeen falls an hour.

All that practice pays off quickly, because new walkers soon took more steps, traveled farther, and fell less. A toddler who never sits still is doing exactly the work this age calls for.

## Walking Changes How They Connect

Walking also changes how a child connects with you. One study followed twenty-five babies for seven months around the time of their first steps.

After they began to walk, their use of gestures grew much faster. They started walking over to a parent to show off a toy, and parents had more chances to answer and to talk about whatever had been carried over.

Some studies also find a jump in words around the time walking starts, though others don't. What is clear is that a walking child can bring the world to you, and that invites conversation.

## Words, Memory, and Me

Three other things take off in these years. Language grows from a handful of words to short sentences, and Module 2 is all about that.

Memory stretches in these years too. Even at fourteen months, a child can copy something a week after seeing it. By two, a memory can last for months, which we'll come to in Lesson 1.3.

And somewhere in the second year, children begin to recognize themselves and to talk about what is mine. That new sense of self is behind a lot of toddler behavior, and it's where Module 3 begins.

## What Lags Behind

So movement, language, memory, and a sense of self all race ahead. Three skills trail well behind them, and they are stopping, waiting, and switching.

Stopping means holding back an action that is already on its way. Waiting means putting off something you want. Switching means letting go of one thing in order to move to another.

These skills aren't missing in a toddler. They are present in early, shaky forms, and they take years to mature. One study gave about a hundred children small tasks like waiting, slowing down, and lowering their voice. The children improved a great deal between about two and almost three.

## Why the Gap: Wiring Speed

Part of the reason lies in the brain's wiring. In the Infant class, we met myelin, the fatty coating that lets a pathway carry its signals faster.

That coating is not laid down everywhere at once. In brain scans of children from one to six, the areas for touch, movement, and vision were already well coated by about the first birthday.

Areas at the front and sides of the brain took far longer. Those include areas used for language and for self-control, and a review of research on the brain's wiring finds it still maturing into young adulthood.

## A Fast Engine and New Brakes

Now put those pieces together. A toddler has a body that can run and climb, strong wants, and a growing memory for what she wants.

The part of the brain that helps hold a rule in mind and stop an action sits right behind the forehead. It is growing fast in these years, and yet it is among the last parts of the brain to finish.

So the engine arrives years before the brakes are ready. The next lesson looks closely at those brakes, and at the alarm that sets a toddler off.

## Knowing the Rule, in the Lab

Here is how that gap looks in an experiment. Researchers gave children around two and a half a rule for sorting pictures into two boxes. Things found inside a house went in one box, and things found outside went in the other.

When the children were asked about each picture, they did well at saying whether it belonged inside or outside. When they had to use the rule and sort the pictures themselves, the younger ones did worse.

By three, knowing and doing matched. In a follow-up with a hundred and twenty children, the mistakes had a pattern, because children tended to repeat whatever they had just done.

## Knowing Is Not Yet Doing

Researchers still debate what causes this. Some think the knowledge is solid and the control over action is weak. Others think the knowledge itself is still faint, strong enough to answer a question and too weak to steer a busy pair of hands.

Either way, the lesson for parents is the same. There is a gap between knowing the rule and being able to follow it, and it shows up clearly in the toddler years.

Think of the girl at the water bowl. She knew the rule well enough to say it out loud, and her hands went in anyway. She wasn't being sneaky, and she wasn't testing her father.

## Tiny Chairs

A playful study shows a similar gap from another side. Researchers gave children between eighteen and thirty months miniature versions of familiar things, like a dollhouse chair and a tiny toy car.

Every so often, a child would make a serious attempt to sit down on the tiny chair, or to climb into the tiny car. It was an honest try and not a game.

Researchers think the plan to act ran ahead of the information about size. Not every toddler does this, but it shows how loosely seeing, knowing, and doing are tied together at this age.

## Which Rules Are Hardest

Some requests are harder than others. One study followed a hundred and eight children from fourteen months to almost four, and compared two kinds of request.

In one, the mother asked her child not to touch some very attractive toys. In the other, she asked her child to keep going with a dull job, like putting toys away.

At every age, sticking with the dull job was much harder than leaving the toys alone. So expect cleanup to need your help for years, long after a simple don't touch has started to work.

## Understanding Comes First

For the youngest toddlers, there is an even simpler reason a rule isn't followed. They may not understand the words yet.

In a small study of thirty children between twelve and eighteen months, following a request went hand in hand with understanding it. The biggest jump came between about fifteen and eighteen months.

Another study asked mothers which rules their young children followed best. Safety rules, and rules about other people's things, came out on top. It may be no accident that those are the rules parents keep short and never bend.

## In Your Living Room

So what does this mean at home? Start by expecting the gap. A rule your toddler can recite still needs you to carry it for a while.

Go close, get down to eye level, and use very few words. Then help the stop happen, by gently holding a hand, moving the bowl, or lifting your child away.

Where you can, change the room instead of testing the brakes. A gate, a high shelf, or a closed door will do more than a rule repeated from across the room.

## Show What to Do

Because stopping is so hard, give those hands something to do instead. Telling a toddler to stop leaves an empty space, and handing her a cup to fill at the sink gives her a new plan.

For jobs like cleanup, work together and keep it short. Hand over one block at a time, or make it a game of filling the basket.

And expect to repeat yourself, calmly, for months. Repeating a rule is not a sign that it isn't working, because at this age you are part of how the rule works.

## A Better Question

When a toddler breaks a rule she clearly knows, it is natural to ask why she keeps doing this. That question tends to lead to answers like stubborn, or doing it on purpose.

Those answers change how we act. In one experiment, some mothers of toddlers were told that their child's misbehavior would be deliberate, and others were told their child was not to blame. The first group felt angrier and disciplined more harshly, and their children showed more upset.

A more useful question is what your child needs right now. Often the answer is help with stopping, or fewer words, or food, sleep, or a hug.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

Some skills race and some trail. Movement, words, memory, and wants surge between one and three, while stopping, waiting, and switching take years.

Knowing a rule comes before following it. A toddler can know a rule before she can follow it, so for now you are the brakes.

Ask what your child needs. When a rule gets broken, that question will take you further than asking why.

## Common myths

Myth: "She knows better, so she's doing it on purpose."
What the research shows: Two-year-olds knew more about a rule than they could act on (Zelazo & Reznick, 1991; Zelazo et al., 1995), and reading misbehavior as deliberate made discipline harsher (Smith Slep & O'Leary, 1998).

Myth: "The toddler brain is mostly built by three."
What the research shows: The brain is still growing in the second year (Knickmeyer et al., 2008), the front and sides are the slowest areas to gain their coating between one and six (Deoni et al., 2015), and the brain's wiring keeps maturing into young adulthood (Lebel & Deoni, 2018).

Myth: "If I have to keep repeating the rule, it isn't working."
What the research shows: Keeping up a dull task was the harder kind of request at every age tested, up to 45 months (Kochanska et al., 2001); repeating and helping is the expected pattern.

Myth: "Walking makes babies talk."
What the research shows: Gestures and showing do increase after walking begins (West & Iverson, 2021), but the link to vocabulary is found in some studies and not others (Walle & Campos, 2014; Moore et al., 2019).
