## Overview

This is Lesson 3.3 of the Growing Minds class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 3 (Autonomy and No). It covers: Where a toddler's drive to do things alone comes from, how early children join in household work, what parents do that brings them in, and how to slow down enough to let a toddler help.

A mother is unloading the dishwasher in a hurry when her twenty-two-month-old arrives, announces me do it, and takes over the spoons. He carries them to the drawer one at a time, and a job that takes her two minutes is going to take ten.

In this lesson, we'll look at where that drive comes from and what it is for. We'll see how early children join in household work, and what parents do that brings them in. We'll finish with how to slow down enough to let a toddler help, and what to do when you can't.

## Wanting to Do It Oneself

Researchers have a plain name for this, which is wanting to do it oneself. In one study, forty-one children from nine months to six years played with a set of tasks. Every so often, the adult interrupted what the child was doing.

The children's reactions fell into stages, and insisting on doing it themselves went along with how far their sense of self had developed.

So me do it belongs with mine and no from the first lesson of this module. It is the new self asking to be the one who makes things happen.

## From Doing to Finishing

What toddlers want from a task also changes. Researchers watched children from fifteen months to almost three during play and cleanup tasks.

The youngest were mostly interested in the activity itself. Older toddlers increasingly aimed at an end result. They checked their work, fixed mistakes, and showed pleasure when it came out right.

A second study, of fifty-seven toddlers, found that the older ones were more insistent on doing the very last step of a task themselves. If you have ever been told off for pressing the elevator button, you have met this finding.

## Pride Arrives

Somewhere near the second birthday, pride shows up. In one small study of twelve toddlers and their mothers, proud reactions to succeeding became common by about twenty months.

A larger set of studies found a shift after twenty-one months. Older toddlers looked up at the adult after doing something themselves, more than after watching the adult do the same thing. They also called their mothers over to see what they had done.

In those studies, praise added to children's pleasure in succeeding, but only modestly.

## Trying Is Catching

Persistence can be picked up by watching. In a set of experiments with two hundred and sixty-two fifteen-month-olds, some babies watched an adult struggle to reach a goal, trying different ways before succeeding. Others watched the adult succeed at once.

Then each baby was given a difficult new task. The babies who had seen the adult struggle made more attempts.

As Module 1 put it, your toddler is studying you. So let her see you try, fail, and try again.

## When Adults Take Over

The opposite also seems to be true. In experiments with four- and five-year-olds, an adult either took over a task, taught the child how to do it, or did neither. The children whose task was taken over persisted less on the next one.

Those children were older, but a study of two-year-olds points the same way. Mothers taught their toddlers a hard task, and researchers scored how they did it. A year later, the children of mothers who had supported their child's own efforts were less likely to avoid challenges.

Children whose mothers had criticized them showed more shame at three, and children who had been corrected and encouraged kept at things longer. So stepping in too fast may have a cost, even when it is kindly meant.

## Toddlers Join the Work

Now put that drive next to a basket of laundry. In a classic study, researchers set up a room like a home and asked parents and other adults to do ordinary chores there. Nobody asked the children to do anything.

Children of eighteen, twenty-four, and thirty months joined in with most of the chores, promptly and on their own. They talked about what they were doing, they showed that they understood the goals, and they even added fitting steps that no adult had shown them.

Experiments with children as young as fourteen to eighteen months show a readiness to help a stranger with a simple problem, and the Preschool class tells that story in full.

## Why They Join In

Why do they do it? One study followed toddlers at eighteen, twenty-one, and twenty-four months through a shared chore. Partway through, the adult was interrupted and stopped working.

At eighteen months, toddlers were less likely to keep going once the adult had stopped. At twenty-one and twenty-four months, they were more likely to carry on by themselves.

The researchers' reading is that toddlers begin by helping because they want to be part of what you are doing. Over the following months, that grows into helping out of concern and a sense of responsibility. On this reading, the company comes first, and the sense of contributing follows.

## Happily Unhelpful

Toddler help is real, and it is also often not helpful. A survey of more than five hundred families with children from one to four asked about nine common chores.

Most parents reported that their child's help was sometimes unhelpful. The researchers describe young children joining in cheerfully, including in ways that make the chore harder, and they titled their paper happily unhelpful.

That is worth knowing in advance. Imperfect helping is what most parents report at this age, and you can expect it without reading it as a failure of the plan.

## What Parents Do That Helps

How do parents bring toddlers in? In one study, forty-six parents did a household chore with their toddler. With eighteen-month-olds, parents gave small concrete requests, like put this one in the basket, and kept guiding the child's attention.

With two-year-olds, parents talked more about what was needed and about the child's part as a helper, and they voiced more approval. So parents used different approaches at the two ages.

An experiment shows that encouragement matters early on. Among babies under fifteen months, those who were encouraged and praised helped twice as often as those who weren't, and they kept helping afterward. Older babies were not affected by it.

## Around the World

Toddlers help in very different cultures, and families ask for that help in different ways. One study compared families in rural Brazil, in a Brazilian city, and in a German city, with children from eighteen to thirty months.

Brazilian mothers tended to be serious and insistent, and German mothers tended to ask, explain, and coax. In the rural Brazilian families and in the German families, the local way of asking went with more helping.

In another study, eighteen-month-olds in Delhi helped more than eighteen-month-olds in a German city. The Delhi mothers reported giving their toddlers more chances to help around the home, and praising them less for it. And in interviews in the United States, Mexican-heritage and European American mothers both said that most of their two- and three-year-olds helped on their own initiative.

## Thanks, Not Payment

What about rewards for helping? The Preschool class covers that research, and the short version is that giving a toddler a prize for helping made helping less likely later.

Toddlers don't seem to need one. In a study of Dutch and Chinese toddlers, children looked happier after helping an adult than they had before, and being thanked did not change that. The helping itself seemed to be rewarding.

So a warm thank you is plenty. Tell him what the help did, for example that now the spoons are all put away, and leave the prizes out of it.

## Real Jobs for Small Hands

What can a toddler actually do? Think of real jobs with a clear ending, cut down to one small piece. A one-year-old can drop socks into the laundry basket, put a spoon in the drawer, or carry a clean diaper to you.

A two-year-old can wipe a spill with a cloth, pour a scoop of food into the dog's bowl, put shoes by the door, or water one plant. Looking after himself counts too. By eighteen months, most children help with dressing by pushing an arm through a sleeve, and hold out their hands to be washed.

Choose jobs that truly need doing, because finishing something real is what he is after.

## Slowing Down

The hard part is time. A toddler's help is slow, and most family mornings are not. So choose your moments, and build the extra minutes into one or two parts of the day where you can afford them. It helps to see the slow version as practice, because when researchers described getting dressed to parents of young children as a chance to learn, the parents took over far less, by nearly half.

Give him one piece of the job, and make it the last piece when you can. You load the washing machine, and he pushes the door shut. Remember that older toddlers are especially keen to do the final step themselves.

Then wait a little longer than is comfortable before you step in. If he is stuck, help with the smallest thing that gets him moving again, and hand the job back.

## When It Goes Wrong

Things will spill, and that is part of the arrangement. When the water goes on the floor, say what happened and what comes next. The water spilled, so let's get the cloth.

Keep your words about the job, and not about what kind of child he is. Research with older children, which the Preschool class describes, suggests that being called a helper can make a mishap feel worse.

And try not to redo his work in front of him. If the towels have to be refolded, do it later, so that what he takes from the moment is that his work counted.

## When Me Do It Isn't Possible

Sometimes me do it can't happen, because the stove is hot or the bus leaves in four minutes. This is a job for the warm limit from the last lesson.

Name the wish, hold the limit, and offer a part. You want to do the buckle yourself. Today I'm doing the buckle, and you can pull the strap tight. Tomorrow we'll leave time for you to try.

Then keep that promise when you can. The message to protect is that his wish to do things is welcome in your home, even on the days when there is no time for it.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

Me do it is the self at work. Toddlers want to make things happen and to finish them, and pride follows from about twenty months.

Toddlers are helpers in the family. They join real work on their own, and their help is often imperfect at first.

Slow down and hand over a piece. Give a real job with a clear end, wait before stepping in, and thank him without prizes.
