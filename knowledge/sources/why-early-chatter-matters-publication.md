## Why Early Chatter Matters — early vocabulary as the building blocks of the mind

This is from a peer-reviewed publication by the Growing Minds Science founder: McArthur, M. & Friend, M. (2024), "Why Early Chatter Matters: The Role of Language in Shaping Futures," *Frontiers for Young Minds*, 12:1426649 (doi:10.3389/frym.2024.1426649), from the Infant and Child Development Lab at San Diego State University.

The words children learn early in life are building blocks for the brain. As a child learns new words and the concepts behind them, they build the foundation on which future learning, relationships, and achievements rest. A rich early vocabulary opens the door to understanding complex ideas, solving problems, and expressing thoughts and emotions more clearly. Early language is even linked to distant future outcomes such as academic success in high school and employment in adulthood. A helpful image is a snowball rolling downhill: the developing mind is the snowball and words are the snow — the more a child knows, the more easily they learn new things, because they have a foundation on which to build.

## From cooing to first words

Before babies speak, they move through stages of cooing and babbling. Although these can sound like random noises, responsive caregivers treat them as meaningful and engage in back-and-forth, serve-and-return interactions — like serving and returning a ball in ping-pong. These early "conversations" lay the groundwork for later language. Cooing typically begins around two months, as babies experiment with soft vowel-like sounds ("oo," "ah"), learning to control their vocal cords and breathing. Around six months, babbling adds consonant–vowel combinations ("ba-ba," "ga-ga") and more deliberate practice at communicating. The leap to first words, usually around one year, is remarkable: words like "mama" and "dada" mark the dawning understanding that words refer to real people, objects, and events in the world — a toddler's first concepts.

## The vocabulary explosion and word-to-world mapping

Between 18 and 24 months, children learn new words rapidly — it is not unusual to learn several new words a day. At 18 months most children say fewer than 100 words, but vocabulary builds at an ever-increasing rate, reaching roughly 300 words by age two; on average children learn more than 150 new words across that six-month window (based on large-scale vocabulary data from Wordbank, an open repository of developmental vocabulary). Crucially, children do not just say words — they learn what the words mean. Learning "dog" means learning what a dog is, what it does, and how it differs from a cat or a bird. This "word-to-world mapping" helps children organize objects by name and carry knowledge from one experience to the next. As vocabulary grows, words become tools for grasping increasingly complex concepts: mastering comparison words like "bigger" and "smaller" introduces ideas foundational to mathematical and scientific thinking, and a broad vocabulary underpins reading comprehension.

## Language, learning, and connection

Early vocabulary supports mathematics, reading comprehension, writing, and social-emotional skills, which in turn support high-school success and employment outcomes later in life. Language is also central to connection: when children learn words to describe emotions ("I'm sad," "I'm happy"), they are better equipped to manage those feelings and communicate them. A child who can articulate what they want or need is more likely to have those needs met — vital for emotional well-being and self-esteem. Language lets children recognize and respond to others' emotions, engage in collaborative play, negotiate roles, and resolve conflicts.

## The environment: serve-and-return and the brains of parent and child

Children first experience language at home through conversations, stories, and songs. The most important feature of this early exposure is that it is *dependent on the child's responses* — it is interactive, not just ambient. These serve-and-return interactions can even be seen in the brains of parents and children: a study using magnetoencephalography (MEG) found that caregiver and child brain activity becomes synchronized during turn-taking interactions. When caregivers talk with their children, respond to their babbling, and encourage their attempts at words, they act like fertilizer for the brain, strengthening the connections that support language. Children who have more of these interactions show greater activation in language-related brain regions. This is the deeper point behind the research that moved "beyond the 30-million-word gap": it is the back-and-forth conversational exchange — responsive interaction — not the sheer number of words overheard, that is most strongly tied to language-related brain function. Environments vary in many ways (household size, access to books and games, languages spoken), which is why sensitive, responsive caregivers — parents, grandparents, and teachers alike — matter so much for early word learning.
