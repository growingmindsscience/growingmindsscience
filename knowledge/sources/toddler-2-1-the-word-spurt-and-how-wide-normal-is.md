## Overview

This is Lesson 2.1 of the From Little Onward class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 2 (Language, the Everyday Version). It covers: How language goes from first words to first little sentences, why word learning speeds up, how far apart two ordinary toddlers can be, and what an early word count can and cannot tell you.

Two cousins, both twenty months old, are playing at a family dinner. One is asking for more juice and announcing that the dog is gone. The other says about ten words and gets everything else done by pointing, and both sets of parents are quietly comparing.

In this lesson, we'll follow language from first words to first little sentences. We'll look at the famous word spurt, and at how wide the range of normal really is. We'll finish with what an early word count can tell you, and what it can't.

## First Words

Most children say their first words around their first birthday, and then, for a while, not much seems to happen.

It helps to know what counts as a word at this age, because it doesn't need to sound right. If your child says ba every time she wants her ball, and only then, that is a word.

The checklists that American doctors use give a floor for this stage. At least three in four children try one or two words besides mama and dada by fifteen months, and three or more by eighteen months. Those figures mark the low end of the range, since most children are already past them.

## Understanding Runs Ahead

Underneath that slow start, a lot is going on. The Infant class showed that understanding comes before talking, and in the toddler years the gap between the two is easy to see.

One small study followed eight babies closely and listed the first fifty words they understood and the first fifty they said. The children understood fifty words at around thirteen months. They did not say fifty words until about eighteen months.

Understanding was also growing about twice as fast as talking. So a toddler with ten spoken words may be carrying a much bigger stock of words she knows, and it pays to notice what your child follows as well as what she says.

## The Pace Picks Up

Around eighteen months, the pace changes. Across large collections of parent reports, most children say fewer than a hundred words at eighteen months. By their second birthday, a typical child says roughly three hundred.

That is more than a hundred and fifty new words in six months, which works out to nearly one new word a day. The first fifty words took about half a year.

This speeding up is what people call the word spurt, or the vocabulary explosion. It raises an obvious question about what suddenly switched on.

## Is It Really a Spurt?

For a long time, the answer seemed to be that something clicks at around eighteen months. When researchers tracked children one by one, the picture turned out to be less tidy.

In one study of eighteen children, thirteen showed a burst of new words, mostly names for things, that lasted up to three months. The other five built their vocabularies more gradually.

A later study of thirty-eight children used a statistical test of whether each child's growth had a true turning point. Only five of them showed one. For the rest, word learning sped up smoothly, more like a ramp than a step.

## Why It Speeds Up

So why does word learning speed up at all? One explanation needs no special switch. A child is working on many words at the same time, and words differ in how hard they are to learn.

A few words are easy, perhaps because a child hears them constantly and what they point to is obvious. Many more are harder and take longer. The easy few arrive first, one at a time. Then the large group of harder words, which the child has been working on all along, starts to arrive together.

A computer simulation built on only those two ideas produced the same slow start and the same speeding up. You can picture a snowball rolling downhill, which stays small for a long while and then grows fast.

## How Wide Normal Is

Now back to those two cousins. The best-known survey of early vocabulary found that the spread between children is very wide.

It gathered parent reports on about eighteen hundred children. At two years old, a child in the middle of the group said roughly three hundred words. About one child in ten said fewer than sixty, and about one in ten said more than five hundred.

Children across most of that spread are developing in the ordinary way. The researchers concluded that a spread this wide challenges the idea of a single typical child.

## Who Talks Early?

Parents often hear that girls talk earlier than boys, or that first children talk earlier than later ones. Both have some truth to them, and both matter much less than you might think.

A study combining nearly fourteen thousand children from ten language communities found girls slightly ahead of boys in gestures, in spoken words, and in combining words. In the survey of eighteen hundred children, though, whether a child was a boy or a girl explained only one or two percent of the differences between children.

Birth order and family background explained even less at this age. So knowing that a child is a boy, or a third child, tells you almost nothing about how many words he will have.

## Two Languages

A child growing up with two languages needs a slightly different count. In one study of toddlers from about two to two and a half, children learning two languages knew fewer words in each language than children learning only one.

When the researchers added the two languages together, the totals were comparable. How many words a child had in each language went along with how much of that language she heard.

So if your toddler hears two languages, count the words in both. Hearing two languages does not cause a language delay, and we'll come back to that later in this module. The Preschool class looks at two languages from three to five.

## Faster at Understanding

While the spoken words pile up, something less visible is changing too. Toddlers get much faster at understanding the words they already know.

Researchers measure this by showing a child two pictures, naming one of them, and filming where her eyes go. At fifteen months, children did not look to the right picture until the word was over.

By two, they were moving their eyes to the right picture before the word had even finished. They were working out the word from its first sounds, which is how adults listen.

## Why Speed Matters

That speed turns out to matter. In one study, children were tested this way at eighteen months and then followed for a year. The ones who were quicker to recognize familiar words went on to add vocabulary faster, up to two and a half.

The study included a group of toddlers who were saying very few words at eighteen months. Among them, the quicker ones were more likely to bloom and pick up speed over the following year.

So two toddlers with the same short list of spoken words can be in quite different places underneath. It is one more reason that a word count tells you only part of the story.

## Two Words Together

The next big step is putting two words together, as in more milk, daddy shoe, or doggie gone. Most children start somewhere between eighteen months and two years.

In one large set of parent reports, the girl in the middle of the group was combining words at about twenty months, and the boy in the middle at about twenty-two months. By their second birthday, at least three in four children say two words together.

Combining goes hand in hand with vocabulary. Across thousands of toddlers, the children with more words were the ones putting them together. These first sentences leave out the little words, and that is normal at this age.

## What Feeds the Snowball

The Infant class gave the main answer to what helps words grow, which is that back-and-forth conversation matters more than the sheer number of words a child hears. It also explained why the well-known figure of a thirty-million-word gap was not supported when it was tested again.

The toddler studies add detail. One followed families with all-day recordings at home. The children who heard more speech addressed directly to them were faster at understanding words, and had bigger vocabularies at two.

Speech that was simply going on around them did not predict vocabulary in that study. Toddlers can pick up a word by listening in, as we saw in the last lesson, but here it was talk aimed at the child that went with growth.

## Quality at Two

A second study looked closely at sixty lower-income families when the children were two. The researchers rated whether parent and child were sharing attention on the same thing, whether they had familiar routines and games, and whether the conversation flowed back and forth.

Those qualities at two explained about a quarter of the differences in children's spoken language a year later. They predicted considerably better than the number of words the mother said.

Thousands of all-day recordings add one more point. Among families of similar income and education, homes differ a great deal in how much back-and-forth a child gets, and the next lesson is about how to do it.

## What Early Words Predict

Parents naturally wonder what a word count at two says about the future. Vocabulary at two does predict later language and reading. One study of more than a thousand children found the link still there in fifth grade.

Two large Danish studies found the same for reading in sixth grade and for school exams at fifteen, but the link is a modest one. In the exam study, everything known about a child before three, including vocabulary and family background, explained between about a tenth and a fifth of the differences at fifteen.

So most of the story is still unwritten at two. An early talker is not guaranteed anything, and a slower start does not settle anything, although very low counts do carry more risk.

## Using the Numbers Well

What should you do with all this? Start by treating word counts as a rough guide. A list on the fridge can be fun, but comparing your child with a cousin or a neighbor will mostly mislead you.

Watch the direction more than the number. Look for new words appearing from month to month, for your child following more of what you say, and for pointing and gestures that keep the conversation going.

If the words are few and those other signs are missing too, or if your instinct says something is off, that deserves a closer look. The last lesson in this module is about exactly when to check and how to ask.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

The word spurt is a snowball. Words come slowly at first and then speed up, and for many children it is a smooth ramp with no sudden switch.

Normal is very wide at two. Some two-year-olds say well under a hundred words, and others say several hundred.

Watch understanding and direction together. What your child follows, and whether new words keep coming, adds a lot to any single count.

## Common myths

Myth: "There is a vocabulary explosion at 18 months, when something switches on."
What the research shows: When individual children were tracked, only 5 of 38 showed a true turning point (Ganger & Brent, 2004), and a simulation shows that smooth acceleration needs no special mechanism (McMurray, 2007).

Myth: "Girls talk much earlier than boys."
What the research shows: Girls are slightly ahead on average (Eriksson et al., 2012), but sex explains 1 to 2 percent of the differences between children (Fenson et al., 1994).
