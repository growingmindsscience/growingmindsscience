## Overview

This is Lesson 2.3 of the Growing Minds class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 2 (Language, the Everyday Version). It covers: What late talking is, how many late talkers catch up, what tilts the odds, where hearing, ear infections, and two languages fit in, and when and how to ask for an evaluation.

At her son's two-year checkup, a mother mentions that he says about fifteen words. His grandmother points out that his father talked late too, and he turned out fine. A friend says to wait until he is three, and the mother isn't sure whose advice to take.

In this lesson, we'll look at what late talking is, how often children catch up, and what tilts the odds. We'll cover hearing, ear infections, and two languages. We'll finish with when to check, and how to ask for an evaluation.

## What Late Talking Means

Researchers use the term late talker for a toddler, usually between eighteen months and two and a half, who says far fewer words than other children the same age and has no other known difficulty.

On one well-tested checklist that parents fill in, a two-year-old counts as a late talker with fewer than fifty words, or with no two-word combinations yet.

Other studies simply take the lowest tenth of children on a vocabulary checklist. Either way, the term describes where a child is today. It is not a diagnosis, and it says nothing yet about why.

## How Common, and Who

Late talking is common, at roughly one toddler in eight. A study of nearly eighteen hundred Australian two-year-olds and a Canadian study of about a thousand families both found it in about thirteen percent.

Two things raised the odds in both studies. One was being a boy, and the other was having a relative who talked late or had a speech or language difficulty.

In the Australian study, parents' education, income, mental health, and parenting style were not linked to late talking. Other studies have found some links with family circumstances, but in one large project everything the researchers measured explained no more than seven percent of the differences at two. So late talking is usually not a sign that a parent did something wrong.

## Most Late Talkers Catch Up

Here is the reassuring part. A British study followed more than eight thousand twins. It took the children in the lowest tenth for vocabulary at two, and looked at them again at three and at four.

At three, a little under half still had language difficulties, and at four it was about four in ten. So roughly six in ten had moved out of difficulty by four.

A review of the longer studies agrees. Most late talkers score in the average range on language tests by the time they are five, six, or seven.

## Catching Up Isn't the Whole Story

The same studies carry a second message. As a group, late talkers stay a little behind children who talked on time, even after they reach the average range.

One study followed twenty-six late talkers to seventeen. They scored in the average range on every language and reading test, but lower than a matched group on vocabulary, grammar, and memory for words.

A study of ninety-six hundred American children found that late talkers at two were more likely to have a small vocabulary at four, and to be less ready for school at five. For a minority the difficulty lasts, and the Preschool class describes that under the name developmental language disorder.

## Can Anyone Tell Which Child?

It would help to know in advance which toddlers will catch up. Unfortunately, nobody can reliably tell for any one child.

In the twin study, the researchers tried to predict which two-year-olds would still be struggling at three and four. Their predictions were too inaccurate to be useful for any one child. The toddlers with the fewest words were not necessarily the ones whose difficulties lasted.

This is the real problem with waiting. The odds favor your child, but the odds describe a group, and you are raising one child.

## What Tilts the Odds

A few signs do shift the odds, and one of the best supported is understanding. In a small early study, every late talker who was still behind a year later had also been behind in understanding at the start.

A review that combined twenty groups of late talkers, more than two thousand children, agreed. How much a toddler understood, and how many words she had, both predicted how she was talking in the preschool years.

Gestures are another sign, because in that small early study the children who stayed behind had also used fewer gestures. Family history raises the chance of talking late, but in the combined review it did not clearly predict who caught up, though some newer studies suggest it adds to the risk.

## Check Hearing First

Whenever a toddler is slow to talk, the first thing to check is hearing. A child who passed the newborn hearing test can still have a hearing loss that began later, as the Infant class mentioned.

In one study in Shanghai, more than twenty-one thousand young children who had passed as newborns were screened again. Fewer than one in a thousand had a permanent hearing loss, though estimates vary widely between studies. Most of those children had no known risk factor, so nothing would have flagged them.

A hearing test for a toddler is painless, and a normal result rules hearing out as the cause for now.

## Ear Infections

Ear infections are the other hearing question, because the fluid that can stay behind the eardrum afterward muffles sound for a while.

A review that combined studies following children over time found either no link or a very small one between fluid in the ears in the early years and later speech and language.

In a large American trial, children under three with long-lasting fluid were given ear tubes either promptly or up to nine months later if the fluid was still there. The two groups did not differ in language or thinking up to age six, or in reading and schoolwork at nine to eleven. So in an otherwise healthy child, ear fluid is unlikely to be the whole explanation, though it is still worth raising with your doctor.

## Two Languages

Families who use two languages are often told that this is why a toddler is slow to talk, but the research does not show that.

One study matched thirty-one toddlers learning two languages with thirty learning one. At thirteen months and at twenty months, the two groups did not differ in how many words they said. The authors suggested that when a child with two languages seems slow, the reason should be looked for somewhere else.

So count your child's words across both languages. If the total is low, treat it as you would for any child, and there is no need to drop a language.

## Markers Worth Knowing

Here are some markers to hold in mind. They come from the checklists American doctors use, which were revised to show what at least three in four children can do at each age.

By fifteen months, a child tries to say one or two words besides mama and dada. By eighteen months, she tries three or more words and follows a simple request without a gesture. By two, she puts two words together. By two and a half, she says about fifty words.

Since most children have reached each marker by that age, a missed one is not a reason to panic, but it is a reason to ask now.

## Signs That Are About More Than Words

A few signs are about more than words, and they deserve a prompt conversation with your doctor. One is a toddler who rarely points to show you things, and another is one who often doesn't turn to her name.

In one study of the baby brothers and sisters of autistic children, those later diagnosed with autism were more likely to miss their name from nine months on. A single missed call means very little, though, because plenty of children who never developed autism missed sometimes too.

The third sign is losing skills. If your child stops using words she had, or stops waving or pointing, call your doctor soon. In a large review, roughly three in ten autistic children lost skills this way. None of these signs is a diagnosis, and each is a reason to have someone look.

## Wait and See?

For years, the usual advice to a mother like the one in our story was to wait and see, because most late talkers do catch up.

That advice has been changing. When the American checklists were revised, one stated aim was to discourage waiting. Speech and language specialists have argued the same, because the children who won't catch up should not be overlooked, and nobody can reliably pick them out.

A national panel found too little evidence to say whether doctors should screen young children who show no signs and whose parents have no concerns. If you have a concern, you are already past that point, and asking is the sensible step.

## What Help Looks Like

Help for a late-talking toddler often means coaching for you. A specialist shows you the moves from the last lesson and helps you use them often.

In a German trial, mothers of two-year-olds who had few words but understood well took a three-month course. At three, three quarters of their children were talking in the normal range, compared with fewer than half of the children who waited.

Coaching doesn't fix everything, though. In an American trial with toddlers who were behind in both understanding and talking, it improved understanding but not talking, at least in the short term. Early help is worth having, and it is not a guarantee.

## How to Ask

Start with your child's doctor. Say plainly what you have noticed, ask for a hearing test, and ask about seeing a speech and language specialist.

In the United States there is a second door. Every state has an early intervention program for children under three. You can call it yourself and ask for an evaluation. You don't need a referral or a diagnosis, and the evaluation is free.

From age three, the same request goes to your local public school, and the Preschool class covers checks from three to five. In other countries, your doctor or health visitor is the place to start. Wherever you are, you can simply say that you have concerns about your child's language and would like an evaluation.

## While You Wait

While you wait for an appointment, keep doing what the last lesson described.

Try not to push for words. Accept a point or a sound as a real turn in the conversation, and give the word back.

And go easy on yourself. The Australian study found no link with parents' education or parenting style, and the clearest risks were being a boy and family history. Asking for a check does not mean that something is wrong, because it is simply how you find out.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

Most late talkers do catch up. About six in ten are out of difficulty by four, though nobody can reliably tell in advance which ones.

Understanding matters as well as talking. A toddler who follows what you say and uses gestures has better odds, and hearing should always be checked.

If you're worried, ask now. In the United States, you can request a free evaluation yourself before age three, with no referral.

## Common myths

Myth: "He'll grow out of it. Just wait."
What the research shows: About six in ten do move out of difficulty by four (Dale et al., 2003), but prediction for an individual child is poor, and late talkers as a group stay somewhat behind (Rescorla, 2009, 2011).

Myth: "It's the ear infections."
What the research shows: Associations are nil to very small (Roberts et al., 2004), and prompt ear tubes made no difference to development (Paradise et al., 2007).

Myth: "Two languages are confusing him."
What the research shows: Matched bilingual and monolingual toddlers did not differ in words produced (De Houwer et al., 2013).
