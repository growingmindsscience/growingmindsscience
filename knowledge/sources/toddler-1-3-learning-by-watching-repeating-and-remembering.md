## Overview

This is Lesson 1.3 of the Growing Minds class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 1 (The Toddler Brain, Briefly). It covers: How toddlers learn from watching and overhearing, how long they remember, where pretend play comes from, why they ask for the same book again, what a normal attention span looks like, and how a steady adult keeps stress from getting in the way.

A father takes a quick phone call at breakfast, pacing the kitchen as he talks. A week later, his twenty-month-old picks up a banana, holds it to her ear, and paces the same stretch of floor, chatting away.

In this lesson, we'll see how much toddlers learn by watching, and how long they remember it. We'll look at why they ask for the same book again and again, and at what a normal attention span looks like. We'll finish with what stress does to all this learning, and how you help.

## Copying Later

Copying something long after you saw it is a big step, because it means holding a memory with no reminder in front of you. Researchers have tested this with a simple method.

In a classic study, fourteen-month-olds watched an adult do six things with six objects, including one action no baby would think of alone. The babies were not allowed to touch anything.

A week later, they came back and were handed the objects. They performed clearly more of the actions than babies who had never seen them, and that included the odd one.

## How Long It Lasts

How long a memory lasts grows quickly across these two years. In one set of experiments, eighteen-month-olds could copy a set of actions two weeks after seeing it.

Two-year-olds still remembered after eight weeks, and showed some signs of remembering at three months. By six months, the memory was gone.

Another study found that babies of fourteen and sixteen months could still copy several actions two months and even four months later. The numbers differ from study to study, depending on the task. Where ages were compared directly, though, older toddlers remembered for longer.

## Memory That Travels

Early memories are also tied tightly to the exact thing a child saw. In one study, one-year-olds who had watched actions on one object often could not repeat them the next day on a slightly different one.

By eighteen months that kind of change no longer got in the way, and up to two and a half memories became more flexible still.

Giving things a name helps with this. When an adult named the object while showing what to do, and again later, two-year-olds were better at using what they had learned on a different object. So talking about what you are doing helps a memory travel.

## Watching When You Aren't Teaching

Toddlers also learn from things that were never aimed at them. In one set of studies, two-year-olds were nearby while two adults talked to each other and used a made-up word for a new object.

Children of two and a half learned the new word just as well by listening in as when an adult taught it to them directly. Other studies found that eighteen-month-olds can learn words this way too, though less reliably at that age.

There is one condition worth knowing. In a small separate study, toddlers learned by listening in only when both adults were paying attention to the conversation. What they learn from is real talk between people who are engaged with each other.

## Watching Feelings Too

Toddlers pick up feelings from the sidelines as well. In one experiment, eighteen-month-olds watched an adult play with a toy, and then saw a second adult react angrily to what the first had done.

When the toddlers were given the toy, they held back from copying, but only while the adult who had been angry could see them. If she left the room or turned her back, they went right ahead.

A study of a hundred and fifty children found the same pattern at fifteen months. So a toddler already tracks who is upset by what, and who is looking. Notice how much the holding back depended on being watched, which fits what we saw about the brakes in the last lesson.

## Pretend Begins

That banana held up as a phone brings several of these skills together. It is a memory of something watched, replayed later, with one object standing in for another.

When researchers followed ninety-eight babies through the second year, copying from memory came first. Pretend play appeared later, mostly between eighteen and twenty-one months.

Early pretending usually copies a child's own day, like drinking from an empty cup. Then it turns outward to feeding a doll, and then one object begins to stand in for another. The Preschool class picks up the story of pretend play from three to five.

## You Are the Lesson

Put this together and one thing follows. Much of what a toddler learns in a day comes from watching the people she loves do ordinary things.

She studies how you sweep, how you greet the neighbor, and how you speak to the dog. She also studies what you do when you are frustrated, which the last lesson showed can be copied too.

This isn't a reason to put on a performance. Nobody is calm and kind all day, and toddlers also learn from watching you recover and make things right, which we'll come to in Module 4.

## Again, Again

Now to the book you have read forty times this month. There is some evidence that all that asking is doing your child good.

In one study, three-year-olds heard stories that contained two made-up words. Some children heard three different stories, and others heard the same story three times, so every child heard each new word equally often.

The children who heard the same story again and again learned the new words well. The children who heard different stories did not end up learning them. That study was with three-year-olds, and younger toddlers have been studied less.

## Why Repetition Works

Why would the same story teach more? One likely reason is that once a story is familiar, a child no longer has to work out what is happening. That leaves more attention for a new word or a new detail.

Repetition also suits a toddler's memory. As we saw, a toddler's memory of something seen once fades within weeks or months, and it is likely that meeting it again helps keep it alive.

The Infant class said to let repetition be the curriculum, and that still holds. The same song, the same game, and the same book are how a toddler makes knowledge stick, and Module 2 has more on reading with a toddler.

## A Normal Attention Span

Parents often worry that their toddler can't stick with anything. Researchers who have watched children at play find that deep, focused attention grows gradually through these years.

In one study, children were watched at ten months, at about two, and at three and a half. With age, casual looking gave way to more focused attention, and the youngest were the most easily pulled away.

So a one-year-old who flits from toy to toy is behaving like a one-year-old. You may see charts that promise a set number of minutes for each year of age. Those are rules of thumb and not research findings, because attention depends so much on the activity.

## Attention Is Shared

A toddler's attention is also not hers alone. Researchers put tiny cameras that track the eyes on one-year-olds and their parents while they played with toys.

When a parent looked at the toy the child was already looking at, the child stayed with it longer. When the parent also touched the toy or talked about it, the child's look lasted longer still, and talk went with the longest looks.

A later study added a twist. Talk that responded to what the child was doing stretched attention, and parents who talked the most overall got less out of each remark. So join in with what has already caught your child's eye.

## What Shortens It

A few things reliably shorten a toddler's focus. As the Infant class covered, a television left on in the background breaks up play, even when a child barely glances at it.

That class also described a study where toddlers played longer and more inventively with four toys than with sixteen. Interrupting works against you too, because each time an absorbed child is pulled to something else, that stretch of focus ends.

So you don't need to train attention. Protect the stretches that happen, by keeping the room calm, offering fewer things, and waiting a moment before you step in.

## Stress and Learning

All of this learning goes better when a child feels safe. The Infant class described how a trusted adult's presence can soften the body's stress response, and called that adult a buffer.

Studies of toddlers add a detail to that picture. In one study, seventy-seven eighteen-month-olds met a series of strange new things with their mothers present. The stress hormone rose only in toddlers who were both cautious by nature and less secure in their bond with their mother.

Cautious toddlers with a secure bond showed no such rise, and a second study found a similar pattern at a doctor visit. These are links and not proof, but they suggest that a steady relationship changes what a scary thing does inside the body.

## Three Kinds of Stress, Briefly

The Infant class also set out three kinds of stress. There is the brief, everyday kind, and the more serious kind that a child gets through with support. Then there is the severe, lasting kind without support, often called toxic stress, which is the one linked to harm.

Almost all of toddler life is the first kind. A hard puzzle, a short wait, a goodbye at the door, and a first haircut are small stresses that build coping when you are nearby.

So the aim is not a toddler who never feels stress. Your job is to be close enough that small stresses stay small, and that is something an ordinary, imperfect parent already does.

## Child Care, Honestly

Parents also read that child care raises stress hormones, so here is what the studies say. On average, a toddler's stress hormone rises a little across a day in group care, and falls across a day at home.

The effect is small, and it varies. In a study of three hundred and twenty toddlers, those in disorganized groups showed clearly more stress. A Finnish study even found higher overall levels in toddlers who were cared for at home.

No one has shown that this mild rise harms children, and researchers suspect it reflects the effort of a long day in a group. What seems to help is a warm bond with the caregiver, a predictable day, and a calm reunion with you, and Lesson 4.2 covers drop-offs.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

Your toddler is studying you. She learns from what she watches and overhears, and she can replay it days or weeks later.

Again is how toddlers learn. Repeating the same book, song, or game is doing real work.

Join in and stay close. Attention grows when you follow your child's focus, and small stresses stay small when you are near.

## Common myths

Myth: "Toddlers only learn what you teach them directly."
What the research shows: Two-year-olds learned new words as well by overhearing as by being taught (Akhtar et al., 2001), and 18-month-olds changed their behavior after watching an adult's anger at someone else (Repacholi & Meltzoff, 2007).

Myth: "Reading the same book over and over is a waste."
What the research shows: Three-year-olds learned new words from a repeated story and not from different stories with the same words (Horst et al., 2011).

Myth: "A two-year-old should be able to focus for four to six minutes."
What the research shows: Focused attention grows gradually and depends on the activity and on who joins in (Ruff & Capozzoli, 2003; Yu & Smith, 2016); no study found supports fixed minutes per year of age.

Myth: "Daycare floods toddlers with stress hormones."
What the research shows: A small average rise across the day (Vermeer & van IJzendoorn, 2006), much larger in disorganized groups (Nystad et al., 2024), with one study finding higher overall levels in home care (Tervahartiala et al., 2019).
