## Overview

This is Lesson 3.2 of the Growing Minds class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 3 (Autonomy and No). It covers: What it means to support a toddler's independence without handing over the house: choices that work and choices that backfire, how to hold a limit warmly, why explaining helps a little, and when to stop.

It is time to leave the playground. A father says so, and his two-year-old looks at him, laughs, and runs for the slide. He feels two instincts pulling at him, one to grab her and march to the car, and the other to plead, bargain, and offer a snack.

There is a third way, and it is where the research points. In this lesson, we'll look at what it means to support a toddler's independence, and at choices that work and choices that backfire. We'll see how to hold a limit warmly, why explaining helps a little, and when to stop.

## Two Ways to Get Cooperation

Researchers describe two broad ways of getting a child to do something. One, called controlling, leans on pressure, like threats or anger. The other is called supporting autonomy, which means protecting a child's sense that her actions are her own.

Supporting autonomy does not mean dropping the request. In a study of a hundred and eighty-two parents of two-year-olds, researchers identified a handful of habits used while asking for something dull but necessary. Parents showed they understood how the child felt, gave a reason the child could follow, described the problem without blame, and showed what to do.

The parents who used those habits more had toddlers who had taken the family's rules on board more fully.

## What It Does for Trying

The studies of toddlers start at twelve months. In one, forty-one mothers played with their one-year-olds. The babies whose mothers supported their own efforts kept at a toy longer, and handled it more skillfully.

A second study measured the same thing at twelve and at twenty months. Toddlers whose mothers supported their independence were more persistent and more capable in play than toddlers whose mothers were more controlling.

So the first thing this style seems to go with is a toddler's willingness to keep trying.

## What It Does for Rules

The more surprising finding is about rules. Researchers watched about a hundred two-year-olds with a parent during two tests of cooperation. One was cleaning up toys, and the other was leaving an attractive toy alone.

They scored how each parent handled it, and then saw the children again at three and a half. Children whose parents had used more supportive strategies at two showed more wholehearted cooperation by three and a half. Children whose parents had used more controlling strategies showed less than before.

Wholehearted cooperation, remember, is the kind that lasts when you leave the room, so pressure may cost more than it gains.

## Over the Longer Run

Longer studies point the same way. In one, mothers' support for independence at around fifteen months was the strongest predictor, among the parenting qualities measured, of how well children could hold back an impulse and keep a rule in mind at eighteen months and at two.

A study of more than thirteen hundred children found that mothers' support for independence in the first three years predicted self-control before kindergarten, and school achievement all the way to high school.

Studies like these watch families and can't prove cause. The Preschool class describes an experiment that tested the idea directly with three-year-olds.

## A Say Is Not the Same as Being in Charge

None of this means letting a toddler run the house, because having a say is different from being in charge.

In a classic experiment, first and second graders were given rules for a painting activity, either as orders or in an informing way, and a third group had no rules.

The children who got the orders painted with less interest and less creativity. The children who got the same limits in the informing way did as well as the children with no limits. Those children were older than toddlers, but the point carries: a limit can be firm and still respect the child.

## Why Choices Help

One of the simplest ways to give a toddler a say is to offer a choice. A review that combined forty-one studies found that having a choice raised interest, effort, and performance, and the effect on interest was stronger for children than for adults.

In that review, small choices worked best, like which color or which cup, and two to four choices in a row worked better than fewer or more.

Newer studies add toddlers to the picture. In a sticker game, children of about two and a half chose to have a choice on seventy percent of turns, and in child care rooms, two-year-olds stayed with a toy longer when a teacher offered a choice of two than when she handed over one. Whether choosing changes behavior is less settled, since studies of preschoolers choosing vegetables have gone both ways.

## Choices That Work

For a toddler, a choice that works has three features. It has two options, both options are fine with you, and it sits inside the limit instead of replacing it.

So the limit is that we are leaving the playground, and the choice is whether to walk to the car or ride on your shoulders. The limit is that teeth get brushed, and the choice is the red toothbrush or the blue one.

Then honor what she picks. A choice you overrule teaches her that choosing is pointless, and the Preschool class returns to why real choices matter.

## Choices That Backfire

Choices can also go wrong, in three ways. The first is the wide-open choice. Adults in experiments were less likely to pick anything from twenty-four or thirty options than from six, although toddlers in the sticker game showed no such overload. The practical reason to offer two is that both must be answers you can accept.

The second is the false choice, which is a question asked when the answer isn't open. Asking whether she is ready to leave, or adding okay to the end of an instruction, invites a no that you can't accept.

The third is offering a choice about the limit itself, or in the middle of a meltdown. If something has to happen, say that it is happening, and put the choice somewhere smaller.

## Warm Limits

Toddlers need limits, as Module 1 said, and the question is how to hold one. The short answer from the research is to be warm and firm at the same time, which this class calls a warm limit.

A review of forty-one studies of preschoolers found that the way parents steer was linked to how well children cooperate, though the links were small. Toddler studies show the direction. Among thirty-month-olds, children whose mothers guided more and forced less cooperated more wholeheartedly.

In a study of two hundred and fifty-six families, insistent control at eighteen months went with less wholehearted cooperation two years later.

## Name the Feeling, Hold the Limit

A warm limit has two parts. You name the feeling, and you hold the limit. You are sad to leave the swings, and it is time to go.

The first part is not a trick to get your way. It tells your child that her feeling makes sense, even though it doesn't change the answer.

The two common slips each drop one half. One is naming the feeling and then giving up the limit. The other is holding the limit while brushing the feeling aside, which Module 1 showed tends to make the upset stronger. Accepting a feeling while holding a limit means keeping both.

## Say What You Will Do, Then Do It

Holding a limit with a toddler usually means acting as well as talking. Say what you are going to do, and then do it calmly. I'm going to pick you up now, and we'll walk to the car.

In one experiment, mothers were on the phone while their toddlers kept demanding attention. Some mothers were asked to reprimand every time. Others were asked to reprimand half the time, and to give warm attention the other half.

The toddlers who got the mix demanded more, and were more upset. Giving in some of the time seemed to teach them to keep pushing, so the same answer each time works better.

## Why Explaining Helps a Little

Should you explain your limits to a toddler? The evidence says a little. In a study of sixty-three mothers and toddlers, researchers recorded their disagreements at two and a half.

Some mothers gave reasons, softened their position where they could, and brought disagreements to a real ending. Six months later, their children scored higher on a set of measures that included understanding feelings and early conscience.

A second study found that reasoning with older toddlers went with better social skills later, while limits and prohibitions alone showed no such link. So giving reasons goes with better understanding later, even on days when it changes nothing in the moment.

## When to Stop Explaining

The trouble starts when explaining is the whole plan. A review written for children's doctors looked at studies of reasoning with toddlers and preschoolers. In most circumstances, words alone did not change young children's problem behavior.

Another study followed two- and three-year-olds. Disruptive behavior rose most where parents reasoned often but never backed it up with a consequence. It fell most where parents reasoned often and did back it up when they needed to.

One research measure of discipline even counts long-winded talking as a common mistake. So give one short reason, once, and then act. In a full meltdown even that is too much, as Module 4 will show.

## Getting Ahead of It

The easiest limit to hold is the one you never need. In a study of twenty-four mothers shopping with their two-and-a-half-year-olds, every mother used two kinds of tactics. Some were reactions to trouble, and others came first, by steering the child's attention elsewhere.

The mothers who used more of the second kind had children who did fewer of the things their mothers didn't want. A study of three-year-olds found the same split, and the children of mothers who mostly reacted had more behavior problems two years later.

So think ahead to the hard spots in your day, and fill them before they fill themselves. Module 1 covered distraction and changing the room, and Module 4 covers routines.

## Back at the Playground

Let's go back to the playground and put it together. Five minutes before leaving, the father gives a heads-up, and then tells his daughter that this is the last slide.

When it is time, he gets close and says it plainly. It's time to go home for dinner, and you wish we could stay. Do you want to walk or ride on my shoulders? She runs anyway, so he tells her he is going to pick her up, and he does it without anger.

She may cry all the way to the car, and that doesn't mean it failed. He named the feeling, held the limit, offered a real choice, gave one reason, and followed through. Repeated over months, with your help, that is how limits get learned.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

Give a say, and keep the limit. Supporting a toddler's independence goes with more trying now and more willing cooperation later.

Offer two real choices inside the limit. Skip the false question, and honor what she picks.

Name the feeling, hold the limit. Give one short reason, say what you will do, and then do it calmly.

## Next: I Do It

So a toddler can have a say without being in charge, and you can stay in charge without a fight over everything. Warm limits take longer to say than orders, and they ask more of you, but they are where the research points.
