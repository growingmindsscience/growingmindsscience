## Overview

This is Lesson 4.2 of the Growing Minds class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 4 (Big Feelings, Transitions, and Repair). It covers: Why changing activities sets off so many meltdowns, what warnings and previews can and can't do, how routines steady a toddler, how to handle the daycare goodbye, and how to see the pile-up before the storm.

It is five o'clock at the park, and a two-year-old has been told it is time to go home. He was happy a moment ago. Now he is running the other way, and when his mother catches up, the bucket goes flying and the screaming starts.

Lesson 4.1 showed that a refused wish and a change of activity are the two most common triggers, and this lesson is about the second. We'll look at why transitions set off meltdowns, what warnings and routines can and can't do, the daycare goodbye, and the pile-up before the storm.

## A Day Full of Transitions

A toddler's day is a chain of endings. Getting dressed, leaving the house, snack, play, lunch, nap, pickup, dinner, bath, and bed are each an ending as well as a beginning.

Researchers who watch early childhood classrooms count fifteen to twenty transitions a day, and in one study more than a third of the time observed was spent moving from one thing to the next.

Transitions are also where the trouble clusters. Among a hundred and forty-five preschoolers, children had more conflict during routines and transitions than in any other part of the day.

## Why Endings Are Hard

Why is stopping so much harder than starting? Two reasons stand out in the research. The first is loss, because leaving the park means giving up something good for something not yet real.

The second is unpredictability, since an ending from nowhere is one the child could not prepare for. Module 1 showed that a toddler can know a rule without being able to follow it, and that the brakes that would let her pause and switch are still being built.

So a two-year-old asked to stop mid-sandcastle must let go of a goal, hold a new one in mind, and manage the feeling in between, with equipment years from ready. The alarm from Lesson 1.2 fires over the loss, and the tantrum follows.

## What Warnings Can Do

The classic advice is to give a warning, and the research on warnings is small and more mixed than the advice suggests. In one study, forty preschoolers moved on more smoothly when given verbal warnings before an activity change.

Two other small studies found the opposite. A two-minute warning given to toddlers in child care did nothing on its own, and neither did a toy to carry through the transition or advance notice given to three noncompliant preschoolers.

What worked in those studies was the warning plus calm follow-through, the adult saying what was coming and then making it happen without a fuss. That is the warm limit from Lesson 3.2, and why a warning is a beginning rather than a solution.

## How to Preview

A toddler's preview is short, concrete, and close to the event. Tell her at the gate what will happen at the end, such as after the slide and the swings we go home for dinner, then give one warning a minute or two before in the same words, and name the last turn as the last turn.

Pictures help some families through transitions. The Preschool class describes picture sequences for three- to five-year-olds, and the toddler research is thinner, since picture schedules have mostly been tested with children who have autism or behavior problems, and the pictures alone did little, so a few photos by the door are a habit to try rather than a finding.

## Make the Ending Part of the Game

The best transitions give the toddler a job. Let him do the last step, which Lesson 3.3 showed older toddlers want anyway. He puts the bucket in the bag and carries it to the gate.

Give him something to carry, something to look for, or a song that belongs to leaving. In one study, transitions in which adults talked, praised, and played along produced fewer rule-breaking episodes than silent ones.

None of this stops every tantrum, and all of it lowers the count.

## Routines: What the Research Says

A routine is the slow version of the same idea, a transition that happens the same way every time, so that the ending is known before it arrives.

A review of a hundred and seventy studies found routines linked with better self-regulation, social skills, and health, most strongly in families under strain. In a British study of nearly eleven thousand three-year-olds, children with regular bedtimes and mealtimes had better emotional self-control.

Among nearly three thousand American low-income families, a consistent bedtime routine from fourteen to thirty-six months predicted better emotion regulation at three and fewer problems at ten. Lesson 1.2 gave the Chicago study, the Preschool class takes routines at three to five from here, and none of these patterns can prove that the routine itself does the work.

## Predictable Days, Steadier Children

Why would routines help a toddler? One answer comes from newer research on predictability itself, which times the moment-to-moment pattern of a parent's signals to a baby and measures instability in a household.

Across three groups of children in two countries, unpredictable parental signals early in life went with more fearfulness later, and in an American cohort of nearly five thousand children, unpredictability from one to nine predicted anxiety and behavior problems at fifteen.

None of these studies tested previews or routines, so the link is indirect, but they show that a young brain does better when the world around it has a pattern, as Lesson 1.2 said of disorganized homes.

## Toddler Routines in Practice

A toddler routine is a sequence rather than a schedule, so the clock can move while the order stays. Bath, pajamas, two books, song, and bed, in that order, on most nights.

Anchor the day with a few fixed points, such as meals, nap, and bed, and let the rest flex around them. Say the sequence out loud as you go, because hearing it is how a toddler learns to expect it.

Keep routines few and short, because a toddler cannot hold a long sequence, and a routine that takes forty minutes is one you will skip. Expect them to carry less weight when your child is ill or the day is upside down.

## The Daycare Goodbye

Drop-off is the hardest transition of the day for many families, and the Infant class covered how parents leave. Parents of toddlers hovered and snuck out the most, long explanations and lingering went with more crying, and a repeated goodbye ritual went with less stress.

Two more studies add to that. In a German day care, one- to three-year-olds whose parents took a long time to leave played less in the first half hour afterward, and in an American preschool, the longer the parent lingered, the less the child settled.

Toddlers were the most distressed age group at drop-off, and by the second half of the year they protested less and their parents left faster, so the goodbye gets easier with practice.

## Settling In Takes Weeks

Starting child care is a transition of its own. In a Norwegian study of a hundred and thirteen toddlers, stress hormone levels were low while a parent was present, rose during the first weeks apart, and were back down by three months, when the children were doing well.

In a German study of seventy fifteen-month-olds, stress hormone levels in the first days without the mother were up to double those at home, and children whose mothers spent more days settling them in stayed securely attached.

So plan for a slow start where you can, and a softer evening after. Keep the goodbye short, with the coat on its hook, one wave at the window, a sentence about coming back after nap, a handover to the same adult, and out the door with a warm face.

## The Pile-Up

Most tantrums at transitions have help, because the child was already tired, or hungry, or had been holding it together in a noisy room all afternoon, and the transition was the last straw.

Lesson 1.2 covered the evidence, including the nap experiments and the way sensitive children reach their limit sooner, and toddlers new to daycare are worn out by evening in the first weeks.

Parents call this the pile-up. Hungry, tired, overstimulated, and lonely for you stack up, and a request that was fine at ten in the morning sets off a storm at five. The skill is to see the stack before the storm.

## Reading the Early Signs

The stack comes with warning signs. Lesson 4.1 described how anger often begins at low intensity, and whining, clinging, repeated demands, flopping, speeding up, or going quiet are a toddler's low-intensity anger.

In that window, a preview, a snack, a change of room, or a few minutes on your lap can change the direction of the hour. Lesson 1.2 and Lesson 3.2 showed that distraction and steering attention before trouble beat reacting to it.

Once the alarm has fired, the window is closed and the next lesson takes over.

## When the Day Is Off

Some weeks the routine is gone, because a trip, an illness, visitors, a move, or a new baby has turned the day upside down, and the number of tantrums goes up. The Family Systems class covers new siblings and family change.

Among more than two thousand children from three to five, those whose routines fell away over two years had more attention, behavior, and emotional problems than those whose routines held.

When the day is off, shrink it. Keep two anchors, usually meals and bed, lower the demands, and expect more meltdowns without reading them as a step backward. The routine is still there to return to.

## What Prevention Can't Do

Prevention lowers the count without taking it to zero, and parents who do everything in this lesson will still have a toddler who melts down several times a week.

Those tantrums come from a brain whose brakes are not finished, and they fade as the brakes grow. A warning without follow-through did nothing in the studies, and nor does a routine that is announced but not kept.

So the goal is fewer and shorter storms, and a toddler who knows what comes next most of the time. When a storm comes anyway, you have not failed, you have arrived at Lesson 4.3.

## Back to the Park

Go back to the park at five o'clock. At the gate on the way in, his mother says that after the slide and the swings they will go home for dinner, and ten minutes before leaving she says it again and names the last two pushes.

He puts the bucket in the bag himself, because that is his job, and carries it to the gate while they sing the leaving song. He is tired and hungry, so there is a banana in the car.

He may still cry at the gate, but he will probably cry less and for a shorter time, and tomorrow the sequence will be more familiar. That is what prevention looks like with a toddler, a pattern rather than a trick.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

Transitions are losses, and previews soften them. Tell your toddler what comes next, give one warning, and then follow through calmly.

Routines are endings that are known in advance. Keep a few short sequences and a few fixed anchors, and say them out loud.

Watch for the pile-up before the storm. Tired, hungry, and overloaded come before most meltdowns, and the early signs are your chance to act.

## Common myths

Myth: "Give a five-minute warning, then two minutes, then one."
What the research shows: In two of three small studies, a warning alone did nothing; what worked was the warning plus calm follow-through. The lesson teaches one warning in the same words, then the warm limit.

Myth: "Toddlers need a visual schedule."
What the research shows: Thin evidence. Picture schedules have been tested mostly with children who have autism or behavior problems, and in the best-controlled studies the pictures alone did little. Offered as a habit to try, not a finding.

Myth: "Routines make children resilient."
What the research shows: Routines are associated with better regulation and fewer problems across many families, with the strongest links in families under strain; the designs are correlational and cannot show that the routine itself does the work.

Myth: "Never let them see you leave; a quick exit while they are distracted is kindest."
What the research shows: Contradicted. Sneaking out went with more distress in the drop-off research the Infant class covers; a brief, warm, predictable goodbye went with less.
