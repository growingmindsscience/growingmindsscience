## Overview

This is Lesson 1.2 of the From Little Onward class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 1 (The Toddler Brain, Briefly). It covers: Why feelings rise faster than control, what the stress response does in a toddler's body, how stopping and waiting grow from one to three, why tired and overloaded toddlers cope less well, and why some children hit the wall faster.

It's half past five, dinner is ten minutes away, and an eighteen-month-old is handed the blue cup when he wanted the green one. Within seconds he is flat on the kitchen floor, and nothing you say seems to reach him.

In this lesson, we'll meet two systems in the brain, a fast alarm and slow brakes. We'll look at what the alarm does to a toddler's body, and at how stopping and waiting grow from one to three. We'll also see why tired or overloaded toddlers fall apart sooner, and why some children hit the wall faster than others.

## Two Systems

Deep in the brain sits a set of areas that work like an alarm. They notice a threat, a loss, or a blocked wish, and they launch a reaction in the body before any thinking is done.

At the front of the brain is the system we met in the last lesson. It works like brakes, slowing a reaction down so that a child can stop, wait, or switch.

This is a simplified picture, because the real brain works as connected networks and not as two separate boxes. Still, it explains a great deal about a toddler's day.

## The Alarm Gets a Head Start

The alarm gets a head start. In brain scans of eighty children from three months to five years, many of the alarm's connections already looked much like an adult's from infancy.

A larger study of more than two hundred babies found that these connections grow dramatically in the first year, and are then fine-tuned in the second.

That doesn't mean the alarm is finished at birth, as parents are sometimes told. It keeps developing through childhood, but it is well connected long before the brakes have matured.

## The Line Between Them

What comes later is the link between the alarm and the front of the brain. In that study of eighty children, the changes to this link that show up in older children had not yet appeared by age five.

One well-known study suggested that the link doesn't settle into its grown-up pattern until about ten. A later analysis found that result less solid, so the exact timing is still uncertain.

What the studies point to is the order. The alarm works early, the brakes come slowly, and the line between them is still being laid all through childhood.

## What the Alarm Does in the Body

When the alarm goes off, the body gets ready to act. The heart speeds up, muscles tighten, and stress hormones are released.

Those hormones are slow to rise. In one small study, toddlers between twelve and eighteen months were tested after mildly stressful events. Their main stress hormone was at its highest twenty minutes later in half of the children, and forty minutes later in the other half.

So a toddler's body can still be on alert well after the crying has stopped. Strong stress also makes the brakes work less well, as it does in adults. It is one reason that reasoning with a child in the middle of a meltdown rarely works, as we'll see in Module 4.

## The Brakes at Ten Months

So how early do the brakes exist at all? Researchers gave a hundred and thirty-five ten-month-olds a simple touchscreen game. Tapping one spot usually brought a reward, and now and then the baby had to hold back and tap somewhere else.

The babies wore a soft cap that uses light to measure activity in the brain. When holding back was needed, areas on the right side of the brain, at the front and toward the top, grew more active.

Those are similar to the areas older children and adults use for stopping. So the brakes aren't missing in a baby, even though they have a long way to go.

## Working Harder, Not Yet Better

The researchers saw the same children again at sixteen months. This time, a wider set of areas was at work, on both sides of the front of the brain.

Yet the toddlers were no better at the game than they had been at ten months. The brain was calling in more help without getting better results so far.

The researchers themselves say this finding needs to be repeated by others. When they tested the children once more at three and a half, they were clearly better at the game.

## Slow, Uneven Progress

Between those ages, progress is steady but slow. On the same kind of game, other toddlers improved bit by bit at eighteen, twenty-one, and twenty-four months. A separate group did better at two and a half than at two.

Progress is uneven as well as slow. At ten and sixteen months, a child who held back well in one game often didn't in another, and doing well early said little about doing well later.

Early skill does begin to tell by two and a half. In a study that followed ninety-six children, self-control at two and a half was strongly tied to self-control at five. Even so, a toddler's brakes will hold in one moment and fail in the next, and that is normal.

## Waiting for a Gift

Waiting shows the same slow growth. Researchers followed a hundred and twenty children from eighteen months to four years. At each age, the child had to wait eight minutes to open a gift while the mother was busy.

At eighteen months and at two, children were quick to get angry and slow to look away from the gift. By three, children typically turned to something else before showing anger, and their anger was shorter.

At four, a typical child spoke to her mother about the wait, and then found something to do. The big shift came between two and three.

## What Good Waiters Do

What separates a toddler who manages to wait from one who doesn't? In a study of sixty-two two- and three-year-olds waiting for a snack and a gift, two things predicted success.

The children who managed looked away from the treat. They also kept their own hands back, for example by holding one hand with the other, and they usually started within the first ten seconds.

So for a toddler, waiting is mostly about where the eyes and hands go. A child staring at the cookie is using weak brakes at full strength, and will not last long.

## Waiting Can Be Shown

Looking away can be learned by watching. In two experiments with children around two, some toddlers watched an adult handle a frustrating wait by turning to something else.

Afterward, those toddlers distracted themselves more during their own wait than children who had seen nothing. In one experiment it worked whether the adult was a parent or a stranger, and in the other, more distraction went along with less upset.

Parents can also offer it directly. In a study of a hundred and thirty-four mothers and two-year-olds, offering a distraction went with calmer waiting. Holding the child back, or brushing off the upset, went with stronger distress in the moments that followed.

## Tired

Everything in this lesson gets harder when a toddler is worn down, and sleep is the best-studied example. The Preschool class describes experiments in which children close to three skipped a single nap. That afternoon they showed more negative feeling, and less mature coping with a frustrating puzzle. Two other small experiments found no clear change in holding back itself, so the effect seems to fall on mood and coping.

The same pattern shows up over months. In a study that followed a hundred and seventeen babies to eighteen months, later bedtimes and less sleep went with more anxious, clingy, and withdrawn behavior.

A study of more than four thousand Australian children found a loop. Sleep problems were followed by more trouble managing feelings, which was followed by more sleep problems. These are links over time and not proof, but they fit what most parents see.

## Hungry and Overloaded

Hunger has been studied far less in toddlers, so here we lean mostly on what parents see every day. A small snack before a hard stretch of the day is cheap insurance.

Noise and disorder have been studied more. Across thirty-five studies of more than sixteen thousand children, homes that were more disorganized and less stable went with slightly weaker self-control, and the link was small.

In one study, two-year-olds without regular routines were worse at waiting when they were five. Noise and crowding made no difference once routines were counted. So a predictable day seems to matter more than a quiet or roomy house.

## Children Who Hit the Wall Faster

Some children reach their limit sooner than others. As the Infant class covered, babies differ in temperament from the start, and some react more strongly to the very same event.

There are small average differences between boys and girls too. Across more than a hundred studies of children from one to nine, girls were slightly better at waiting. The difference is small, and the two groups overlap a great deal.

A child who melts down fast at two is not giving you a forecast of who she will be. It usually means her alarm is quick, and that her brakes need more of your help for now.

## Sensitive to Sound and Touch

Some toddlers are bothered far more than others by loud sounds, scratchy clothes, or messy hands. In a study of about a thousand pairs of two-year-old twins, these children also tended to be more fearful and quicker to get upset. The sensitivity also ran partly in families.

For most children, it eases. In a follow-up of nearly a thousand twins, about three in four of those who were highly sensitive at two no longer were at seven.

For a smaller group it lasts. In another study, strong sensitivity in the preschool years went with more anxiety at six. If sensitivities are getting in the way of eating, dressing, or going out, mention it to your child's doctor.

## Back to Half Past Five

Go back to the kitchen floor at half past five. That boy was tired and hungry, the house was busy, and his alarm went off over a cup.

Nothing was wrong with him, and nothing was wrong with his parents. The phrase to retire is that he should know better by now, because knowing was never the problem.

What helps is working with both systems. Look after sleep, food, and a predictable rhythm before the hard parts of the day. When waiting is needed, turn eyes and hands toward something else. And once the alarm is ringing, your child needs to borrow your calm before anything else, which is the heart of Module 4.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

The alarm is early and the brakes are late. Feelings rise fast in a toddler because the system that slows them is still being built.

Worn-down toddlers cope less well. Enough sleep and a predictable day give them a better chance.

Looking away is the first waiting skill. You can offer a distraction, and you can show your child how it's done.

## Common myths

Myth: "He should know better by now."
What the research shows: Knowing runs ahead of doing (Lesson 1.1), and the ability to wait changes most between two and three (Cole et al., 2011).

Myth: "Boys can't wait and girls can."
What the research shows: A small average difference with a great deal of overlap (Silverman, 2021).
