## Overview

This is Lesson 2.2 of the From Little Onward class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 2 (Language, the Everyday Version). It covers: The small habits of everyday talk that help a toddler's language grow: responding, leaving a gap, saying it back with a little more, following their focus, and using routines, books, and songs. And what you can happily skip.

A toddler at the window spots the garbage truck and shouts truck. Her father comes over and says yes, a big green truck, and it's lifting the bin. She watches for a moment, and then she says big truck.

In this lesson, we'll look at the kinds of everyday talk that help language grow, like that small exchange at the window. We'll cover responding, adding a little, routines, books, and songs. We'll finish with what you can happily skip.

## Answer What They Start

The Infant class introduced serve and return, the back-and-forth in which a baby does something and you respond. With a toddler, the serves become words, points, and half-words, and your response still matters.

One study followed forty children from nine months to twenty-one months. Mothers who responded more to what their children were doing and saying had children who tended to reach language steps sooner. That included first words, the first fifty words, putting words together, and talking about the past.

Two features of a helpful response stand out. It comes quickly, and it is about the thing your child is already focused on.

## Leave a Gap

Conversation is turn-taking, and toddlers are slow to take their turn. A review of twenty-six studies found that young children take about a second to respond in a conversation.

A second is a long silence between adults, so it is tempting to jump in and answer for them.

So after you say something to your toddler, wait. Count slowly to two in your head before you fill the gap, because the answer may be on its way. The Infant class called this waiting, and it still helps now.

## Add a Little

When your toddler says a word or two, you say it back with a little more. Dog becomes yes, the dog is running. More juice becomes you want more juice. Researchers call this an expansion.

One study followed seventy-seven families from fifteen months to four and a half years. The more of a child's remarks the mother expanded between two and almost three, the faster the child's vocabulary grew. Those children also understood more words at four and a half.

A Spanish study found a similar pattern. How often adults repeated a two-year-old's words back, usually in a longer form, predicted the child's language at two and a half.

## Say It Back the Right Way

Toddlers make charming mistakes, like me do it, or two foots. You don't need to correct these, and parents often do something gentler. A child says I goed outside, and the parent answers yes, you went outside.

A close study of five children between two and four found that adults did this more often after a mistake than after a correct sentence. The children sometimes repeated the fixed part in their next turn, or said yes and carried on.

This kind of saying it back is called a recast, and it has also been tested as a therapy. A review of studies with children who have language difficulties found a benefit for their grammar, though the evidence is still limited.

## No Need to Make Them Say It

Notice what is missing from both of those moves, which is that nobody asks the child to repeat anything. Many parents feel they should finish with say dog, or can you say truck.

The Spanish study looked at something close to this. How often the adults said things back predicted later language, but how often the children repeated the adults of their own accord did not.

Some parents also shorten their own speech to match, saying things like doggie run or shoe on. A small study of children with language delay tested this with phrases for the children to copy. The clipped phrases gave no advantage, and some children used more of the little words after hearing whole sentences. So there is no need to clip your sentences.

## Follow, Don't Redirect

The Infant class showed that babies learn words better when you talk about what they are already looking at. With toddlers, the same rule covers telling them what to do.

In one small study, researchers sorted mothers' instructions to their thirteen-month-olds into two kinds. Some followed what the child was already doing, like push the car when the child was holding it. Others pulled the child toward something new. Only the first kind went along with a bigger vocabulary nine months later.

A second study, from ten to twenty-one months, found that intrusive instructions, the kind that push a child's attention elsewhere, went along with slower vocabulary growth. So your toddler's interest is the lesson plan.

## The Day Has Different Words

You may not need a special language time, because the ordinary day supplies variety of its own. One study filmed forty mothers at home with their one-year-olds and sorted their talk by what was going on.

The amount of talk, the range of words, and what the talk was about all changed with the activity. Feeding, grooming, playing with toys, and sharing a book each brought their own kind of language.

An older study of toddlers found that the setting shaped what mothers said as well. So a toddler who is with you through the day's routines is hearing many kinds of talk. Variety of words matters at this age, and that is a thread the Preschool class picks up.

## What Books Add

Books add something that conversation alone doesn't. One team compared the text of a hundred picture books with the same amount of everyday talk to young children, and the books contained more different words.

Books also seem to change how adults talk. In a study of sixty-nine fathers from low-income families and their two-year-olds, the fathers used a wider range of words and asked more questions while reading than while playing with toys. Those same features went with bigger vocabularies in their children.

And in trials where parents were coached to share books, children's spoken vocabulary improved by a modest amount. Most of those children had language difficulties or were at risk of them.

## Reading With a Toddler

Reading with a toddler rarely looks like reading. She turns three pages at once, goes backward, points at the same duck six times, and walks off halfway through.

All of that is fine. Talking about the pictures and about what interests her is a good way to share a book. Name what she points at, and add a little to what she says. Ask a question she can answer, like where is the duck, and then wait. You don't have to read every printed word.

When she wants the same book again, say yes, for the reasons we saw in the last lesson. The Preschool class teaches a fuller method for reading together, and what you do now is where it begins.

## Songs and Rhymes

Parents often ask whether songs and rhymes help language. The honest answer is that the research is thin but encouraging.

A few small studies have asked parents how much they sing with their babies and young toddlers, and then measured language. Children who were sung to more tended to understand more words and use more gestures, and in one study they said more words too.

Studies like these can't show that the singing caused the difference. Still, a song is slow, repeated, and shared face to face. So sing because you both enjoy it, and count any language benefit as a bonus.

## You Can Skip the Flashcards

Now for what you can skip, starting with flashcards and word drills. There is very little research on flashcards for toddlers, and nothing solid showing that they build vocabulary.

The nearest study compared two ways of teaching new words to two-year-olds. Children who were shown picture cards learned fewer words than children who used a word-learning app, especially an app shared with an adult. Across the study, the group of children who stayed focused for longest learned the most new words.

So interest is the thing to protect. A toddler is likely to learn more from a dog, a book, or a walk that has caught her attention than from a card.

## Three More Things to Skip

You can also skip three other things. The first is correcting, because saying it back the right way gives her the right form without stopping the conversation.

The second is videos sold as teaching words. The Infant class covered a study in which children who watched one for a month learned no more words than children who never saw it. In that study, the most learning happened when parents taught the words during everyday activities.

The third is talking nonstop. The Infant class said that you don't need to narrate all day, and that holds here. Quiet stretches of play are fine.

## It Shifts as They Grow

What helps most shifts a little across these two years. One study followed a hundred and forty-six mothers and children at fourteen months, at two years, and at three years.

The researchers separated two things. One was sensitivity, meaning how well a mother read and responded to her child. The other was stimulation, meaning how much she taught, explained, and added. At fourteen months, sensitivity mattered more for vocabulary, and the effect of stimulation grew across the toddler years.

So respond first, and add more as your child's language grows.

## These Habits Can Be Learned

None of this takes a special talent, and that has been tested. Researchers have run many trials in which parents were coached in habits like these.

One review combined eighteen studies of children from one and a half to five who had language difficulties. Another combined twenty-five trials with more than seventeen hundred children, most of whom had language difficulties or were at risk of them. Both found that when parents were coached, children's language improved.

The gains were modest, which is worth saying plainly. In the larger review, they showed up in what children said more than in what they understood. So these are habits that can be taught, and they make a difference you can measure.

## What It Looks Like in a Day

Here is how it might look across one day. At breakfast, your toddler says nana, and you say you want a banana, so here's your banana.

While she is getting dressed, you follow her eyes to the sock in her hand and tell her that's your sock, and it goes on your foot. On a walk, she points at a bus, and you name it and wait.

At bath time you sing the same song as yesterday, and before bed you open the same book and talk about the duck. That makes five small moments, and none of them took extra time. On the days when you are tired and quiet, don't worry, because it is the pattern over months that counts.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

Respond first, and then wait. Answer what your toddler starts, and leave a second or two for her turn.

Say it back with a little more. Expanding and gently fixing what she says goes with faster language growth, and you can skip the correcting and quizzing.

Use the day you already have. Meals, dressing, play, and books each bring their own words, so you can skip the flashcards.

## Common myths

Myth: "Flashcards teach toddlers words."
What the research shows: No toddler trial found. In the one comparison located, picture cards did less well than an app, and sustained interest predicted learning (Walter-Laager et al., 2017).

Myth: "Correct their mistakes so they learn the right way."
What the research shows: Adults mostly reformulate, and children take the reformulation up (Chouinard & Clark, 2003); recasts help grammar in children with language difficulties (Cleave et al., 2015).

## Research behind this lesson

Finding: 40 children: maternal responsiveness at 9 and 13 months predicted the timing of first imitations, first words, 50 words, combinatorial speech and talk about the past, tracked by interviews to 21 months, over and above the children's own behavior.
Sources: Tamis-LeMonda et al. (2001), Maternal responsiveness and children's achievement of language milestones, Child development, DOI 10.1111/1467-8624.00313.
Confidence: HIGH. The responsiveness was measured at 9 and 13 months, so the predictor sits at the infant and toddler boundary; the milestones are toddler milestones.

Finding: Features of responsiveness that support word learning: temporal contiguity (promptness), contingency (being about what the child is doing), and multimodal, didactic content.
Sources: Tamis-LeMonda et al. (2014), Why Is Infant Language Learning Facilitated by Parental Responsiveness?, Current Directions in Psychological Science, DOI 10.1177/0963721414522813.
Confidence: HIGH. A review.
