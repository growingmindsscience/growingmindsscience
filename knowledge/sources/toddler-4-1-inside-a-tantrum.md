## Overview

This is Lesson 4.1 of the Growing Minds class Toddler Years (1 to 3), Module 4 (Big Feelings, Transitions, and Repair). It covers: What a tantrum is, how common and how long tantrums are at this age, the shape they take from the first scream to the last sniff, and the signs that are worth a conversation with your child's doctor.

A twenty-month-old in a supermarket wants to hold the bananas. Her father says not right now, and within seconds she is on the floor by the cereal, screaming, with her legs kicking and a dozen strangers pretending not to look.

This module is about those minutes, and the ones around them. In this lesson, we'll look at what a tantrum is, how common and how long tantrums are at this age, and the shape they take from start to finish. Then we'll look at what is typical, and at the signs worth a conversation with your child's doctor.

## The Alarm at Full Volume

Module 1 gave us a fast alarm and slow brakes. A tantrum is what it looks like when the alarm is at full volume and the brakes have let go altogether.

Researchers describe a tantrum as a sudden, intense burst of negative feeling out of proportion to what set it off. The word matters less than the picture, in which a wish is blocked, the body floods, and for a few minutes the toddler is not choosing anything.

Hold that framing through this module. Nothing in a tantrum is a decision, and the brakes that would make it one are, as Lesson 1.2 showed, years from finished.

## How Common

Nearly every toddler has them. A review puts the figure at up to eighty-five percent of young children in countries like ours, and the Preschool class describes a study in which more than eight in ten of fifteen hundred preschoolers had tantrums sometimes.

In a Finnish survey of a hundred and thirty-two children, nearly nine in ten had tantrums, about two thirds began at two or three, and more than half had stopped by five.

A Belgian survey of more than seven hundred children found tantrums one to six times a week, lasting two to four minutes, and if that sounds like your house, your house is ordinary.

## When They Start and When They Fade

Tantrums become common at around eighteen months, which is no accident, because it is when walking, wanting, and the word no have all arrived, and the brakes have not.

In a Dutch study of eight hundred and sixty-one children from one to five, tantrums grew less frequent with age, but the ones that happened tended to last longer. Older children have fewer, longer storms.

By five or six, fewer than half of children are still having tantrums at all. The decline is slow and uneven, and it comes from the brakes growing, not from anything you say during the storm.

## How Long They Last

How long is a tantrum? The answer is shorter than it feels. In the largest study of its kind, parents of three hundred and thirty-five children from eighteen months to five wrote accounts of tantrums as they happened.

The most common length was between half a minute and one minute. Three quarters of all tantrums were over within five minutes.

Even when clinicians timed three hundred and thirty tantrums in children with serious behavior problems, the average was about three and a half minutes. Memory stretches them, so a tantrum that seems to go on forever has usually been a few minutes.

## Anger First, Then Sadness

Those detailed accounts also revealed a shape. Researchers coded what the children did in each half minute, and found two separate processes running at once, which they called anger and distress.

Anger rises fast, peaks at or near the start, and then falls away, and screaming, stamping, kicking, and throwing belong to it. Distress rises slowly through the tantrum, and whining, crying, and reaching for comfort belong to that.

Recordings of children's voices confirm the two groups, with screaming and yelling in one family of sounds and crying, whining, and fussing in another. A tantrum is anger with sadness underneath it, and the sadness outlasts the anger.

## Why the Shape Matters

That shape is the most useful thing in this lesson. The first minute is anger, and almost nothing reaches a child in it. The last minutes are sadness, and a child in them is usually looking for you.

In the study of three hundred and thirty-five children, tantrums in which the child dropped to the floor in the first half minute were shorter, and parents stepped in less. The storm often burns out faster when it is allowed to peak.

In a study of seventy-five Chinese toddlers whose mothers took a toy away, anger often began at low intensity and only sometimes escalated. Lesson 4.3 is about what to do with all of this, and for now, notice that the ending of a tantrum is a reach for comfort.

## Hitting, Biting, and Kicking

Many tantrums include hitting, biting, or kicking, and these are typical too. Among five hundred and eleven seventeen-month-olds in a population study, close to eighty percent had already hit, bitten, or kicked someone.

In a survey of nearly five hundred American children under two, ninety-four percent had done something physically aggressive in the past month, and a Norwegian study of more than a thousand children found that hitting peaks at about twenty to twenty-two months.

The Preschool class covers the decline from the third birthday on. For now, a one-year-old who hits is doing what one-year-olds do, and learning not to hit is the work of the next few years.

## Hurting Themselves

Some toddlers hit or bang their own heads in a tantrum, which frightens parents more than anything else. In the Dutch study, about two in five children fell into a profile with both aggression and self-hitting, so it is not rare.

Rhythmic head banging in a crib is a different thing, found in older surveys in four to fifteen percent of typically developing children. It starts before twelve months, happens when a child is drowsy or alone, and fades by three.

Either kind is distressing to watch, and neither usually causes injury beyond a bruise. We'll come back to where self-hurting fits among the signs worth checking.

## What Sets Them Off

What starts a tantrum is something parents agree on. In a survey of nearly three hundred parents of one- to three-year-olds, nine in ten named a refused request, and as many named having to start, stop, or change an activity.

In the clinic study that timed tantrums, half began with a child being asked to do something.

So the common triggers are a blocked wish and a transition. Lesson 4.2 takes up transitions in full, because they are the more preventable of the two.

## Words and Tantrums

Language matters more than most parents expect. Among two thousand and one mothers of children from twelve to thirty-eight months, toddlers with fewer spoken words had more frequent and more out-of-control tantrums.

Late talkers at two to two and a half were nearly twice as likely to have severe tantrums as children whose language was typical, and the link was already there in the second year.

Among a hundred and twenty children followed from eighteen months to four, those whose language grew fastest looked least angry by four, partly because they could ask for help and look elsewhere. Words are brakes in the making, which is one more reason Lesson 2.3 matters.

## Some Children More Than Others

Some toddlers have more tantrums than others, and Lesson 1.2 covered much of why. Children who react strongly from the start, who are worn down, or who are bothered by noise and texture reach their limit sooner.

In the Belgian survey, more intense toddlers had more frequent tantrums six months later, and a study that checked on three hundred and thirty-three toddlers every two months from their first birthday found each child's irritability fairly steady over the year.

So a toddler who melts down often at fourteen months will probably still do so at two, which does not mean something is wrong. It means you know your child, and can plan for her.

## What Typical Looks Like

Put the pieces together and a typical picture appears. Most toddlers have tantrums, several times a week, lasting a few minutes, starting around eighteen months and peaking in the second and third years, with hitting mixed in.

Researchers who study the line between typical and worrying describe the typical tantrum as one that happens in an expectable situation, such as being told no, being tired, or having to leave.

The tantrums that concerned them came out of nowhere, went on and on, or broke things. Context, length, and destructiveness mattered more than the simple count.

## The Signs Worth Checking

The Preschool class gives four signs for three- to five-year-olds, behavior that is frequent, intense, lasting, and everywhere. For toddlers, researchers have looked for something similar.

Among three hundred and fifty fourteen-month-olds, five things best picked out the toddlers whose irritability was getting in the way at home. They were frustrated by small things, hit, bit, or kicked in tantrums, could not cheer up when grumpy, were grumpy during fun, and had tantrums in public.

Frequent on its own is common, and in a Quebec birth cohort about one in eight seventeen-month-olds already showed frequent defiant behavior. When a version of that screen was tried at two-year checkups, a quarter of toddlers scored high, far more than will ever have a problem, so a high score is a reason to look again, not a diagnosis.

## What the Follow-Up Studies Show

Looking again is the key. Among more than four hundred preschoolers followed for several years, a quarter were highly irritable at the start, but only two thirds of those stayed that way, and fewer than one in a hundred who started low became high.

Quality predicted lasting trouble more than count. Among two hundred and seventy-nine preschoolers, children with behavior disorders were violent in tantrums more often, had them at school, and recovered more slowly, and depressed children were more likely to hurt themselves.

In the Dutch study, frequent tantrums went with later acting-out problems, long tantrums with later worry and withdrawal, and the aggressive, self-hurting profile predicted both. From preschool to school age, hitting, biting, or kicking in tantrums and trouble recovering from anger were the two behaviors that most predicted wider trouble.

## When to Ask

So here is the short list. Ask your child's doctor when tantrums happen many times a day for weeks, regularly last more than fifteen or twenty minutes, usually involve hurting himself or others, leave him unable to recover, or happen everywhere and with everyone.

Add language to the list, because a toddler with few words and severe tantrums deserves the hearing and language check from Lesson 2.3. In one comparison, autism did not make preschoolers' tantrums more common, but it did make them more likely to come without a clear trigger or over a failure to communicate.

And notice your own load. Parents of children with problematic tantrums carry a measurably heavier burden, and if the tantrums are wearing you down, that counts as a reason to ask.

## Three Things to Take With You

Here are three things to take with you.

Tantrums are typical and short. Most toddlers have several a week, most are over within five minutes, and they peak around two and fade by five.

A tantrum has a shape. Anger comes first and burns out fast, and sadness follows and reaches for you.

Quality matters more than count. Tantrums that come from nowhere, last very long, hurt someone, or leave a child unable to recover are the ones to raise with your doctor.

## Common myths

Myth: "The terrible twos" as a stage that begins at two and ends at three
What the research shows: Reframed. Tantrums become common around 18 months, peak in the second and third years, and fade through the fifth; in the Finnish survey two thirds began at two or three and more than half were over by five.

Myth: "Head banging means autism"
What the research shows: Not supported. Head banging in tantrums is common (two in five toddlers fell into the aggressive and self-hitting profile), and rhythmic crib head banging occurred in 4 to 15 percent of typically developing children in older surveys. Autism did not raise tantrum frequency in the one preschool comparison found.
