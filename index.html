Foundations: birth to one

At 2 years, 3 months

What is growing now
Researchers estimate the infant brain forms about a million new neural connections every second. Attachment, sensory maps, and the rhythm of back-and-forth interaction are the main work of this year.
What helps
Serve and return: when your baby babbles or looks at something, respond. That ordinary loop, not flashcards, is what builds the architecture.

First words, first steps: one to two

At 2 years, 3 months

What is growing now
Walking opens the world, and language understanding races far ahead of speech. Joint attention, the act of looking at something together, is how words get attached to things.
What helps
Narrate the ordinary. Name what your child is already looking at. Conversation, not instruction, is how vocabulary grows at this age.

Autonomy and big feelings: two to three

At 2 years, 3 months

What is growing now
“No” is a developmental milestone, not defiance. Toddlers are practising self-determination while the prefrontal cortex is still profoundly immature, so big feelings outrun the brakes.
What helps
Real choices where you can, calm limits where you must. Your steadiness during a meltdown is the scaffold their regulation is built on.

The imagination years: three to four

At 2 years, 3 months

What is growing now
Pretend play takes off, and it is serious cognitive work: holding two realities at once, taking perspectives, rehearsing social roles. Early self-regulation begins here.
What helps
Protect unstructured play. Join the pretend world on your child’s terms sometimes, because following their lead is what makes it developmentally rich.

Taught in Class 3, Preschool years →

Self-control, slowly: four to five

At 2 years, 3 months

What is growing now
Executive function (working memory, flexible thinking, impulse control) is under construction, and it develops on a wide, normal range. Readiness is a window, not a race.
What helps
Games with rules, real responsibilities, and routines they can predict. Practice, not pressure, is how self-control grows.

Why the science matters

In my work with families, the parents who do best are, almost without exception, the ones who understand the science.

Matthew McArthur, founder

Parents who know how development works stay calmer, read their children more accurately, and change the things that actually matter. Not because they memorised scripts, but because they can see what is happening in front of them.

Every class teaches you to read three things.

Your child
What is being built at this age, what is typical and what is not, and why the hardest behaviours are so often the development itself, showing up on schedule.
Yourself
How your own nervous system, history, and stress enter the room, and what regulation actually looks like in an adult, because a child’s calm is borrowed before it is owned.
Your influence
Which parts of the environment shape development (conversation, routines, serve and return) and which worries the research lets you set down for good.
