When it’s time to leave the playground

You can stay warm and still leave. Tears don’t mean it went wrong.

Skip to classes by age

  1. Time to leave

    It’s time to go. They’re not ready.

  2. Acknowledge the feeling

    “You really wanted to stay.”

  3. Hold the boundary

    “It’s time to leave. I’ll help you.”

  4. Move together

    The feeling can stay. You can move forward together.

There’s a whole lesson on moments like this.

In the Toddler years class, it’s one of four short lessons on big feelings and transitions: the shape a tantrum takes, heading one off before it’s time to leave, staying calm in the middle of it, and repair afterward.

Why this exists.

When parents understood what was happening developmentally, hard moments made more sense.

That pattern held across more than 100 families in early intervention, and it left parents with more ideas for what to try next.

Children build the adults they become one ordinary day at a time, and understanding that work makes it lighter to carry.

The person behind the classes.

Matthew McArthur holds an M.A. in Developmental Psychology from San Diego State University, where he studied how the home shapes early development, after working with developmental researchers at UCLA. With Dr. Margaret Friend, he wrote “Why Early Chatter Matters” for Frontiers for Young Minds, a journal reviewed by young readers. He then spent two years in state-funded early intervention, coaching more than 100 families of children under three.
