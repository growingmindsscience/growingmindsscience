The Developmental Lens

Leaving the playground

18 months to 4 years · Regulation, autonomy, transitions

One ordinary moment, read in four steps.

What are we actually seeing?

Two-minute warning given. “Time to go.” A run back to the slide. A body on the ground. Loud, fast crying. Ten minutes ago, everything was fine. Nothing about the playground changed. The plan did.

From the outside nobody can see “defiance” or “tired”. We can see the sequence, and the sequence is the clue.

What might be developing beneath the surface?

  • Switching rules is hard at this age. Most three-year-olds keep sorting by the first rule after being told the new one. Leaving asks for a similar switch, in a moment that matters far more to a child. Well established in laboratory tasks. Zelazo, 2006.
  • The brakes are the last part to arrive. Holding back or stopping an action relies partly on brain systems that grow fastest in the preschool years and keep developing long after. Well established. Diamond, 2013.
  • Autonomy is the work of this age. “No” is part of becoming a person with a will of one’s own, and it often shows up right when an adult’s plan takes over. Supported. Brownell and colleagues, 2007; Erikson, 1950.
  • Once overwhelmed, reasoning does not land. When a child is overwhelmed, words and reasons tend not to land until they have calmed down. Young children borrow a calm adult to get there. Supported; the two-zone picture is a conceptual model.

What can this understanding change?

None of this makes leaving easy. It changes what you are responding to.

  1. Say what is next before the end. A plan said out loud gives your child something to hold on to, even if it does not stop the protest.
  2. One small choice inside a firm limit. “Slide or swing for the last one?”
  3. If it tips over, regulate first. Fewer words, a lower voice, a steady body. The explanation can wait for the car.
  4. Expect it to be hard sometimes anyway. Tuesday smooth, Wednesday on the floor: that is ordinary at this age, not proof the method failed.

What might you notice next time?

  • What came just before the hard moment?
  • Which part of the transition was hardest, and was it the same part as last time?
  • What helped you stay steady?

Observations, not scores. Curiosity about one child, not a verdict.
