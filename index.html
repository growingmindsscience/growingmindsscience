Leaving the playground

The Developmental Lens takes an ordinary moment and shows what may be developing underneath it: observe, understand, respond, reflect. Here it is on the moment every parent of a toddler knows. Scientific copy is a prototype awaiting source review.

What are we actually seeing?

It is time to leave the playground. You give the two-minute warning, then say “time to go”. Your two-and-a-half-year-old runs back to the slide. When you reach for a hand, they drop to the ground and cry. Ten minutes ago, everything was fine. Nothing about the playground changed. The plan did.

What is observable

  • A happy child, absorbed in play.
  • An announcement from an adult.
  • A run in the opposite direction.
  • A body that goes to the floor.
  • Crying that is loud and fast.

What is not observable

Intent. From the outside, nobody can see whether this is “defiance”, “manipulation” or “being tired”. We can see the sequence, and the sequence is the clue.

Most common from about 18 months to 4 years, with wide variation between children and between days.

What might be developing beneath the surface?

Four processes can be read in this one moment. None is a flaw. Each is a skill still being built.

  • In a classic card-sorting task, children under about four keep sorting by the first rule even after being told clearly to switch. Leaving the playground asks for exactly that switch, from “play” to “go”, while the first rule is still running.

    Well established in laboratory tasks. Zelazo and colleagues, 2003.

  • Inhibitory control, the ability to stop an action already under way, depends on prefrontal systems that develop slowly across childhood. The body and the feelings come first.

    Well established. Diamond, 2013.

  • Between roughly 18 months and 3 years, a child is discovering that they are a separate person with preferences of their own. “No” is practice at being a person, and it tends to arrive at the exact moment a plan is imposed.

    Supported; the size of specific effects varies. Erikson, 1950; Kochanska and Aksan, 1995.

  • A nervous system past its limit cannot use language or logic until it has come back down. Young children borrow a calm adult to get there.

    Supported. The two-zone picture of the brain is a conceptual model, not a scan. Diamond, 2013; Growing Minds toddler class, Module 4.

  • Being late, being watched by other parents, being tired: your own state shapes the return your child gets, and the research on co-regulation says that return matters more than the script.

    Supported. See “When your own nervous system is the variable”.

What can this understanding change?

None of this makes leaving easy. It changes what you are responding to.

  1. Say what is next before the end. “Two more slides, then we go home for lunch.” A plan that is announced is one the child can start holding; the switch begins earlier and in smaller steps.

    Predictable routines are well supported. Whether a warning helps on a given day is not something the research can promise.

  2. Give the last move a shape. One real choice inside a firm limit: “Slide or swing for the last one?” Choices inside limits support autonomy without handing over the decision to leave.

    Supported. Kochanska and Aksan, 1995.

  3. Carry the plan for them. Narrate the next step while you walk. The working memory that holds “home, lunch, nap” is yours for now.

  4. If it tips into a meltdown, regulate first and teach later. Fewer words, a lower voice, a steady body, staying close. The explanation can wait for the car.

    Supported. Toddler class, Module 4; “Self-regulation, briefly”.

  5. Expect it to be hard sometimes anyway. A smooth exit on Tuesday and a floor exit on Wednesday is development, not a failed method.

What might you notice next time?

  • What came before the hard moment: the warning, the walk to the gate, or the car seat?
  • Which part of the transition was hardest, and was it the same part as last time?
  • Did advance notice change anything, for your child or for you?
  • What helped you stay steady, and what made it harder?

These are observations, not scores. The point is curiosity about one child, not a verdict.

Growing now

“No” is a developmental milestone, not defiance. Toddlers are practising self-determination while the prefrontal cortex is still profoundly immature, so big feelings outrun the brakes.

What helps

Real choices where you can, calm limits where you must. Your steadiness during a meltdown is the scaffold their regulation is built on.

Growing now

Pretend play takes off, and it is serious cognitive work: holding two realities at once, taking perspectives, rehearsing social roles. Early self-regulation begins here.

What helps

Protect unstructured play. Join the pretend world on your child’s terms sometimes, because following their lead is what makes it developmentally rich.

Growing now

Executive function (working memory, flexible thinking, impulse control) is under construction, and it develops on a wide, normal range. Readiness is a window, not a race.

What helps

Games with rules, real responsibilities, and routines they can predict. Practice, not pressure, is how self-control grows.

Three things worth understanding

Your child

What is being built at this age, what behavior reveals, and what it does not.

30%

of the time is how often even well-attached parents and babies are in sync, in Tronick’s second-by-second films. What builds the brain is coming back.

Yourself

How your own state, history and expectations enter the room, because a child’s calm is borrowed before it is owned.

  • You read the moment before you react.
  • You know which worries to set down.
  • You know what to ask, and when.
  • You change what actually matters.

Your influence

Which parts of the environment shape development, which are normal variation, and which are outside anyone’s control.

Three kinds of influence, sorted

  • What you can changeConversation turns, routines, your own regulation, what is on the screen
  • Normal variationWhen the first word comes, temperament, how fast walking arrives
  • Outside anyone’s controlIllness, a hard year, a move, the weather of a given Tuesday

A conceptual sorting, not a complete list. The classes teach where each line falls.

Why trust Growing Minds

  • Taught from San Diego, California
  • Sources cited on every page
  • Educational, not clinical

Matthew McArthur studied how the home shapes early development: parent stress, screen time, changes at home, and the back-and-forth of everyday interaction, in children from six months to five years, at San Diego State University, after working with developmental researchers at UCLA. He wrote about early language for children in Frontiers for Young Minds, then spent years coaching families of children under three in state-funded early intervention.

Growing Minds Science is what that work looks like explained at a kitchen table: the mechanism, the evidence, and the limits of what anyone can know about one child. Every page cites its sources. None of it is medical or psychological advice.
