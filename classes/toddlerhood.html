From Module 4, Lesson 1, “Inside a Tantrum.” Fig. 1 above is the idea this lesson builds on.

Module 1 gave us a fast alarm and slow brakes. A tantrum is what it looks like when the alarm is at full volume and the brakes have let go altogether. Researchers describe a tantrum as a sudden, intense burst of negative feeling out of proportion to what set it off. The word matters less than the picture, in which a wish is blocked, the body floods, and for a few minutes the toddler is not choosing anything.

How long is a tantrum? The answer is shorter than it feels. In the largest study of its kind, parents of three hundred and thirty-five children from eighteen months to five wrote accounts of tantrums as they happened. The most common length was between half a minute and one minute. Three quarters of all tantrums were over within five minutes.

Those detailed accounts also revealed a shape. Researchers coded what the children did in each half minute, and found two separate processes running at once, which they called anger and distress. Anger rises fast, peaks at or near the start, and then falls away. Distress rises slowly through the tantrum, and whining, crying, and reaching for comfort belong to that. A tantrum is anger with sadness underneath it, and the sadness outlasts the anger.

From the lesson transcript, lightly trimmed. The lesson goes on to what is typical at this age and which signs are worth a conversation with your child’s doctor; the next three lessons cover heading tantrums off, staying calm in the moment, and repair afterward.

  • A working picture of the toddler brain: a fast alarm, slow brakes, and a memory that is learning by watching you.
  • What the word spurt looks like, how wide normal is, the talk that grows language, and when a late talker is worth a check.
  • Why “mine,” “no,” and “I do it” are the new self arriving, and how choices and warm limits turn them into help.

What this class draws on.

Each lesson names its studies as it goes, with the sample and the finding in plain words. A few of them:

  1. A scan study following children from birth to age two, and the brain’s slower, steady growth after the first year. Module 1, lesson 1
  2. The classic deferred-imitation experiments: fourteen-month-olds copying what they watched an adult do, long after seeing it. Module 1, lesson 3
  3. Serve and return, carried from the Infant class: a study following forty children from nine months, and what parents’ responses to early words predicted. Module 2, lesson 2
  4. The parent-report vocabulary checklist used to identify late talkers, and the research on which late talkers catch up. Module 2, lesson 3
  5. The mirror test of self-recognition, and Deci and Ryan’s work on autonomy support versus control. Module 3, lessons 1 and 2
  6. The largest tantrum study of its kind: parents of 335 children, eighteen months to five, recording tantrums as they happened, and the anger-and-distress shape it revealed. Module 4, lesson 1
  7. Co-regulation research on how a calm adult brings a toddler back, and the still-face and repair studies behind Module 4’s close. Module 4, lessons 3 and 4

Who teaches it.

Created and taught by Matthew McArthur, a Child Development Specialist. At San Diego State University he studied how parent stress, changes at home, screen time, and back-and-forth interaction shape language and thinking from 6 months to 5 years, after working with developmental researchers at UCLA, and he wrote “Why Early Chatter Matters” for Frontiers for Young Minds.

He then spent two years in state-funded early intervention, coaching families with children under 3 who were experiencing delays.
