“Pacifier use will delay my child's speech.”

Claims · Evidence-graded claim · Language · Reviewed 2026-10-15

Not enough evidence · Majority view

There is not enough evidence that ordinary pacifier use delays speech; the clearer tradeoffs are ear infections and, with long use, teeth.

For ordinary pacifier use in the first couple of years, the evidence for speech harm is weak. The studies are few, small, and mixed, and a 2021 systematic review called the evidence inconsistent and inconclusive. No solid body of research shows that typical pacifier use delays speech or language development. The honest grade is simply that we do not have enough evidence, and the loud warnings outrun the data. The most consistent signal, still weak, is that longer-lasting sucking habits, from a pacifier or a thumb, have been linked to speech-sound problems in three of the four studies the review found. One study of 100 children found that those who used a pacifier more often during the day made more unusual speech-sound errors, a link seen only in the younger children (under about three), and the authors note these errors may resolve over time. What the literature does document is different and worth knowing. Pacifier use is associated with more ear infections, and guidance suggests cutting back in the second half of the first year for that reason. Use that continues past about age two is associated with dental changes, which become more marked after about age four. There is also a protective association with SIDS when a pacifier is offered at sleep, which is why safe-sleep guidance recommends offering one. If you are breastfeeding, current safe-sleep guidance says to wait until breastfeeding is firmly established, meaning a good milk supply, a comfortable latch, and healthy weight gain, before offering a pacifier; how long that takes varies from baby to baby. The pragmatic read fits this evidence: keeping the pacifier for sleep and soothing, rather than all day, may be a sensible middle path, and weaning by about age two or three may help limit the dental effects, which grow the longer use continues.

Sources

  • Burr S, Harding S, Wren Y, Deave T. The relationship between feeding and non-nutritive sucking behaviours and speech sound development: a systematic review. Folia Phoniatrica et Logopaedica. 2021;73(2):75-88. (Systematic review: evidence inconsistent and inconclusive; long sucking habits the most consistent signal)
  • Strutt C, Khattab G, Willoughby J. Does the duration and frequency of dummy (pacifier) use affect the development of speech? International Journal of Language & Communication Disorders. 2021;56(3):512-527. (Most speech outcomes unrelated; frequent daytime use linked to atypical errors in younger children)
  • Sexton S, Natale R. Risks and benefits of pacifiers. American Family Physician. 2009;79(8):681-685. (Clinical review: ear infections, SIDS, and dental effects)
  • Nelson AM. A comprehensive review of evidence and current recommendations related to pacifier usage. Journal of Pediatric Nursing. 2012;27(6):690-699. (Review of benefits and risks: soothing, SIDS, ear infections, breastfeeding, teeth)
  • Moon RY, Carlin RF, Hand I; Task Force on Sudden Infant Death Syndrome and the Committee on Fetus and Newborn. Sleep-related infant deaths: updated 2022 recommendations for reducing infant deaths in the sleep environment. Pediatrics. 2022;150(1):e2022057990. (Current AAP safe-sleep policy: offer a pacifier at sleep)

How claims get graded

Every claim is graded on two independent axes. Evidence strength asks: how good is the research behind the claim as parents actually state it? Randomized trials and meta-analyses outrank single observational studies, which outrank expert opinion. Consensus asks a different question: where does the field actually sit? A claim can rest on limited evidence while researchers still broadly agree, and both facts belong on the label.

Every empirical statement in a summary is tied to the sources listed on its page, and an automated check enforces the honesty rules: claims below strong evidence cannot use proof-language, and claims with limited or insufficient evidence must say so in plain words. The check runs on every update to this site; a summary that overclaims cannot ship.

The grades are educational summaries of published research, not medical advice, and they can change; each page shows when it was last reviewed.

This page summarizes published research for educational purposes. It is not medical advice, and it is not a recommendation for or against any practice for your specific child; those calls belong with you and your child's clinician.
