“Tummy time actually matters for my baby's development.”

Claims · Evidence-graded claim · Milestones · Reviewed 2026-10-15

Moderate evidence · Scientific consensus

Yes: awake tummy time is linked to better motor development; the head-shape evidence is mixed.

Tummy time is standard baby advice with a systematic review behind it. Across 16 studies of over 4,000 infants, more awake tummy time was linked to better gross motor development, including rolling and crawling, and to less flattening across the back of the head. The findings for flattening on one side were mixed. The honest grading note: most of the underlying studies are observational, so the strength here is moderate rather than strong; babies who get more floor time may differ from babies who don't in other ways too. But the direction is consistent for skills like rolling and crawling, and the practice costs nothing. What the evidence does not support is tummy-time guilt. The World Health Organization suggests at least 30 minutes a day, spread across the day, for babies who are not yet moving on their own. The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests starting with 3 to 5 minutes, two or three times a day, and working up to 15 to 30 minutes a day by about 7 weeks. Short, frequent sessions are how most babies get there. If your baby fusses, try a change: lie back and set them on your chest so they can see your face, or put yourself or a toy within reach.

Sources

  • Hewitt L, Kerr E, Stanley RM, Okely AD. Tummy time and infant health outcomes: a systematic review. Pediatrics. 2020;145(6):e20192168. (Systematic review, 16 studies: linked to better gross motor development and less flattening across the back of the head; findings for one-sided flattening unclear)
  • World Health Organization. Guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5 years of age. Geneva: World Health Organization; 2019. (At least 30 minutes of awake prone time a day for babies not yet mobile)
  • Moon RY, Carlin RF, Hand I; Task Force on Sudden Infant Death Syndrome and the Committee on Fetus and Newborn. Sleep-related infant deaths: updated 2022 recommendations for reducing infant deaths in the sleep environment. Pediatrics. 2022;150(1):e2022057990. (Current AAP safe-sleep policy: supervised, awake tummy time)
  • American Academy of Pediatrics. Back to Sleep, Tummy to Play. HealthyChildren.org. Last updated September 8, 2026. (Parent guidance: 3 to 5 minutes, two or three times a day, working up to 15 to 30 minutes by 7 weeks)

How claims get graded

Every claim is graded on two independent axes. Evidence strength asks: how good is the research behind the claim as parents actually state it? Randomized trials and meta-analyses outrank single observational studies, which outrank expert opinion. Consensus asks a different question: where does the field actually sit? A claim can rest on limited evidence while researchers still broadly agree, and both facts belong on the label.

Every empirical statement in a summary is tied to the sources listed on its page, and an automated check enforces the honesty rules: claims below strong evidence cannot use proof-language, and claims with limited or insufficient evidence must say so in plain words. The check runs on every update to this site; a summary that overclaims cannot ship.

The grades are educational summaries of published research, not medical advice, and they can change; each page shows when it was last reviewed.

This page summarizes published research for educational purposes. It is not medical advice, and it is not a recommendation for or against any practice for your specific child; those calls belong with you and your child's clinician.
