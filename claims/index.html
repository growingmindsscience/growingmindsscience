Parenting claims, graded

Clear about what’s known. Honest about what’s not. The internet states every parenting claim with the same confidence. The research does not. Each claim here is stated the way a parent would say it, then graded on two axes: how strong the evidence actually is, and how united the field actually is.

How claims get graded

Strong evidence

2 claims

“Talking to my baby, even before they can respond, builds their language.”

Scientific consensus · Language

Yes. Talking with your baby builds language, and back-and-forth exchanges look especially important.

“Reading to my toddler actually changes their language development.”

Scientific consensus · Language

Yes. Reading together builds language, and reading interactively, with questions and pauses, can add to the benefit.

Moderate evidence

2 claims

“Tummy time actually matters for my baby’s development.”

Scientific consensus · Milestones

Yes: awake tummy time is linked to better motor development; the head-shape evidence is mixed.

“Screen time is harming my toddler’s language development.”

Majority view · Screens

Heavy, passive, background screen use is linked to less language input; the dose and the type matter more than the screen.

Limited evidence

1 claim

“Baby-led weaning is better than spoon-feeding purées.”

Majority view · Feeding

A reasonable option, not a clearly better one; the few trials so far show mixed results on growth.

Not enough evidence

1 claim

“Pacifier use will delay my child’s speech.”

Majority view · Language

There is not enough evidence that ordinary pacifier use delays speech; the clearer tradeoffs are ear infections and, with long use, teeth.

Contradicted by evidence

3 claims

“Hearing two languages will confuse my baby and delay their speech.”

Scientific consensus · Language

Bilingual children babble on time, and their vocabulary is on track when you count both languages.

“A baby walker will help my baby learn to walk sooner.”

Scientific consensus · Products & gear

Walkers don’t help babies walk sooner, and the injury record led the AAP to call for a ban.

“Teething causes fevers.”

Majority view · Milestones

Teething can nudge temperature up slightly; a real fever should not be blamed on teeth.

How claims get graded

Two questions, asked of every claim, and the same honesty rules for all of them.

Every claim is graded on two independent axes. Evidence strength asks: how good is the research behind the claim as parents actually state it? Randomized trials and meta-analyses outrank single observational studies, which outrank expert opinion. Consensus asks a different question: where does the field actually sit? A claim can rest on limited evidence while researchers still broadly agree, and both facts belong on the label.

Every empirical statement in a summary is tied to the sources listed on its page, and an automated check enforces the honesty rules: claims below strong evidence cannot use proof-language, and claims with limited or insufficient evidence must say so in plain words. The check runs on every update to this site; a summary that overclaims cannot ship.

The grades are educational summaries of published research, not medical advice, and they can change; each page shows when it was last reviewed.
