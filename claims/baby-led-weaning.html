“Baby-led weaning is better than spoon-feeding purées.”

Claims · Evidence-graded claim · Feeding · Reviewed 2026-10-15

Limited evidence · Majority view

A reasonable option, not a clearly better one; the few trials so far show mixed results on growth.

Baby-led weaning arrived with big claims: better self-regulation, less picky eating, lower obesity risk. When a modified version of baby-led weaning was tested in a randomized trial, the BLISS study, babies' body mass index and overweight rates did not differ clearly from spoon-fed babies at one or two years. A second trial, in Turkey, found baby-led babies were a little lighter at one year, about 0.7 kg on average, so the growth picture is mixed. Choking was no more common in the baby-led group when families were taught how to prepare safer foods, but about a third of all babies in the trial choked at least once between 6 and 8 months, so safe preparation matters whichever way you feed. Iron levels were similar when parents were advised to offer an iron-rich food at every meal. Evidence on pickiness and appetite is mixed. In the trial, parents reported less fussiness at one year, but most of this evidence relies on parent reports, and families who choose baby-led weaning often differ in other ways. So the honest grade is limited: not contradicted, not established, and a 2026 review of the trials reached the same cautious reading. The practical translation is unusually low-stakes. Both roads lead to a toddler who eats table food. Pick the one that fits your household, mix them freely, and spend the reclaimed worry elsewhere.

Sources

  • Taylor RW, Williams SM, Fangupo LJ, et al. Effect of a baby-led approach to complementary feeding on infant growth and overweight: a randomized clinical trial. JAMA Pediatrics. 2017;171(9):838-846. (The BLISS trial (a modified baby-led approach): no clear difference in growth at 12 and 24 months)
  • Fangupo LJ, Heath AM, Williams SM, et al. A baby-led approach to eating solids and risk of choking. Pediatrics. 2016;138(4):e20160772. (BLISS choking outcome: no group difference; 35% of all infants choked at least once between 6 and 8 months)
  • Daniels L, Taylor RW, Williams SM, et al. Impact of a modified version of baby-led weaning on iron intake and status: a randomised controlled trial. BMJ Open. 2018;8(6):e019036. (Iron intake and status similar with advice to offer iron-rich food at each meal)
  • Dogan E, Yilmaz G, Caylan N, et al. Baby-led complementary feeding: randomized controlled study. Pediatrics International. 2018;60(12):1073-1080. (Second trial (280 infants): baby-led infants about 0.7 kg lighter at 12 months)
  • Márquez-Díaz RR, Ruíz-Fernández MD, Arcas-Rueda M, et al. Baby-led weaning vs traditional spoon-feeding: a systematic review of infant growth, choking risk, and iron status among infants aged 6-12 months. Nutrition Reviews. 2026. doi:10.1093/nutrit/nuaf288. (Systematic review of the trials: choking and iron no worse; growth evidence limited and conflicting)
  • Brown A, Jones SW, Rowan H. Baby-led weaning: the evidence to date. Current Nutrition Reports. 2017;6(2):148-156. (Review of the early, mostly observational evidence: possible benefits, but weak conclusions)

How claims get graded

Every claim is graded on two independent axes. Evidence strength asks: how good is the research behind the claim as parents actually state it? Randomized trials and meta-analyses outrank single observational studies, which outrank expert opinion. Consensus asks a different question: where does the field actually sit? A claim can rest on limited evidence while researchers still broadly agree, and both facts belong on the label.

Every empirical statement in a summary is tied to the sources listed on its page, and an automated check enforces the honesty rules: claims below strong evidence cannot use proof-language, and claims with limited or insufficient evidence must say so in plain words. The check runs on every update to this site; a summary that overclaims cannot ship.

The grades are educational summaries of published research, not medical advice, and they can change; each page shows when it was last reviewed.
