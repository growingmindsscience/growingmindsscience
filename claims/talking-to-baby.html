“Talking to my baby, even before they can respond, builds their language.”

Claims · Evidence-graded claim · Language · Reviewed October 15, 2026

Yes. Talking with your baby builds language, and back-and-forth exchanges look especially important.

Evidence strength Strong evidence

Contradicted by evidenceStrong evidence

Consensus Scientific consensus

Fringe positionScientific consensus

7 sources

This is one of the best-supported ideas in early language research, with one modern refinement.

Decades of research link the talk children hear to their language skills, and a 2021 meta-analysis found that both the amount and the quality of parents’ talk are linked to children’s language, with quality, meaning varied words and fuller sentences, the stronger link.

The famous ‘30 million word gap’ made the finding a headline, but that number comes from one small study of families recorded in the 1980s and has been fairly criticized; later work finds the gap is smaller and depends on what is counted.

Newer studies that record a whole day at home suggest that back-and-forth exchanges, where you respond to your baby and give them a turn, may matter even more than the total number of words. In one long-term study, more of these exchanges at 18 to 24 months went with stronger language and IQ scores about ten years later, even after accounting for the mother’s education level.

A smaller brain-imaging study of 4- to 6-year-olds found a similar pattern in language areas of the brain. And in a randomized trial, coaching parents of 6-month-olds to use parentese, the slower, higher-pitched, sing-song style, and to take turns led to more back-and-forth exchanges and stronger language skills by 18 months. These studies rely on automated counts that are imperfect, so the exact size of the effect is still being worked out.

The practical translation is freeing: you do not need to monologue all day or perform enrichment. Talking to your baby helps, and responding to your baby seems to help even more: treat their sounds, gestures, and eventually words as turns, answer them, and wait for the next one. Every tool on this site that touches language is built on this idea.

How claims get graded

Every claim is graded on two independent axes. Evidence strength asks: how good is the research behind the claim as parents actually state it? Randomized trials and meta-analyses outrank single observational studies, which outrank expert opinion. Consensus asks a different question: where does the field actually sit? A claim can rest on limited evidence while researchers still broadly agree, and both facts belong on the label.

Every empirical statement in a summary is tied to the sources listed on its page, and an automated check enforces the honesty rules: claims below strong evidence cannot use proof-language, and claims with limited or insufficient evidence must say so in plain words. The check runs on every update to this site; a summary that overclaims cannot ship.

The grades are educational summaries of published research, not medical advice, and they can change; each page shows when it was last reviewed.

This page summarizes published research for educational purposes. It is not medical advice, and it is not a recommendation for or against any practice for your specific child; those calls belong with you and your child’s clinician.
