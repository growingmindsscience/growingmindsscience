Evidence-graded claim · Language

“Talking to my baby, even before they can respond, builds their language.”

Strong evidence Scientific consensus Reviewed 2026-07-20

Yes, and the back-and-forth matters even more than the word count.

This is the best-supported claim in early language science, with one important modern refinement. Decades of research link the amount of speech directed at a child to vocabulary growth, and the famous '30 million word gap' figure made that finding a headline; the specific number comes from a small 1990s sample and has been fairly criticized. But the refined finding is stronger, not weaker: longitudinal work following children's real language environments found that conversational turns, the back-and-forth exchanges where an adult responds to a child and waits, predict language outcomes and even language-related brain activity better than raw word counts do, and independent of family income. The practical translation is genuinely freeing. You do not need to monologue all day or perform enrichment. You need to respond: treat your baby's sounds, gestures, and eventually words as conversational serves, return them, and wait for the next one. Narration helps; responsiveness does the heavy lifting. Every tool on this site that touches language is built on that mechanism.

Sources

  • Gilkerson J, Richards JA, Warren SF, et al. Language experience in the second year of life and language outcomes in late childhood. Pediatrics. 2018;142(4):e20174276. (Longitudinal: early conversational turns predict outcomes a decade later)
  • Romeo RR, Leonard JA, Robinson ST, et al. Beyond the 30-million-word gap: children's conversational exposure is associated with language-related brain function. Psychological Science. 2018;29(5):700-710. (Conversational turns and Broca's-area activation, independent of SES)

How claims get graded

Every claim is graded on two independent axes. Evidence strength asks: how good is the research behind the claim as parents actually state it? Randomized trials and meta-analyses outrank single observational studies, which outrank expert opinion. Consensus asks a different question: where does the field actually sit? A claim can rest on limited evidence while researchers still broadly agree, and both facts belong on the label.

Every empirical statement in a summary is tied to the sources listed on its page, and an automated check enforces the honesty rules: claims below strong evidence cannot use proof-language, and claims with limited or insufficient evidence must say so in plain words. The check runs on every update to this site; a summary that overclaims cannot ship.

The grades are educational summaries of published research, not medical advice, and they can change; each page shows when it was last reviewed.

This page summarizes published research for educational purposes. It is not medical advice, and it is not a recommendation for or against any practice for your specific child; those calls belong with you and your child's clinician.
