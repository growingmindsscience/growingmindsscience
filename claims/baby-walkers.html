“A baby walker will help my baby learn to walk sooner.”

Claims · Evidence-graded claim · Products & gear · Reviewed 2026-10-15

Contradicted by evidence · Scientific consensus

Walkers don't help babies walk sooner, and the injury record led the AAP to call for a ban.

This one earns the library's rarest combination: the product does not deliver its named benefit, and it carries a documented injury cost. Studies comparing babies who used walkers with babies who did not found that walker babies did not walk sooner. One study found they sat, crawled, and walked later and scored lower on development tests. Those studies were not randomized, and a review found research in this area generally low in quality, with motor delays that were mostly temporary. So the delay is less certain than the lack of benefit. One study's authors suggested a reason: a walker blocks a baby's view of their own legs as they move. Time in a walker may also take the place of floor play. Meanwhile, walkers give pre-walking babies speed and reach they cannot control. An estimated 230,000 US children under 15 months were treated in emergency rooms for walker injuries between 1990 and 2014, about three in four from falls down stairs. These numbers back the American Academy of Pediatrics' call for a ban on making and selling walkers. Injuries fell after a 2010 US safety standard, but walkers are still sold there. Canada banned them in 2004. A stationary activity center gives the upright time without the wheels. Floor time is how babies build these skills. If a walker is already in your house, keep it far from any stairs and make sure there is plenty of floor time too.

Sources

  • Siegel AC, Burton RV. Effects of baby walkers on motor and mental development in human infants. Journal of Developmental & Behavioral Pediatrics. 1999;20(5):355-361. (Observational comparison: walker infants sat, crawled, and walked later)
  • American Academy of Pediatrics, Committee on Injury and Poison Prevention. Injuries associated with infant walkers. Pediatrics. 2001;108(3):790-792. (Injury data and the AAP's call for a ban)
  • Sims A, Chounthirath T, Yang J, Hodges NL, Smith GA. Infant walker-related injuries in the United States. Pediatrics. 2018;142(4):e20174332. (US injury data 1990 to 2014 and the decline after the 2010 safety standard)
  • Pin T, Eldridge B, Galea MP. A review of the effects of sleep position, play position, and equipment use on motor development in infants. Developmental Medicine & Child Neurology. 2007;49(11):858-867. (Systematic review of 19 studies on positioning and equipment: generally poor quality; motor delays mostly temporary)
  • Canada Consumer Product Safety Act, S.C. 2010, c. 21, Schedule 2, item 3 (baby walkers). (The current Canadian ban (first imposed in 2004 under the Hazardous Products Act))

How claims get graded

Every claim is graded on two independent axes. Evidence strength asks: how good is the research behind the claim as parents actually state it? Randomized trials and meta-analyses outrank single observational studies, which outrank expert opinion. Consensus asks a different question: where does the field actually sit? A claim can rest on limited evidence while researchers still broadly agree, and both facts belong on the label.

Every empirical statement in a summary is tied to the sources listed on its page, and an automated check enforces the honesty rules: claims below strong evidence cannot use proof-language, and claims with limited or insufficient evidence must say so in plain words. The check runs on every update to this site; a summary that overclaims cannot ship.

The grades are educational summaries of published research, not medical advice, and they can change; each page shows when it was last reviewed.

This page summarizes published research for educational purposes. It is not medical advice, and it is not a recommendation for or against any practice for your specific child; those calls belong with you and your child's clinician.
