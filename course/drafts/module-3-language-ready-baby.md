## Module 3 · The Language-Ready Baby

Your baby will probably not say a word you recognize until somewhere around
their first birthday, give or take a wide margin. That fact makes the first year
look, from the outside, like a waiting room. It is not. Long before the first
word, your baby is doing an enormous amount of listening work: sorting the
sound stream, learning which contrasts their language cares about, finding the
edges of words nobody has told them exist, and discovering that vocalizing at a
person produces a result. This module is about that work, about the small
number of things that genuinely support it, and about the much larger number
of things that have been sold to parents as though they did.

One framing note before we start, because it shapes everything that follows.
You have almost certainly encountered the claim that children in lower-income
homes hear thirty million fewer words by age three, and that this gap explains
later school outcomes. This course does not teach that claim, because the
evidence for it is contested and the framing behind it is worse than contested.
We deal with it directly in "What this module is not saying." For now: the
target is not word count. Nobody in this module is going to ask you to talk more.

### Lesson 3.1: What newborns already hear

Hearing is not switched on at birth. The cochlea and the auditory pathway are
functional well before a full-term baby is born, and the uterus is not a silent
place. It carries the parent's heartbeat, digestion, breathing, movement, and
voice. Sound from outside the body reaches the fetus too, though the tissue and
fluid act as a low-pass filter: high frequencies are muffled, low frequencies
come through. What survives that filtering is not the crisp consonants of
speech. It is the rhythm, the pitch contour, the stress pattern, the melody. A
fetus in the third trimester is getting something like speech heard through a
wall, plus the birth parent's voice conducted directly through the body, which
comes through considerably better than anyone else's.

The classic demonstration that this leaves a trace is DeCasper and Fifer (1980),
published in Science. They gave newborns a pacifier wired to measure sucking
patterns, and arranged it so that different sucking rhythms produced a recording
of either the birth parent's voice or an unfamiliar woman's voice. The newborns
adjusted their sucking to produce the birth parent's voice. Within days of being
born, they were doing work to hear a specific voice, and it was the one they had
been hearing from the inside for months.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** DeCasper & Fifer (1980) is a small-N classic. The study
> tested roughly ten newborns. It has been influential and the general finding
> that newborns show prenatal auditory familiarity has held up across related
> work, but the original sample is tiny by modern standards and this course
> should not present a ten-infant study with the same confidence as a multi-lab
> replication. Present it as a founding demonstration, not a settled effect size.

What that finding does not mean is that anything you did or did not do during
pregnancy programmed your baby's language. Playing recordings at a pregnant
belly, speaking a target language at it, or worrying that you were too quiet is
not what this research supports. What it supports is something calmer: your
baby arrived already knowing the sound of you, and already tuned to the rhythm
of the language spoken around them. That is a starting condition, not an
achievement to be optimized.

Now the more surprising half of the lesson. Alongside that familiarity with one
voice and one rhythm, newborns arrive as almost completely general listeners
for the fine detail of speech. Every spoken language uses a subset of the
consonant and vowel contrasts the human vocal tract can make. English
distinguishes the "r" in "rake" from the "l" in "lake," and Japanese does not
treat that difference as meaningful. Hindi distinguishes two "d" sounds made
with the tongue in slightly different places, and English speakers typically
cannot hear the difference at all without training. Salish languages distinguish
two "k"-like sounds made far back in the throat that English speakers hear as
identical.

Young infants, regardless of what language is spoken around them, discriminate
essentially all of these contrasts. An infant in an English-speaking home
distinguishes the Hindi consonants that their parents cannot. An infant in a
Japanese-speaking home distinguishes "r" from "l." Researchers establish this
with a simple and rather elegant method: play a repeating sound until the baby
gets bored and stops turning toward it, then switch to the other sound. If the
baby turns back, they heard a change. Run it enough times with enough infants
and you can map out what an infant's auditory system treats as the same and
what it treats as different.

The picture that comes out is of a system that starts wide. Your baby is not
being taught to hear speech sounds. Your baby can already hear speech sounds,
including sounds from languages nobody in your household speaks. What
happens over the first year is not addition. It is specialization, and that is
Lesson 3.2.

There is a practical consequence worth naming here, because parents ask about
it constantly. If your household runs on more than one language, your baby's
auditory system is entirely equipped for that. Exposure to two or more
languages does not delay language development, does not confuse infants, and
does not require you to segregate languages by person or by room. Bilingual
infants may know somewhat fewer words in each individual language while
knowing a comparable or larger number overall, and they hit the structural
milestones of language on the ordinary schedule. If you speak a language, speak
it to your baby. Speaking a heritage language badly in the sense of being out of
practice is still a real language environment. Speaking a language you are
fluent and comfortable in is worth more than speaking one you are stilted in
because you believe it will help them later.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Bilingual exposure being safe and normal is well supported
> and should stay. The separate and much-repeated claim that bilingualism
> confers a general executive-function advantage is contested. Lehtonen et al.
> (2018), a meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin, found little evidence for
> a bilingual advantage in executive function in adults once publication bias
> was accounted for. This module deliberately does not sell bilingualism as a
> cognitive enhancer. Keep the framing at "fine, normal, no delay," and flag if
> any downstream marketing copy tries to upgrade it.

One more thing that belongs in this lesson because it undercuts a lot of
anxiety. The auditory system's early competence means that your baby is
picking up the structure of your language from ordinary ambient speech and
ordinary speech directed at them. There is no evidence that a particular
quantity of high-quality audio input in the newborn period is doing work that
your normal talking is not. The newborn period is for feeding, sleeping,
holding, and recovering. The language system is already running in the
background.

### Lesson 3.2: Narrowing in, and statistical learning

Between about six and twelve months, that wide-open discrimination changes
shape. Werker and Tees (1984), published in Infant Behavior and Development,
gave this its clearest early demonstration. They tested infants from
English-speaking homes on non-native consonant contrasts, including contrasts
from Hindi and from Nthlakampx, a Salish language. Infants around six to eight
months discriminated the non-native contrasts. Infants around ten to twelve
months, from the same population, largely did not. Adult English speakers did
not either. The infants had not lost hearing. They had reorganized around the
distinctions their language actually uses.

Two things about this are worth sitting with. First, the timeline is fast. This
is a matter of a few months, in the same year the baby is also learning to sit
and crawl. Second, this is not a loss to mourn. It is what a system does when it
commits to a job. Treating every possible acoustic difference as potentially
meaningful is expensive. Deciding that the two Hindi "d" sounds are the same
thing frees the system to notice the differences that actually change meaning in
the language being spoken. The infant is becoming a specialist in the language
they live in.

The narrowing is also not a slammed door. Adults learn second languages, and
adults can be trained to hear non-native contrasts, with effort. Older children
who move into a new language environment acquire it. The first year is when
this happens most easily and most automatically, and that is a real and
interesting fact. It is not a deadline, and nothing in this course will treat it
as one.

The second big finding of this lesson is about a different problem the baby is
solving at the same time. Speech does not come with gaps between words. When
you listen to a language you know, you hear discrete words, and that feels like
a property of the sound. It is not. Listen to a language you do not know and you
hear a continuous stream. Your baby is in that position with your language: an
unbroken run of sound, from which the individual words have to be extracted
before any of them can be attached to meanings.

Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996), in Science, showed that eight-month-olds can
do this using statistics alone. They built an artificial language out of
nonsense syllables, assembled into three-syllable "words," and played it as a
continuous monotone stream with no pauses, no stress, no intonation, and no
meaning. Nothing in the signal marked where words began or ended except one
thing: within a word, one syllable reliably predicted the next, and at word
boundaries the next syllable was much less predictable. The infants listened for
two minutes. Then they were tested, and they responded differently to the
"words" than to sequences that spanned a boundary. In two minutes, with no
feedback and no meaning, eight-month-olds had tracked which syllables go
together.

This is a genuinely remarkable result and it has replicated and extended widely,
including to other domains such as tone sequences and visual sequences. It tells
you that a large part of early language learning is your baby running a
statistical engine over whatever speech they get. It also tells you something
reassuring about what that engine needs: repetition and consistency, which
ordinary family speech supplies by the bucketload. You say the same handful of
phrases about the same handful of routines all day. That is not a poverty of
input. It is exactly the shape of input the engine is built to eat.

But statistics alone are not the whole story, and this is where the most
important finding in the module comes in. Kuhl, Tsao and Liu (2003), in PNAS,
ran a study on nine-month-old infants from English-speaking homes. One group
got twelve sessions of live interaction with a Mandarin-speaking adult, who
played with them, read to them, and talked to them in Mandarin. Two other
groups got exactly the same Mandarin material, matched for content and amount,
delivered by audio recording or by video recording. A control group got the same
sessions in English.

The infants who had the live sessions retained Mandarin phonetic
discrimination: tested afterward, they performed at roughly the level of infants
who had been hearing Mandarin all their lives. The infants who got the audio and
the infants who got the video showed no such learning. They looked like the
control group that had never heard Mandarin at all.

The exposure was equated. The sound was equated. What differed was whether a
person was there, looking at them, responding to them, following where they
looked, handing them things at the moment they reached. That difference was the
whole difference between learning and not learning. Kuhl described this as
"social gating": for this kind of phonetic learning, the social channel is not
an enhancement on top of the audio, it is the condition under which the audio
counts.

You can read this two ways, and only one of them is honest. The unhelpful
reading is that every interaction must be maximally engaged or it is wasted.
That is not what the study shows and it would be an unbearable standard. The
honest reading is much simpler and much kinder: the thing that makes language
input work is a person, and you are already a person. A recording is not a
substitute for you, which mostly matters as a reason to stop buying recordings,
not as a reason to worry that you are not doing enough. The mechanism the
research identifies as necessary is the one you supply by existing in the room
and paying attention some of the time.

### Lesson 3.3: Babbling is a conversation

Babbling gets treated as background noise, or as a cute developmental
milestone with no function beyond being cute. It is neither. It is your baby
practicing motor control of the vocal tract, and it is your baby running
experiments on you.

The rough sequence, with the usual warning that the range is wide and that
being outside it is information rather than a verdict:

Around two to three months, cooing. Open vowel-ish sounds, made in the back of
the mouth: "ooh," "aah," "ngah." These often arrive in the middle of a face-to-
face exchange, when your baby is calm and alert and looking at you. They are
the first vocalizations that are clearly not crying and clearly not digestion.

Around four to six months, vocal play. Squeals, growls, raspberries, sudden
shifts of pitch. Your baby is finding the range of the instrument. This stage
sounds chaotic because it is chaotic on purpose.

Around six to ten months, canonical babbling. This is the big one. Canonical
babbling means well-formed consonant-vowel syllables with adult-like timing:
"ba," "da," "ma," "ga." It usually starts as marginal babbling, where the
transitions are slow and mushy, and sharpens into reduplicated babbling, where a
syllable repeats: "bababa," "dadada." Later in the first year it typically
becomes variegated: "badagu," strings mixing different syllables, often with
sentence-like intonation, so it sounds as though your baby is delivering an
opinion in a language you do not speak.

The onset of canonical babbling is one of the more informative single markers in
the first year, which is why it appears in the clinician note at the end of this
module. Its absence by around ten to twelve months is worth mentioning to a
pediatrician, not because it is a diagnosis but because it is a reasonable prompt
for a hearing check.

Now the part that changes how you hear all of it. Babbling is not a fixed
program unspooling on a timer. It is shaped, in real time, by how people
respond to it.

Goldstein, King and West (2003), in PNAS, put nine-month-olds in a play session
with their mothers, who wore headphones and followed instructions from the
experimenters. In one condition, mothers responded immediately to their baby's
vocalizations by moving closer, smiling, and touching. In another, mothers
produced exactly the same amount of the same behavior, but at times unrelated to
what the baby did. Same warmth, same quantity, same person. The only variable
was whether the response arrived contingently, right after the baby vocalized.

Within the session, the infants receiving contingent responses changed how they
babbled. Their vocalizations became more mature in structure, with more
consonant-vowel syllables and more adult-like phonology. The infants whose
mothers were equally warm but noncontingent did not show that shift. The change
appeared over minutes, inside a single session.

Goldstein and Schwade (2008), in Psychological Science, pushed on this further
and showed that the content of the response matters too. When caregivers
responded to infant babbling with specific speech, infants restructured their own
vocalizations toward the phonological patterns they heard in the response.
Infants were not merely vocalizing more because someone reacted. They were
taking the response as material and using it.

This is the finding that should reorganize your sense of what you are doing when
you answer a baby who cannot talk. You are not being polite to someone who
cannot understand you. You are closing a loop that the baby's vocal learning
system is specifically built to use. The response tells them two things at once:
that making sounds at a person produces an effect, and what sounds are worth
making.

Which brings up the single most practical skill in this module, and it is not
talking. It is pausing.

Watch an adult conversation and you will notice that turns alternate with
strikingly little gap. Human conversational turn-taking runs on a tight timing
structure across cultures. Babies are getting inducted into that structure long
before they have words to put in their turn. When you say something to your baby
and then stop and look at them expectantly, you have created a slot. Very often,
if you wait, they will fill it: with a coo, a syllable, a kick, a wide-open
mouth, a raised eyebrow. Then you respond to what they did, and you have a
three-turn conversation with a person who has no vocabulary.

The pause is doing the work. Most adults, especially anxious ones, do not leave
enough of one. Baby response times are slower than adult response times, so a
gap that feels awkwardly long to you is often about right for them. If you take
one behavioral thing from this entire module, take this: say your bit, then shut
up for a count of three and watch their face.

A word about quiet babies, since this lesson could easily be read as pressure.
Babies vary enormously in how much they vocalize, and a lot of that variation is
temperament rather than skill. A watchful, low-volume baby who tracks faces,
responds to sound, shares gaze, and eventually babbles is not behind. The
clinician note at the end of this module lists the things that are actually worth
raising. "Quieter than my sister's baby" is not on it.

### Lesson 3.4: What actually predicts language, and what does not

This lesson has to do two jobs. It has to tell you what the evidence supports,
and it has to be honest about how weak the causal footing is under most of it.
Most of the research linking early caregiver behavior to later language is
correlational. Families who do more of one thing differ from families who do
less of it in a hundred other ways, including genetically, and the study designs
mostly cannot separate those. This does not make the findings worthless. It
makes them a description of what tends to go together, not a set of instructions
with guaranteed outputs.

Start with the most robust finding in the area, because it is also the least
demanding.

Infants prefer infant-directed speech. Given a choice, babies orient longer
toward the slower, higher-pitched, more melodic, more exaggerated register that
adults slip into around babies, compared to the same speaker's adult-directed
speech. This is the finding the ManyBabies Consortium chose for its first
project, precisely because it was foundational enough that the field needed to
know whether it was real. ManyBabies 1, published in 2020 in Advances in Methods
and Practices in Psychological Science, ran the test across dozens of labs on
several continents with well over two thousand infants and a preregistered
protocol. The preference replicated. It was clearly present, it was larger in
older infants than younger ones, and it was stronger for infants hearing their
own language community's speech.

That register is not something you have to be taught. Most adults, including
people who insist they would never do it, produce it automatically when talking
to a baby. Its features are functionally useful: the exaggerated pitch contours
hold attention, and the stretched, clearer vowels make the sound categories
easier to sort. You do not need to perform it. You need to not suppress it out
of self-consciousness.

Next, the finding people most often distort. Weisleder and Fernald (2013), in
Psychological Science, recorded full days of the language environments of
Spanish-learning infants from low-income families using all-day audio recorders,
and separated the recordings into speech directed at the child and speech merely
audible in the environment. They then measured how quickly the children could
identify a spoken word in a lab task, and their vocabulary later on. Directed
speech, not overheard speech, related to processing efficiency, and processing
efficiency in turn related to vocabulary at twenty-four months.

The useful lesson is about direction rather than volume: speech aimed at your
baby does work that speech happening near your baby does not. A television on in
the room, adults talking over the baby's head, a podcast in the car: that is
ambient, and the data give no reason to think it is contributing. Ten minutes of
someone talking to your actual baby is worth more than three hours of language
in the air.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Weisleder & Fernald (2013) is correlational. Directed speech
> was measured, not assigned. Families that produce more directed speech differ
> systematically from families that produce less, including in ways that are
> heritable and in ways that track material circumstances such as work hours,
> housing, and the number of adults available. This study cannot license a
> causal instruction of the form "talk to your baby more and their vocabulary
> will rise." The permitted framing is: directed speech is what tracks the
> outcome, ambient speech does not, and that is a reason to aim speech at your
> baby when you are already talking, not a reason to believe you can dose your
> way to a vocabulary. Any copy that turns this into a causal promise should be
> rejected at review.

Third, responsiveness. Tamis-LeMonda, Bornstein and Baumwell (2001), in Child
Development, followed infants and found that caregiver responsiveness, meaning
prompt and appropriate reactions to what the child was already doing and
attending to, predicted the timing of language milestones better than sheer
quantity of caregiver talk. The pattern that keeps showing up across this
literature is contingency: language input that arrives in response to the child,
about the thing the child is already looking at, is doing more work than input
that arrives on the adult's schedule about the adult's topic. This is
correlational as well, but it converges with the experimental babbling work in
Lesson 3.3, and that convergence is worth something.

Putting the supported material together, what predicts language is not
impressive. Talk to your baby, not just near them. Talk about what they are
currently attending to. Respond when they vocalize. Leave pauses. Do it in
whatever language you actually speak. That is the list.

Now the things that do not work, which matter because they cost money and
attention.

Baby sign. Kirk, Howlett, Pine and Fletcher (2013), in Child Development, ran an
actual randomized controlled trial, comparing infants whose caregivers were
trained in symbolic gesturing, infants whose caregivers were trained to use
verbal labels equivalently, and a non-intervention control. The gesture-trained
infants showed no advantage in language development over the controls. This is a
supported null result and it is a good one, because it isolates the thing being
sold. Gesturing with your baby is fine and enjoyable, and pointing and reaching
are genuinely important parts of communication. Paid programs promising that
signing will accelerate speech are selling something the trial did not find.

Educational videos. DeLoache et al. (2010), in Psychological Science, tested
infants aged twelve to eighteen months with a popular educational baby DVD over
several weeks, comparing infants who watched with a parent, infants who watched
alone, infants whose parents taught the same words without any video, and a
no-exposure control. The infants who watched the DVD learned no more words than
the infants who did not. Parents who liked the DVD believed their children had
learned from it. The word tests disagreed.

This connects straight back to Kuhl, Tsao and Liu: the screen fails for the same
reason the audio and video conditions failed in that study. It does not respond
to your baby. It cannot follow their gaze, wait for their turn, or answer the
sound they just made.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Screen time. The evidence that infants do not learn language
> from screens is strong and experimental. The broader observational literature
> linking early screen exposure to worse language or attention outcomes is much
> weaker: effect sizes are small, the designs are confounded by everything that
> co-varies with household screen use, and reverse causation is plausible since
> harder-to-soothe children get handed screens more. This module should say
> "screens do not teach infants words" and should not say "screens harm your
> baby's brain." Flagged so review can check no downstream copy escalates it.

The through-line of everything above is that the active ingredient is a
responsive person. That is also why the "more words is better" framing is the
wrong target, which is where we go next.

### Try this week

**1. The three-second pause.**
What to do: several times a day, when your baby is calm, alert, and looking at
you, say one short thing to them. Then stop, keep looking at them, keep your
face open, and silently count to three.
What you are looking for: anything that fills the slot. A coo, a syllable, a
mouth opening, a kick, a sharp intake of breath, an eyebrow. Then respond to
whatever they did, as though they had said something, and pause again.
What it means if nothing happens: usually that they are not in the right state.
Vocal exchanges happen in the calm-alert window, which is narrow in young
babies and can be minutes long in a whole day. Try again after a nap or in the
middle of a diaper change, which is a reliably face-to-face moment. If nothing
comes back for weeks and there is also no reaction to sound, see the clinician
note.

**2. Answer the babble with words, not just noise.**
What to do: when your baby produces a syllable, respond promptly with real
speech that picks up what they did. If they say "ba," you might say "ba! Yeah,
the ball," rather than only smiling or only echoing "ba ba ba" back.
What you are looking for: over days, not minutes, whether they vocalize more
when you do this, and whether the sounds start including more consonants.
Goldstein and Schwade found infants restructuring toward what they heard in the
response.
What it means if nothing happens: at three months, nothing should happen yet,
because consonant-vowel babbling has not arrived. Under six months this activity
is mostly for the turn-taking, not the sounds. It is not a test.

**3. Aim your talking, once a day, on purpose.**
What to do: pick one routine you already do, such as a bath, a change, or
unpacking groceries, and talk through it to your baby's face rather than over
their head. Name the thing they are actually looking at, when they are looking
at it.
What you are looking for: their gaze coming to your face and going back to the
object, and eventually their eyes going to the thing you named.
What it means if nothing happens: following someone else's attention develops
across the second half of the first year and is not expected early. In young
babies, look for face-watching instead. Persistent lack of eye contact with
familiar people is worth raising.

**4. Turn the background off for twenty minutes.**
What to do: once a day, kill the TV, radio, or podcast during a period when you
are with your baby. You are not doing this for silence. You are doing it so that
the speech that exists is speech aimed at them.
What you are looking for: honestly, mostly a change in you. Most people talk to
the baby more when nothing else is talking.
What it means if nothing happens: nothing is wrong. This is an adjustment to
your own environment, not a test of your baby. Skip it on the days when the
background noise is what is keeping you upright.

**5. Use your own language, out loud.**
What to do: if you speak a language other than the community language, use it
with your baby, including songs and phrases you learned as a child.
What you are looking for: your own fluency and comfort, which is the thing that
actually varies the input quality.
What it means if nothing happens: there is nothing to look for in the short
term. This is included because parents are routinely and wrongly advised to drop
a heritage language to protect the community language. There is no good evidence
supporting that advice.

### What this module is not saying

**It is not saying the thirty-million-word gap is settled science.** Hart and
Risley (1995) reported a large difference in cumulative words heard by age three
between children in professional and welfare-recipient families, and that figure
became one of the most repeated numbers in early childhood policy. Sperry,
Sperry and Miller (2019), in Child Development, attempted to replicate the
finding across five communities and did not. The original study rested on a very
small number of families, counted only speech directed at the child by a primary
caregiver in the home, and used that count as though it measured the child's
whole language environment. In communities where children are talked to by many
adults and older children, where multiple people talk at once, and where
overheard and multiparty speech carries a large share of the load, that method
systematically undercounts. The deficit framing that followed misread
non-mainstream caregiver language environments as impoverished when what was
actually impoverished was the measurement. This course does not use the word gap
as evidence for anything.

**It is not saying word count is the target.** No number is the target. There is
no daily word quota, no app that should be counting for you, and no evidence
that pushing your talk volume upward produces the outcomes the correlational
studies describe. Contingency and direction are what the evidence points at.
Volume is the thing that got measured because it was easy to measure.

**It is not saying baby sign accelerates speech.** Kirk et al. (2013) ran the
randomized trial and found no language advantage from gestural training. Sign
with your baby if you enjoy it. Do not pay for it as an accelerator.

**It is not saying educational videos teach words.** DeLoache et al. (2010)
found no word learning from a popular baby DVD. Screens are sometimes a
reasonable choice for a parent who needs fifteen minutes. They are not
instruction.

**It is not saying a quiet baby is a behind baby.** Vocal output varies hugely
and much of that variation is temperament. Receptive language, meaning what your
baby understands, runs ahead of expressive language throughout this period and is
harder to see from outside. A quiet baby who responds to sound, watches faces,
shares attention, and babbles on some schedule is not a project.

**It is not saying the first year is a closing window.** Perceptual narrowing is
real and the first year is when phonetic tuning happens most automatically. It is
not a deadline. Second languages are learnable later, children who change
language environments acquire the new one, and plasticity in language systems
continues well beyond infancy.

**It is not saying bilingual exposure makes babies smarter.** It does not delay
language, it does not confuse infants, and it is a completely fine thing to do.
The claim that it produces a general cognitive or executive-function advantage is
contested and this course does not make it.

### When to raise it with a clinician

This is information, not a verdict. Almost every baby who shows one of these
turns out to be fine, and the reason to mention them is that the follow-up is
usually simple, often just a hearing check, and earlier is easier than later.

Worth raising with your pediatrician:

- No babbling, meaning no consonant-vowel syllables such as "ba" or "da," by
  around nine to twelve months.
- No apparent response to sound at any age: not startling at loud noises, not
  quieting or turning toward a familiar voice. Raise this immediately rather
  than waiting, at any age.
- Loss of skills that were previously present. A baby who was babbling and
  stops, or who was responding to their name and no longer does, should be
  discussed promptly. Regression is the one item on this list that is worth an
  appointment rather than a mention at the next well visit.
- No shared gaze, no pointing, and no gestures such as reaching, showing, or
  waving by twelve months.

Two practical notes. First, hearing changes. A newborn hearing screen that passed
does not rule out hearing loss that developed later, including after ear
infections, so a past pass is not a reason to dismiss a current concern. Second,
you are allowed to ask without a strong case. "I want to rule out a hearing
issue" is a complete sentence at a well-child visit.

The CDC's "Learn the Signs. Act Early." program maintains free milestone
checklists and a tracking app, organized by age, along with plain guidance on
what to do if something concerns you: https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/

### Module summary

- Hearing works before birth. Newborns show a preference for the birth parent's
  voice, which they have been hearing filtered through the body for months
  (DeCasper & Fifer 1980, small sample, treat as a founding demonstration).
- Newborns discriminate essentially all the phonetic contrasts used by human
  languages, including contrasts their own caregivers cannot hear.
- Between roughly six and twelve months, that generality narrows to the
  contrasts their own language uses (Werker & Tees 1984). This is
  specialization, not damage, and it is not a deadline.
- Eight-month-olds can extract word-like units from two minutes of continuous
  nonsense speech using nothing but the statistics of which syllable follows
  which (Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996). Ordinary repetitive family talk is
  exactly the right input for this.
- Statistics are not sufficient on their own. Nine-month-olds learned Mandarin
  phonetic contrasts from live sessions with a person and learned nothing from
  identical audio or video (Kuhl, Tsao & Liu 2003). The social channel is the
  condition, not a bonus.
- Babbling develops from cooing around two to three months, through vocal play,
  to canonical consonant-vowel syllables around six to ten months, then to
  variegated strings. The range is wide.
- Babbling is shaped by responses in real time. Contingent caregiver reactions
  made infant vocalizations more mature within a single session, while equally
  warm but noncontingent reactions did not (Goldstein, King & West 2003), and
  infants restructure their sounds toward what they hear in the response
  (Goldstein & Schwade 2008).
- The most useful skill is pausing. Say something, stop, look, and count to
  three. Baby response times are slower than adult ones.
- Infants prefer infant-directed speech, and this replicated across dozens of
  labs and thousands of infants (ManyBabies Consortium 2020). You do not have to
  perform it, just not suppress it.
- Speech directed at your baby tracks later processing efficiency and
  vocabulary; speech merely audible in the room does not (Weisleder & Fernald
  2013, correlational, cannot license a causal instruction).
- Responsiveness predicted language milestone timing better than quantity of
  talk (Tamis-LeMonda, Bornstein & Baumwell 2001, correlational, converges with
  the experimental babbling work).
- Baby sign training produced no language advantage in a randomized trial (Kirk
  et al. 2013), and a popular baby DVD produced no word learning (DeLoache et al.
  2010). The word gap is not the story, word count is not the target, and a quiet
  baby is not a behind baby.
