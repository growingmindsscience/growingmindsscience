## Module 4 · Connection and Security

This module is about the relationship your baby is building with you, and about
what the research does and does not actually say regarding it. Attachment is
probably the most talked-about idea in all of parenting, and it is also the one
most likely to be sold back to you as a product, a technique, or a warning. So
this module does two jobs at once. It explains what attachment researchers have
found over sixty years of work, and it draws a clear line around what that work
cannot tell you. If you take one thing from this module, take this: security is
built out of ordinary, repeated, imperfect interaction, and the repair of
mismatches matters more than the absence of them.

### Lesson 4.1 - What attachment actually is

Start with the word itself, because it has been stretched almost past usefulness.

In everyday conversation, "attachment" usually means something like closeness,
or affection, or how much a baby likes a person. In the research literature it
means something narrower and more specific. Attachment is the system a baby
uses to get protection from a particular adult when the baby is frightened,
hurt, sick, or overwhelmed. That is the whole job of it. It is a safety system,
not a love meter.

John Bowlby, who built the theory across the volumes of *Attachment and Loss*
beginning in 1969, argued that human infants arrive with a behavioural system
that has one goal: stay close enough to a protective adult to survive. Crying,
clinging, following, reaching, and later crawling toward you are all outputs of
that system. When your baby is calm and the world seems fine, the system idles
and exploration takes over. When something goes wrong, the system switches on
and your baby seeks you.

Bowlby gave us two phrases that are still the most useful shorthand in the
field. The first is **safe haven**: the place a baby goes when distressed, to be
comforted and settled. The second is **secure base**: the person a baby uses as
a launching point for exploring the world, checking back periodically, moving
further out as confidence grows. Both are the same relationship doing two
different things. A baby who can be soothed by you and who can also leave you to
go investigate the dog is using you both ways.

Notice that both of those are descriptions of a *relationship*, not descriptions
of a baby. This is the single most commonly mangled point in popular attachment
writing, so it is worth being blunt about. A baby does not have an attachment
style the way a baby has an eye colour. A baby has an attachment relationship
with each specific caregiver, and those relationships can differ from one another
within the same family. A baby can be securely attached to one adult and less
securely attached to another. There is no such thing as "an insecure baby." There
are only relationships with more or less security in them, and relationships can
change.

#### How anyone measures this

Mary Ainsworth, working with Bowlby's framework, developed the procedure that
turned the theory into something testable. In *Patterns of Attachment*
(Ainsworth, Blehar, Waters & Wall, 1978), she described the Strange Situation: a
roughly twenty-minute laboratory sequence, typically run at around twelve months,
in which a baby experiences a new room, a stranger, two brief separations from
the caregiver, and, crucially, two reunions.

The thing that surprises most people is that the separations are not what get
scored. The **reunions** are. The question is not how upset a baby gets when you
leave. Babies vary enormously in that, and how much a baby cries tells you more
about temperament than about the relationship. The question is what a baby does
when you come back: whether they seek you, whether contact with you actually
works to settle them, and whether they can then go back to playing.

From that, Ainsworth described patterns:

- **Secure.** The baby shows distress or at least notices the separation, seeks
  the caregiver on return, is comforted by contact, and returns to exploring.
- **Insecure-avoidant.** The baby appears to attend to toys rather than to the
  caregiver on return, showing little overt seeking.
- **Insecure-resistant** (sometimes called ambivalent). The baby seeks contact
  but is hard to settle, mixing approach with protest.

A fourth category, **disorganized**, was described later by Main and Solomon and
is discussed further below.

In typical low-risk samples, secure is the largest group, usually somewhere
around half to two-thirds. That fact gets misused constantly. Secure being the
majority pattern does not make it a grade, a target, or a verdict on anyone. The
insecure patterns are not disorders. They are, in the main, sensible strategies a
baby has worked out for a particular relationship, and plenty of children in every
category grow up perfectly well.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Attachment classification stability from infancy into
> adulthood is far weaker than popular writing implies. Longitudinal work shows
> meaningful discontinuity, especially where family circumstances change. The
> course should not imply that a twelve-month classification predicts an adult's
> romantic life. Please confirm the framing here is conservative enough.

#### Does this travel?

van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988, *Child Development*, 59, 147-156) pooled
Strange Situation studies across eight countries and found something important in
both directions. The distribution of classifications varied somewhat between
countries, but the variation *within* cultures was larger than the variation
*between* them. In other words, the differences people like to attribute to
national parenting styles were smaller than the ordinary spread inside any given
country.

That is a genuinely useful finding, and it is also not a blanket licence to treat
the Strange Situation as culturally universal. The procedure was designed around
assumptions about how often babies are separated from a primary caregiver and
how many caregivers a baby routinely has, and those assumptions do not hold
everywhere.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Cross-cultural applicability of the Strange Situation is
> actively debated, particularly regarding samples where multiple caregiving and
> continuous carrying are the norm and the procedure's separations are far more
> unusual than in the original samples. Presented here as contested rather than
> settled. Please review.

#### About disorganized attachment

There is a fourth classification, disorganized, in which a baby shows
contradictory or apparently confused behaviour at reunion, such as approaching
and then freezing, or moving toward the caregiver while turning away. It is
associated in the research with frightening or severely inconsistent caregiving
environments and with higher risk in some later outcomes, and it is a real and
useful research category.

It is also, in popular content, one of the most abused terms in developmental
psychology. So, plainly: disorganized attachment is a classification produced by
trained coders watching a specific standardized procedure and applying a manual.
It is not a mood, not a trait you can spot at home, and not something a parent
can or should assess in their own baby from a video or a checklist. If you are
genuinely worried about your baby's relationship with you, or about anything
frightening in your household, the right move is a conversation with your
paediatric provider or a mental health clinician who works with infants, not a
self-diagnosis from an internet post.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Disorganized attachment is routinely over-interpreted in
> popular parenting content. Confirm this passage is brief, non-diagnostic, and
> routes clearly to a clinician.

Finally, one boundary condition worth stating because it shows what the extreme
end actually looks like. The Bucharest Early Intervention Project (Nelson,
Zeanah, Fox and colleagues, 2007, *Science*) studied children raised in severely
depriving institutions, where there was effectively no consistent caregiver at
all. That work found substantial developmental harm, and improvement for children
placed into foster families, with better outcomes for earlier placement. It is
important evidence about severe deprivation. It says nothing about ordinary
households where a tired adult is doing their best, and it should never be
recruited to make an ordinary parent anxious.

### Lesson 4.2 - The still-face experiment and why repair matters

In 1978, Edward Tronick and colleagues published a short paper describing a
procedure that has since been shown to more parents than almost any other piece
of infant research (Tronick, Als, Adamson, Wise & Brazelton, 1978, *Journal of
the American Academy of Child Psychiatry*). You may well have seen the video.

The setup is simple. A parent sits face to face with their baby, close, and they
play as they normally would: smiling, talking, responding, the ordinary back and
forth. Then, on cue, the parent stops. They hold a still, neutral face and do not
respond at all for a couple of minutes. Then normal interaction resumes.

What babies do in that middle phase is remarkably consistent. First they try
harder. They smile more, they vocalize, they reach, they point, they do the
things that have previously worked to bring the adult back. When that fails, most
babies escalate, then withdraw: they look away, they slump, they may fuss or cry,
they may self-soothe. When the parent comes back online, most babies re-engage
fairly quickly, though often with some residual wariness.

Mesman, van IJzendoorn and Bakermans-Kranenburg (2009, *Developmental Review*)
pulled together the accumulated still-face literature and confirmed that this is
one of the more robust effects in infancy research. Across many studies and many
samples, babies reliably decrease positive affect and increase negative affect
and gaze aversion during the still-face phase. It replicates.

#### What the video is usually used to say, and what it actually shows

The still-face clip is almost always presented as a warning: look what happens
when you are not emotionally available. Put your phone down, or this.

That is not a fair reading of the study, and it is worth explaining why.

The still-face condition is deliberately strange. It is not distraction. It is
not being busy. It is a parent who is physically present, close, staring
directly at their baby, and giving *nothing*. Babies are not upset because
attention paused. They are upset because they are receiving a strong, direct
social signal that has been abruptly stripped of all its content. The experiment
is designed to be a violation, not a simulation of everyday life.

What the paradigm actually demonstrates, and this is the genuinely important
finding, is that babies in the first months of life already **expect** social
interaction to be mutual and contingent. They have built a working model of how
exchanges go, and they detect and protest a break in it. That is a statement
about how sophisticated your baby already is, not an indictment of you.

#### Mismatch is the normal state

Here is the part of Tronick's work that almost never makes it into the popular
version, and it is the part parents most need.

Tronick and Gianino, studying ordinary face-to-face interaction frame by frame,
described normal parent-infant exchange as a continuous cycle of **mismatch and
repair**. The adult misreads a cue. The baby looks away just as the adult leans
in. The timing is off. One of them wants more, the other wants a break. Then one
or both of them corrects, and the pair comes back into coordination. Then it goes
off again. This happens over and over, all day.

The claim that comes out of that work is that coordinated states are the
*minority* of interaction time, and that the two partners spend much of their
time out of sync and repairing. A figure of roughly thirty percent coordination
is widely quoted.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** The frequently cited "about 30% of interactions are
> coordinated" figure derives from a small observational corpus of micro-coded
> mother-infant dyads, not from a large or meta-analytic base. The qualitative
> point (mismatch is common and repair is routine) is well supported; the precise
> percentage should be presented as illustrative rather than as a stable
> population estimate. Please confirm the hedging here.

Take the specific number lightly. Take the structure seriously, because it
reframes the entire task. If attunement were about accuracy, parenting would be a
test you fail hundreds of times a day. It is not about accuracy. The repeated
experience your baby is accumulating is not "this person always reads me
correctly." It is "when this goes wrong, it gets fixed." That expectation, that
ruptures are survivable and repairable, is a far more useful thing to carry
through a life than an expectation of perfect understanding, which no relationship
of any kind will ever deliver.

This has a practical consequence that a lot of parents find enormously relieving.
You do not have to catch every cue. You do not have to respond within a fixed
number of seconds. You missed it, your baby protested, you noticed and came back:
that whole sequence is not a failure followed by a recovery. That sequence *is*
the mechanism. Repair is not damage control. It is the curriculum.

It also means that the moments you feel worst about, the times you snapped or
zoned out or handed the baby over because you had nothing left, are not deposits
in some deficit account. What matters is what happens next. Coming back, settling
yourself, re-engaging, that is the whole move, and you have almost certainly done
it hundreds of times without labelling it.

One honest caveat. The mismatch-and-repair account describes ordinary variation
in ordinary relationships. It is not a claim that anything at all can be repaired,
and it is not intended to minimize the effects of frightening or chronically
unresponsive environments, which are a different phenomenon at a different scale.

### Lesson 4.3 - Separation, stranger wariness, and the eight-month reorganization

Somewhere in the second half of the first year, often between about six and ten
months, a lot of babies change in a way that catches parents completely off
guard. A baby who was happily handed around at family gatherings starts to
stiffen and turn away from an aunt. A baby who was content in the next room
starts to protest the moment you leave. Bedtime, which had settled, comes apart.
Someone will tell you the baby has regressed.

They have not regressed. They have gotten smarter, and this is one of the
clearest examples in the whole first year of a new ability showing up disguised
as a problem.

As always, the range is wide. Some babies show intense separation distress and
stranger wariness; some show very little; some show it early, some late, some in
waves that come and go for months. Being outside the typical window is
information, not a verdict, and the absence of stranger wariness is not a warning
sign in itself.

#### Why it happens when it happens

Three things are coming together at once in that stretch.

First, your baby has become able to reliably tell familiar from unfamiliar. That
sounds trivial. It is not. It requires holding a stable representation of known
faces and comparing incoming people against it. Wariness of a stranger is
evidence that the comparison is working.

Second, memory for people and objects has improved enough that your baby can hold
you in mind when you are not there. This is the same underlying advance that
shows up as searching for a hidden toy. Before this, out of sight was, functionally,
out of mind, and there was nothing to miss. Now there is a *you* who continues to
exist somewhere else, and your baby can notice the gap between where you are and
where they want you.

Third, most babies become mobile around this period, which changes the whole
safety calculation. A baby who can move away from you has, for the first time, a
real need for a system that tracks how far away you are and pulls them back. The
secure base behaviour from Lesson 4.1 becomes visible precisely because there is
now something to be a base for.

Put those three together and separation protest is not a step backward. It is the
predictable output of a baby who can now represent you, prefer you specifically,
and notice your absence. It is, in a real sense, the attachment relationship
becoming observable.

#### What actually helps at goodbyes

The evidence base for specific goodbye techniques is thinner than the confidence
with which they are usually recommended, so treat what follows as reasonable
practice consistent with the theory rather than as tested protocol.

**Say goodbye.** Sneaking out avoids the protest in the moment and undermines the
thing you want, which is a baby who can predict what you do. A short, clear,
consistent goodbye gives your baby something to learn.

**Keep it short and keep it the same.** A long, anxious, repeatedly extended
departure gives a baby more evidence that something here is worth being worried
about. A predictable phrase and a predictable exit is easier to build an
expectation around.

**Let the transfer be to a person, not to an absence.** Handing over into
someone's arms, with a moment of engagement between them, is easier than putting
a baby down and leaving.

**Expect the protest and expect it to end.** Caregivers routinely report that
crying stops within a few minutes of a parent's exit. That is not a trick and it
is not a reason to feel dismissed. It reflects the fact that the protest is a
signal, and once it has done its job and the situation has stabilized, the system
settles.

**Reunions are worth more than departures.** This follows directly from what the
Strange Situation actually scores. A warm, unhurried return, where you let your
baby take the contact they want before you move them on to the next thing, is the
part with the most theoretical weight behind it.

#### Non-parental care

Now the question underneath all of this, which many parents are carrying quietly
and which some have been made to feel guilty about.

The NICHD Early Child Care Research Network followed over a thousand American
children from infancy in a large longitudinal study, examining childcare
arrangements alongside attachment security and later outcomes. Its findings on
this point have been consistent and are worth stating plainly: routine
non-parental care, in itself, did not determine attachment security. Children in
childcare were not, as a group, less securely attached to their parents. What
mattered was the quality of care and the quality of the parenting the child
received, and the strongest predictors of child outcomes throughout the study were
family and parenting variables rather than the mere fact of childcare.

The study did find that risk accumulated in combination: insecure attachment was
more likely where low parental sensitivity co-occurred with poor-quality,
unstable, or very extensive care. That is a statement about compounding
disadvantage, not about the decision to work.

Two honest limits. This is a longitudinal correlational design, not a randomized
one, so it cannot fully separate the effects of care arrangements from the
characteristics of families who choose them. And it is a study of a particular
country and period, with its own childcare market and parental leave landscape.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** NICHD findings on childcare and attachment are correlational
> and have been subject to secondary reanalysis and dispute, particularly around
> the quantity-of-care and later-behaviour findings. This passage deliberately
> keeps to the attachment-security result and states the design limit. Please
> confirm the summary is accurate and appropriately narrow.

The practical version: your baby can have a secure relationship with you and warm
relationships with other caregivers at the same time. These are not in
competition, and babies form multiple attachment relationships as a matter of
course. What the evidence points at is the quality of the interaction your baby
gets, from whoever is providing it, not the number of hours you personally
supply.

### Lesson 4.4 - Co-regulation in the first year

A newborn cannot calm themselves. This is not a skill they are withholding or one
they will acquire if properly trained. The neural machinery for regulating one's
own arousal is under construction for years, and in the first twelve months it is
barely started. What your baby has instead, in the meantime, is you.

That is what co-regulation means. Your baby borrows your nervous system. When you
pick them up, when your breathing is slow, when your voice drops and steadies,
when your body is not braced, your baby's own arousal comes down along with
yours. When you are frantic, they usually get more frantic. This is not mystical.
It runs through entirely ordinary channels: touch, pressure, rhythmic movement,
the pitch and pace of your voice, the tension in your arms, and the fact that
your baby has been reading your face for social information since the first weeks.

#### What soothing is actually doing

It is worth being clear about the mechanism, because there is a persistent worry
that comforting a baby teaches them to need comforting.

Soothing does two things. In the moment, it brings a physiological state down out
of distress: heart rate settles, the crying stops, the body unclenches. Over
repetition, it does something slower and more important. Each time distress rises
and then reliably comes back down with your help, your baby is getting one more
instance of a pattern: this state is survivable, and it ends. The eventual
capacity to self-regulate is built out of thousands of experiences of being
regulated by someone else. You are not creating dependence. You are running the
process that dependence eventually resolves into.

Which also means there is no age in the first year at which responding to
distress becomes counterproductive as a matter of developmental principle. Some
practical arrangements around sleep and settling are debated, and that debate
belongs in the module on sleep rather than here, but the general claim that
comfort spoils a baby has no developmental basis.

#### How much does responsiveness actually matter?

Here is where this module has to do something that most attachment content does
not do, which is tell you the size of the effect.

De Wolff and van IJzendoorn (1997, *Child Development*) meta-analysed the
accumulated studies linking parental sensitivity to attachment security. They
found a real, replicated association. They also found that it was **modest**. The
effect size for sensitivity was around r = .24, which in plain terms means
sensitivity is one meaningful contributor among many, and it is nowhere near
sufficient to determine the outcome. Their paper's own framing, that sensitivity
is an important but not exclusive condition, is the accurate one.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** The sensitivity-to-attachment effect size is modest (roughly
> r = .24 in De Wolff & van IJzendoorn, 1997) and popular writing routinely
> overstates it, sometimes to the point of implying that parental responsiveness
> alone determines attachment classification. This module states the size
> explicitly. Please confirm the number and the interpretation.

That modest number is not a disappointment. It is the most reassuring finding in
this module, and it is why it is included. It means the relationship is not a
precision instrument. It is not sitting on a knife edge waiting for you to
mistime a response. There is enormous room in it for bad days, bad weeks, missed
cues, and being a person with your own limits.

#### Can sensitivity be changed? Yes, and briefly

The other half of the picture comes from Bakermans-Kranenburg, van IJzendoorn and
Juffer (2003, *Psychological Bulletin*), a meta-analysis of interventions
designed to improve parental sensitivity and attachment security. Two findings
matter here.

First, it works. Interventions that focus on parental sensitivity do improve
sensitivity, and, less strongly, improve attachment security. This is one of the
places in developmental research where we have something closer to causal
evidence, because many of the included studies were randomized.

Second, and this is the finding that should be much better known: the
interventions that worked best were **brief and narrowly focused on behaviour**.
Short programmes with a clear behavioural target outperformed long, broad,
multi-component programmes that tried to address everything. More was not better.
Less, aimed at something specific, was better.

Read that alongside the modest effect size and a fairly clear picture emerges of
what the evidence actually supports. It supports doing a small number of ordinary
things reasonably often. It does not support an intensive, all-encompassing
parenting philosophy, and there is no evidence that maximum effort produces
maximum security.

#### "Good enough" is the empirical standard, not a consolation prize

The phrase "good enough" comes originally from Donald Winnicott, a paediatrician
and psychoanalyst, and it was a clinical intuition rather than a finding. What is
striking is how well the data has treated it.

Put the three results in this module together. Sensitivity predicts security at a
modest effect size. Coordinated interaction is the minority state and repair is
the norm. Brief, focused intervention outperforms comprehensive intervention.
Every one of those points in the same direction: the system is robust, it
tolerates a great deal of ordinary error, and it is built for a caregiver who is
tired and distracted and sometimes annoyed, because that is the only kind of
caregiver there has ever been.

So the practical target is not attunement. It is availability. Being reachable
often enough, coming back after ruptures, and letting yourself be the person your
baby settles against. That is not the minimum viable version of the thing. As far
as the evidence goes, it is the thing.

One last note on you. Co-regulation runs on your state, which means your own
regulation is not a self-care luxury bolted onto the parenting; it is part of the
mechanism. Anything that keeps you steadier, whether that is sleep, help,
treatment for anxiety or depression, or someone taking the baby for two hours,
feeds directly into what your baby is borrowing. If you are struggling, getting
support is not a detour from this work. It is this work.

### Try this week

**1. The reunion, deliberately.**
*What to do:* Next time you have been apart from your baby for more than a few
minutes, whether that is a work day, a nap, or ten minutes in another room, slow
your return down. Get to their level, greet them, and let them come to you and
take as much contact as they want before you move on to the next thing. Do not
rush them back into play.
*What you are looking for:* Whether contact with you actually changes their
state. Does the fussing wind down? Does the body soften? Do they then turn back
toward the room and their toys?
*If nothing happens:* Some babies barely register short separations, and some are
mid-nap-transition and just want to be put down. A baby who does not need much
comforting at reunion is not showing you a problem. You are looking for whether
you *work* as a source of settling when they want one, not for how much they want
one.

**2. Watch one mismatch and one repair.**
*What to do:* During any ordinary five minutes of face-to-face time, look
specifically for a moment where you and your baby are out of sync. You lean in as
they look away. You offer the rattle and they wanted your face. Then watch what
happens over the next fifteen seconds as one of you adjusts.
*What you are looking for:* The correction. Notice that you are already doing
this, constantly, without naming it. That cycle is the mechanism from Lesson 4.2.
*If nothing happens:* If you cannot spot a clean example, that is a limitation of
watching in real time, not evidence that your interaction is unusually smooth or
unusually rough. Micro-coding this takes trained observers and slow video. Try
again another day, or let it go.

**3. Name the goodbye.**
*What to do:* Pick one short phrase and one small gesture and use exactly the
same ones every time you leave, even for five minutes in another room. Say it,
do it, go. Do not extend it, and do not sneak out.
*What you are looking for:* Over a couple of weeks, whether your baby begins to
show any anticipation of the phrase, and whether the protest shortens. Also
notice how it changes *you*, since a script removes some of the decision-making
from a moment that tends to be emotionally loaded.
*If nothing happens:* Under about six months, most babies will not respond to the
routine at all, because the underlying memory and person-permanence abilities are
still developing. Later, plenty of babies protest just as loudly with a perfect
routine as without one. The routine is for predictability, not for preventing
distress.

**4. One two-minute state check on yourself.**
*What to do:* Next time your baby is crying and you feel your own system winding
up, take two minutes before or during the soothing to deliberately slow your own
breathing and drop your shoulders, and notice your voice pitch. If it is safe to
do so and you are past your limit, put your baby down somewhere safe and step
away for a moment first.
*What you are looking for:* Whether your baby's state follows yours at all, on
any lag. Sometimes it is quick. Often it is not.
*If nothing happens:* Plenty of crying has causes that no amount of your calm
will resolve, including pain, hunger, illness, and long unexplained evening
crying. Your steadiness is not a switch and failing to soothe a crying baby is not
a failure of co-regulation. Being calm while unable to fix it still counts.

**5. Audit one source of attachment advice.**
*What to do:* Take one piece of attachment content you have seen recently, from
anywhere, and check it against this module. Does it treat attachment as a fixed
trait of the baby? Does it use the still-face video as a warning about phones?
Does it claim a specific practice such as carrying, bedsharing, or feeding method
produces secure attachment?
*What you are looking for:* The gap between "attachment security" as a
measured relationship construct and "attachment parenting" as a commercial set of
practices. They share a word and very little else.
*If nothing happens:* If the content holds up, good, keep the source. This is not
an exercise in finding fault. It is an exercise in noticing which claims come
with a study behind them.

### What this module is not saying

- **Not saying that attachment style is fixed for life.** Classification at
  twelve months describes a relationship at that time. Longitudinal stability
  into adulthood is much weaker than popular accounts suggest, and relationships
  change when circumstances change.
- **Not saying that your baby's attachment determines their adult romantic
  relationships.** The lines drawn from infant classification to adult partnership
  in popular writing far exceed what the longitudinal evidence supports.
- **Not saying that returning to work or using childcare damages attachment.**
  The NICHD Early Child Care Research Network found that non-parental care in
  itself did not determine attachment security, and that family and parenting
  quality were the stronger predictors of outcomes.
- **Not saying you must respond perfectly, instantly, or every time.** The
  measured association between parental sensitivity and attachment security is
  modest, and mismatch followed by repair is the normal structure of interaction,
  not a deviation from it.
- **Not saying that babies are born moral.** The Hamlin helper and hinderer
  studies, widely reported as showing innate moral evaluation in infants, failed
  to replicate in a large multi-lab study (Lucca et al., 2025, ManyBabies, 1,018
  infants). Do not use them as evidence for anything, including anything about
  attachment or social preference.
- **Not saying that "attachment parenting" is the same thing as attachment
  security.** Attachment security is a research construct measured by trained
  coders. Attachment parenting is a commercial set of practices around carrying,
  bedsharing, and feeding. The research construct does not endorse the practice
  set, and no specific practice on that list has been shown to produce security.
- **Not saying that you can assess disorganized attachment at home.** It requires
  trained coding of a standardized procedure. Concerns go to a clinician.
- **Not saying that the first year determines everything.** Plasticity is real
  and continues well past infancy. Even the Bucharest work, at the extreme end of
  deprivation, found improvement with changed circumstances.
- **Not saying that the still-face experiment shows what checking your phone does
  to your baby.** The still-face condition is a deliberate, sustained,
  face-to-face violation, not a model of ordinary divided attention.

### Module summary

- Attachment is a safety system, not an affection score. It is what a baby does
  when frightened, hurt, or overwhelmed.
- Bowlby's two useful terms: **safe haven** (where a baby goes to be comforted)
  and **secure base** (the person a baby launches from to explore).
- Attachment is a property of a *relationship*, not a trait of a baby. The same
  baby can have different relationships with different caregivers.
- Ainsworth's Strange Situation scores the **reunion**, not the separation. How
  much a baby cries when you leave is mostly about temperament.
- Secure is the majority pattern in typical samples, but it is not a grade, and
  insecure patterns are not disorders.
- Disorganized attachment is a research classification requiring trained coders.
  It is not something to assess at home. Real worry goes to a clinician.
- The still-face effect is robust and replicated (Mesman et al., 2009), and what
  it demonstrates is how much your baby already expects interaction to be mutual.
- Tronick's under-told finding: ordinary interaction is out of sync much of the
  time, and **repair**, not accuracy, is what builds the expectation that things
  can be put right.
- Separation distress and stranger wariness around six to ten months are a
  cognitive achievement, not a regression. Your baby can now hold you in mind.
- High-quality non-parental care is compatible with secure attachment. What
  matters is the quality of the interaction, not who supplies every hour of it.
- Sensitivity predicts security at a modest effect size, around r = .24 (De Wolff
  & van IJzendoorn, 1997). It matters and it does not determine the outcome.
- Brief, focused interventions outperform long comprehensive ones
  (Bakermans-Kranenburg et al., 2003). More effort is not the mechanism.
  "Good enough" is where the evidence actually lands.
