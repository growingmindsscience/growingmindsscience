# Worksheet · Module 5 · Bodies in Motion

**What this is for:** a place to record what you see during the five activities
from Module 5, so you end the week with observations rather than a verdict.

None of this is a milestone test. Module 5 makes the case that motor development
is a particular body solving particular problems with the strength, motivation,
and surfaces available, which is why the WHO windows of achievement are wide and
why plenty of healthy babies never crawl on hands and knees at all. You are
counting practice here, not scoring performance.

---

## 1. Floor time audit

**What to do:** For two ordinary days, keep a rough tally of how much awake time
your baby spends on a flat surface (floor or firm mat, room to move) versus in a
seat, swing, bouncer, carrier, or car seat outside the car. Do not change
anything on day one. Just count.

**Write it down**

Day 1 floor time (rough): `[________]`  Container time: `[________]`

Day 2 floor time (rough): `[________]`  Container time: `[________]`

Where most of the container time comes from:
`________________________________________________`

One slot I could convert to floor time:
`________________________________________________`

**What you are looking for:** the ratio, and where the container time is coming
from. Most families find one or two specific slots, often the stretch while you
make dinner, doing most of the work.

**What it means if nothing happens:** if the ratio already looks fine to you, you
are done. There is no target number to hit. If container time is high, the fix is
not elimination, it is finding one predictable slot to convert to floor time. One
slot is a real change.

## 2. Tummy time, five short bouts

**What to do:** Instead of one long session, try five short ones spread across a
day. Thirty seconds to two minutes each. Go down to eye level with your baby, or
do it chest-to-chest with you reclined. Try after a nap, not after a big feed.

**Write it down**

Bouts today: `[ ] [ ] [ ] [ ] [ ]`   Roughly how long each: `[________]`

What I saw (tick what applies, across the week):

Head lifting `[ ]`  Weight shifting onto forearms `[ ]`  Turning to track my face
or a sound `[ ]`  Pushing up `[ ]`

Tolerance across the week (not within a session):
`________________________________________________`

What helped (eye level, chest-to-chest, rolled towel, timing):
`________________________________________________`

**What you are looking for:** head lifting, weight shifting onto the forearms,
turning to track your face or a sound, and pushing up. Also watch tolerance
across the week rather than within a session.

**What it means if nothing happens:** if your baby protests every time, pick them
up and try again later. Protest is not a sign you have done it wrong. If after a
couple of weeks of short bouts your baby still cannot lift and hold their head at
all in a position where you would expect it, or if their head control seems to be
going backwards, that is worth mentioning to your pediatrician rather than
working around.

---

## 3. Motivate a reach, do not deliver it

**What to do:** Put something genuinely interesting just outside your baby's
current reach: a toy, a safe household object, your own face. Then wait. Give it
several seconds of silence rather than narrating or moving the object closer.

**Write it down**

What I offered: `________________________________________________`

The attempt I saw (arm launching, shoulder rotating, body leaning, leg pushing,
roll initiated): `________________________________________________`

Which hand or hands: Left `[ ]`  Right `[ ]`  Both, across tries `[ ]`

**What you are looking for:** the attempt. The attempt is the whole point, not
the acquisition.

**What it means if nothing happens:** if your baby loses interest, the object was
not interesting enough or was too far. Move it slightly closer and try again, or
use your face, which usually outperforms toys. If your baby consistently reaches
with only one hand and never the other, note that specifically and mention it at
your next visit. Strong, persistent one-sidedness is one of the few motor
observations worth flagging early.

## 4. Contingency check

**What to do:** Find something your baby can cause: a rattle they hold, a crinkle
book, a toy that moves when kicked, or simply you responding every single time
they vocalize. Do the same thing in the same spot for a few days running.

**Write it down**

The setup: `________________________________________________`

Same spot each day? Yes `[ ]`  No `[ ]`

Day 1 rate compared to their usual: Lower `[ ]` Same `[ ]` Higher `[ ]`

Day 3 rate compared to their usual: Lower `[ ]` Same `[ ]` Higher `[ ]`

Did it come back faster on day three than day one?
`________________________________________________`

**What you are looking for:** escalation. Does the kicking or batting increase
above their usual rate once they work out the connection? That is the
Rovee-Collier effect in your living room.

**What it means if nothing happens:** very young babies need many repetitions and
the same context each time, so try holding the setting constant for several days
before concluding anything. If your baby seems not to respond to your responses
at all, across contexts and over weeks, that is worth raising with your
pediatrician. It is much more often a matter of state, timing, and difficulty
than a signal of anything.

## 5. Write your own sleep question down

**What to do:** Before your next pediatric visit, write two sentences: what your
baby's sleep actually looks like over a typical week, and what specifically is
not working for you. Not what you think you are supposed to want. What is not
working.

**Write it down**

Sentence one, what a typical week actually looks like:
`________________________________________________`

`________________________________________________`

Sentence two, what specifically is not working for me:
`________________________________________________`

`________________________________________________`

Is this a problem to solve, or a normal pattern I have been told to be ashamed
of? `________________________________________________`

**What you are looking for:** clarity about whether you have a problem to solve
or a normal pattern. Night waking at six to twelve months is common and normal.
If it is not costing you anything, there is nothing to fix.

**What it means if nothing happens:** if writing it down reveals that everyone is
coping, that is a real result and you can stop reading sleep content. If it
reveals that you are not coping, that is a legitimate medical conversation to
have, and exhaustion is a good enough reason on its own to ask for help.

**Safe sleep does not change, whatever you decide.** From the AAP 2022
recommendations, as stated in Module 5: back to sleep for every sleep, naps
included, for the whole first year. Firm, flat, separate sleep surface designed
for infant sleep, in a crib, bassinet, or play yard meeting current safety
standards. No pillows, no loose blankets, no bumpers, no positioners, no
inclined sleepers, no stuffed animals. Room-share without bed-sharing, ideally
for at least the first six months. Avoid smoke exposure. Offer a pacifier at
sleep onset once feeding is going well. Car seats and infant seats are not sleep
surfaces, so move your baby to a firm flat surface on their back when you get
home. If you are feeding at night and exhausted, a chair or bed cleared of
pillows and blankets is safer than a sofa or armchair, because falling asleep
with a baby on a sofa or armchair carries a particularly high risk.

## One thing to put down this week

Module 5 spends most of its length dismantling the idea that the first year is a
ladder with month numbers stamped under each step. Newly walking babies fall
dozens of times a day and the falling is the practice. The WHO windows are wide
because healthy variation is wide. Sleep and feeding, past a short list of safety
items, are mostly not the variable.

A note on the limits of this page: wide normal variation is not the same as never
worry. Loss of a motor skill your baby previously had, persistent stiffness or
persistent floppiness, or strong asymmetry in how your baby moves are reasons to
see a clinician rather than reasons to wait.
