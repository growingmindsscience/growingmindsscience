# Worksheet · Module 6 · Everyday Life

**What this is for:** somewhere to write down what you notice during the five
activities from Module 6, which are all about the ordinary container the rest of
the course happens inside: play, attention, routines, and you.

There is no new technique in this module and there is none on this page. The
point of Module 6 is that the things you are already doing, badly and
inconsistently and while tired, are the mechanism. Treat everything below as
watching, not testing.

---

## 1. The kitchen drawer swap

**What to do:** Put the battery-operated toys away for a few days. Offer, on the
floor, a small collection of ordinary household objects: nesting measuring cups,
a wooden spoon, a silicone spatula, a scarf, a clean empty tub with a lid, a
ball, a metal bowl. Sit nearby and let your baby lead. Do not demonstrate unless
they get stuck and frustrated.

**Write it down**

Objects I offered: `________________________________________________`

The one they stayed with longest: `________________________________________________`

Repeated variations I saw (banging on two surfaces, dropping from two heights, in
and out of a container): `________________________________________________`

Glances up at me: None `[ ]`  A few `[ ]`  Often `[ ]`

**What you are looking for:** longer engagement with one object than you
expected, repeated variations on a single action, and occasional glances up at
you.

**What it means if nothing happens:** if your baby loses interest fast, that is
about age and state, not about the objects. Very young babies do not sustain
object play for long, and a tired or hungry baby will not engage with anything.
Try again on a different day, at a different point in the wake window. It is also
fine if your baby simply prefers one object and ignores the rest. That is a
preference, not a failure of the activity.

## 2. The gentle hide

**What to do:** With a baby somewhere past about six months who is sitting
reasonably steadily, partly cover a toy they are interested in with a cloth,
leaving a bit visible. See what happens. If they retrieve it, try covering it
fully next time. If you like, and only in a light and unserious way, hide it
under cloth A twice, then move it in full view to cloth B and see where they
reach.

**Write it down**

Partly covered: did they pull the cloth off? Yes `[ ]`  No `[ ]`

Fully covered: did they search? Yes `[ ]`  No `[ ]`  Did not try `[ ]`

If I tried the A and B version, they reached toward: A `[ ]`  B `[ ]`  Neither `[ ]`

Did they stay interested, or was it time to stop?
`________________________________________________`

**What you are looking for:** whether they pull the cloth off, and if you tried
the A and B version, whether they reach to the old location.

**What it means if nothing happens:** a baby who does not search for a fully
hidden object is showing you where their working memory currently is, which
changes over months, not days. A baby who reaches back to A is doing the textbook
thing, and per Diamond's account it reflects memory load and motor habit, not a
missing understanding of objects. Neither result is a test outcome. Do not repeat
this to get a better score, and stop the moment it stops being fun for them.

## 3. Follow their attention, not yours

**What to do:** For a few minutes a day, deliberately reverse the usual
direction. Instead of showing your baby things, watch what they are already
looking at and comment on that. "You found the light." "That's the dog." If they
look at something across the room, look at it too, then look back at them.

**Write it down**

What they were already interested in:
`________________________________________________`

What I said about it: `________________________________________________`

Did they check back to my face? Yes `[ ]`  No `[ ]`  Not sure `[ ]`

Later in the year, tick anything you happen to see. This is a note of what turned
up, not a checklist to complete:

Looks where I look `[ ]`   Follows a point `[ ]`   Points themselves `[ ]`
Shows me things `[ ]`   Hands me things `[ ]`   Checks my reaction afterward `[ ]`

**What you are looking for:** whether they check back to your face. Later in the
year, whether they look where you look, and toward the end of the year whether
they start pointing, showing, or handing you things, and whether they check your
reaction after doing it.

**What it means if nothing happens:** gaze following and pointing arrive across a
wide window, and plenty of babies are late to point and entirely typical. Nothing
here is a screen and a single session shows you nothing. If, closer to fifteen to
eighteen months, you are not seeing pointing, showing, or your baby checking your
face when something surprising happens, that is a conversation to have with your
pediatrician, calmly and early.

## 4. One anchored sequence

**What to do:** Pick one part of the day and make its order reliable, without
making its clock time reliable. Bedtime is the usual choice, but the pre-nap
wind-down or the arrival-home sequence works just as well. Same steps, same
order, same tone, whenever it happens.

**Write it down**

The sequence I picked: `________________________________________________`

Steps, in order:
1. `________________________________________________`
2. `________________________________________________`
3. `________________________________________________`
4. `________________________________________________`

Nights or days it happened in the usual order: `[ ] [ ] [ ] [ ] [ ] [ ] [ ]`

Any sign of anticipation (settling earlier in the sequence, recognising the next
step, less resistance at transitions):
`________________________________________________`

Anything that disrupted the week (illness, travel, teething, a leap):
`________________________________________________`

**What you are looking for:** over a week or two, signs of anticipation.

**What it means if nothing happens:** some babies show anticipation clearly,
others do not show it in any way you can see, and a fortnight is a short window.
Also, if the week involved illness, travel, teething, or a developmental leap,
expect the pattern to vanish and return later. The sequence is not being graded
and neither are you. If you miss it entirely for three nights, it is not undone.

## 5. One honest ask

**What to do:** Ask one person for one specific, concrete thing this week. Not
"let me know if you need anything," which is not an ask. Something with edges:
two hours on Saturday morning, one shop run, one load of laundry, one phone call
where you get to talk and they mostly listen. If your honest answer is that there
is no one to ask, then make the ask a professional one: book the appointment you
have been putting off, or raise it at your baby's next pediatric visit.

**Write it down**

Who I asked: `________________________________________________`

What exactly I asked for: `________________________________________________`

Did I say it out loud? Yes `[ ]`  No `[ ]`  Nearly `[ ]`

If there was no one to ask, the professional version I chose:
`________________________________________________`

**What you are looking for:** whether you can actually say it out loud. That is
the entire exercise. The help is a bonus.

**What it means if nothing happens:** if you could not do it, that is worth
noticing rather than criticising yourself for. Difficulty asking for help is
extremely common in new parents and it is more common when someone is depressed
or anxious, which is precisely the situation where it matters most. If this one
was the hardest item on this list, please read Lesson 6.5 again and consider that
a reason to talk to a clinician rather than a reason to try harder alone.

**Signposting, without euphemism, as Module 6 states it.** If you are having
thoughts of harming yourself, or thoughts of harming your baby, that is not a
thing to sit with alone and it is not evidence that you are dangerous or a bad
parent. Intrusive, unwanted, frightening thoughts about harm coming to the baby
are extremely common in the postpartum period and are usually the opposite of
intent, but they are still a reason to tell someone, because the right support
exists and is effective. Contact your doctor or midwife, or go to an emergency
department. In the US, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline is available by call
or text, at any hour, and it is an appropriate route for a parent in crisis. If
you are outside the US, your local emergency number and your health service's
crisis line serve the same function. If you are ever afraid you might act, treat
it as urgent and get to emergency care.

smaller: your baby does something, you notice and respond, they notice that you
noticed, repeated across thousands of ordinary moments nobody remembers.
