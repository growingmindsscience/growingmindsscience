# Quizzes

Six short quizzes, one per module. They are not tests. Nothing here is scored,
nothing is reported, and there is no pass mark.

The point of these is calibration, not recall. You will not be asked for a
number, a date, or a researcher's name, because knowing those would not help you
at three in the morning. You will be asked whether you can apply the idea, and
several questions have "the evidence does not settle this" as a genuinely correct
answer, because that is often the honest state of the field and recognising it is
a real skill.

Most of the wrong answers are things you have probably been told by someone
confident. That is deliberate.

---

## Quiz 1 · What Is Actually Being Built

Covers: what your newborn arrived able to do, how experience becomes brain
structure, contingency, and what sensitive-period research does and does not
license.

**1.1** Newborn vision is poor by adult standards, and clearest at close range.
What is the most accurate way to think about that?

- A. A limitation to be corrected with high-contrast cards and visual stimulation
- B. A filter that delivers a narrow, high-value slice of the world, roughly the
  distance to the face of whoever is holding them
- C. Evidence that the newborn brain is mostly empty and waiting to be filled
- D. A sign that the first weeks are the least developmentally useful part of the
  year

**1.2** Your baby coos. You look at them, say something back, and wait. What is
the ingredient in that exchange that the evidence points to?

- A. The number of words you used
- B. The richness of your vocabulary
- C. Contingency: your response depended on what they just did, closely enough in
  time that they could notice
- D. Sustained eye contact for as long as possible

**1.3** Your three-month-old is staring at the ceiling fan. You have five
minutes. Which would you do?

- A. Move into their line of sight with a black and white card, since that is
  what the visual system needs
- B. Talk about the fan, since that is what has their attention
- C. Put on classical music so the time is not wasted
- D. Leave them to it, since interrupting a baby's focus is harmful

**1.4** Sensitive-period research, including the visual deprivation work that won
a Nobel Prize, is often quoted to parents. What does it actually establish?

- A. That extra early stimulation builds a stronger brain
- B. That specific systems need basic, ordinary input to be present at all during
  a particular period, and that its absence is not fully recoverable later
- C. That cognitive and emotional development have a deadline at age three
- D. That the more input a baby receives in the first year, the better the
  outcome

**1.5** Studies find that caregivers who respond more to their infants tend to
have children who reach language milestones earlier. What can you correctly
conclude from that?

- A. Responding to your baby causes them to talk sooner
- B. Parents who respond less are holding their children back
- C. The two things travel together, but the design cannot establish that one
  caused the other
- D. Nothing at all, since correlational findings are worthless

**1.6** You had a bad week. You were short, distracted, and missed a lot. What
does the evidence say about that?

- A. Missed weeks accumulate into measurable deficits
- B. It can be offset by extra enrichment activities afterwards
- C. Mismatch followed by coming back is the normal structure of interaction, not
  a deviation from it, and no single interaction is load bearing
- D. The first three years are the window, so lost time in them is lost

### Answers and why

**1.1 · B.** A is the appealing answer because "poor vision" sounds like a
problem, and there is a whole product category built on solving it. But the
sequence is a curriculum, not a defect. A newborn who could see the whole room in
sharp detail would be flooded, and instead the system delivers exactly the most
informative object available, which is a human face at holding distance. Acuity,
colour, and depth all arrive over the following months without any help from you.

**1.2 · C.** B and A feel right because so much parenting advice is about
language volume, and volume is easy to imagine increasing. But the finding that
does the work is contingency: the baby produces a signal, and something reliably
and promptly happens because of it. That is how a baby learns they are an agent
whose actions change the world, and it is also why background talk and audio in
the room do not substitute. Nothing you say to a screen changes what it does next.

**1.3 · B.** A is tempting because it feels like doing something developmental,
and D is tempting because "follow the baby" can be over-read into never
interacting. Following beats directing: when you name the thing they already
selected, the word arrives attached to a meaning they already have. When you
redirect them to your topic, you have to win the attention first and the word
lands on nothing. C is not harmful, it is just not doing the thing the option
implies.

**1.4 · B.** A and D are the versions that get quoted at parents, and they are
where most of the pressure comes from. Every one of those findings concerns
whether basic, universally available input was present at all. None of them
concerns whether input was optimised or maximised. Sensitive period research is
about floors, not ceilings, and the collapse of that distinction is the single
most common misuse in this literature.

**1.5 · C.** A is the sentence almost every parenting article writes, and it is
the one the study cannot support. Responsive caregivers differ from less
responsive caregivers in many ways at once, including circumstances, available
time, stress load, mental health, and characteristics they share with their
child. D overcorrects: the finding is real and useful as a description of what
tends to travel together, and it converges with experimental work on caregiver
sensitivity. It is just not an instruction with a guaranteed output.

**1.6 · C.** D is the claim you have heard most often and it is an overclaim; A
sounds like scientific caution but describes a mechanism nobody has demonstrated.
A baby has thousands of interactions a week and the system is built with
redundancy. What the research on caregiver and infant interaction repeatedly
finds is that pairs are out of sync a great deal of the time and that the return
to connection is itself part of the process. B is not needed, because there is
nothing to offset.

## Quiz 2 · Reading Your Baby

Covers: behavioral states, engagement and disengagement cues, crying, and
temperament.

**2.1** A settling technique worked beautifully yesterday and does nothing today.
What is the most likely explanation?

- A. Your baby has adapted to it and it has stopped working
- B. Your baby is in a different state, and the technique matched the state rather
  than the baby
- C. You did it slightly wrong the second time
- D. The technique was never effective and yesterday was luck

**2.2** You are face to face with your baby and they turn their head away. What
would you do?

- A. Move into their sightline and get a bit more animated to win them back
- B. Stop adding input, lean back slightly, keep your hands supporting them, and
  wait
- C. End the interaction immediately and put them down
- D. Nothing differently, since gaze aversion at this age is random

**2.3** What does the best current evidence say about the shape of crying across
the first months?

- A. It rises to a reliable peak at around six weeks in every baby, then falls
- B. It is high in the early weeks and declines clearly by around three months,
  and a universal peak is not something the data support
- C. It is flat across the first year and varies only by parenting
- D. It is unpredictable and nothing general can be said about it

**2.4** Someone tells you that picking your baby up whenever they cry will teach
them to cry more. What is the accurate response?

- A. They are right, and you should stretch out your response times
- B. They are right for babies over four months
- C. There is no support for the claim that comforting a young baby promptly
  produces more crying or more dependence
- D. Responding quickly has been proven to reduce later crying

**2.5** It is the middle of the night, nothing has worked for forty minutes, and
you feel something rise in you that frightens you. Which is the right move?

- A. Keep holding your baby and keep trying, since putting them down would be
  abandoning them
- B. Put your baby on their back in an empty crib or bassinet, leave the room,
  close the door, and take five or ten minutes
- C. Wake someone else and hand the baby over, but only if you are truly at the
  limit
- D. Drive around with them until they settle

**2.6** Your baby is intense, reacts strongly, and takes a long time to adjust to
anything new. What does the temperament research suggest you do with that
information?

- A. Work on making them less intense
- B. Accept that their trajectory is largely set and adjust your expectations for
  who they will become
- C. Adjust the conditions to fit this particular baby: fewer transitions, more
  warning, longer runway, more predictable rhythms
- D. Nothing, since temperament labels have no practical use

### Answers and why

**2.1 · B.** A and C are the natural readings because they put the variable in
the technique or in you, which is where we usually look. But your baby is not
simply awake or asleep. They move through distinct behavioral states, and the
state determines what they can do and what will help. Bouncing organises a baby
in active alert and wakes a baby in quiet sleep. Same action, opposite result.

**2.2 · B.** A is what almost everyone does, especially a parent already worried
they are not connecting, and it is the single most common misread in early
parenting. Faces are high-arousal input, and a baby who cannot yet turn their own
arousal down has one good tool available: break the visual connection. Working
harder raises the input at the exact moment they were trying to lower it, and
then they have to escalate to get the break they asked for politely. C is
sometimes right, but give them the twenty seconds first, because many babies turn
back on their own.

**2.3 · B.** A is the version most parents are handed, and the decline it
describes is real and worth knowing. The largest pooled analysis of parent diary
data, however, reports no statistical evidence for a universal crying peak: the
durations across the first six weeks were not statistically distinguishable from
one another. The decline by around three months is the solid finding, and it is
also the one that helps you. Whether your particular baby has a dramatic peak or
just a long noisy stretch that fades is not something the evidence pins down, and
you do not need it to.

**2.4 · C.** D is tempting because it is the reassuring mirror image, and there
is a classic study in that direction, but it has been challenged and the design
cannot separate response speed from everything else that differs between
families. C is the honest and sufficient version. An infant in the first months
does not have the machinery to form a manipulative strategy; they have a distress
signal and no alternative. Respond on the timeline that works for your family
without carrying a fear of doing damage by comfort.

**2.5 · B.** A is what guilt tells you, and it is the dangerous answer. A baby
crying alone on their back in a safe, empty sleep space for ten minutes is not
being harmed, at all, and stepping away is a good decision you should make
without waiting until you are certain you need to. C is fine if someone is there,
and a fresh adult often gets a different result, which is not evidence you were
doing it wrong. Never shake a baby, not once, not gently, not to get their
attention, and tell every person who cares for your baby that stepping away is
always allowed and nobody gets judged for it.

**2.6 · C.** A is the instinct and it aims at the one thing that is not very
movable. B overreads the research in the other direction: early reactivity shifts
the odds of later cautiousness, it does not set the outcome, and most highly
reactive infants do not become inhibited adults. The idea worth keeping is
goodness of fit. Outcomes follow from the match between a particular baby and a
particular set of conditions, which means the useful question is almost never
what is wrong with this baby, or what am I doing wrong, but what needs adjusting
so these two people fit together better.

## Quiz 3 · The Language-Ready Baby

Covers: what babies hear, how babbling works, what tracks later language, and
what has been tested and did not deliver.

**3.1** You have the radio on and you are chatting to another adult while your
baby is in the room. Later you spend ten minutes talking to your baby directly
during a nappy change. What does the evidence suggest about those two stretches?

- A. All language in the environment counts equally, so the radio hours matter
  more by sheer volume
- B. Speech aimed at your baby is what tracks later processing and vocabulary;
  speech merely audible in the room does not
- C. Overheard adult conversation is the richer input because the grammar is more
  complex
- D. Neither matters before six months

**3.2** In one study, nine-month-olds heard a language they had never
encountered, either from a live person or through identical audio and video
recordings. What happened?

- A. All three conditions produced the same learning
- B. The recordings worked better because they were perfectly consistent
- C. The infants learned the new speech contrasts from the live sessions and
  learned nothing from the identical audio or video
- D. None of the conditions produced any learning

**3.3** A program offers to teach you baby sign language, promising it will get
your baby talking sooner. What does the trial evidence show?

- A. Signing produces a substantial speech advantage
- B. A randomized trial found no language advantage from gestural training
- C. Signing delays speech
- D. It has never been tested

**3.4** You say something to your baby and they are quiet. What is the most
useful thing to do next?

- A. Say something else, so the input keeps flowing
- B. Repeat yourself more slowly and loudly
- C. Stop, keep looking at them, keep your face open, and silently count to three
- D. Try a different word, since that one clearly did not land

**3.5** How many words a day should you aim to say to your baby?

- A. About 30,000
- B. As many as possible, since volume is the mechanism
- C. There is no number, and word count is not the target
- D. It depends on your baby's age in months

**3.6** Which of these is actually worth raising with your pediatrician?

- A. Your baby is quieter than your sister's baby was
- B. Your baby babbles less on days when they are tired
- C. Your baby was babbling and responding to their name, and has stopped doing
  both
- D. Your baby prefers babbling to your partner rather than to you

### Answers and why

**3.1 · B.** A has an intuitive logic, which is that language is language and
more of it must be better. But the daylong-recording work separates speech
directed at the child from speech merely present in the environment, and it is
the directed portion that relates to how quickly a child later identifies a
spoken word and to vocabulary. Ten minutes of someone talking to your actual baby
is worth more than three hours of language in the air. Note that this study is
correlational, so it is a reason to aim your speech when you are already talking,
not a reason to believe you can dose your way to a vocabulary.

**3.2 · C.** B is the intuition behind every screen-based language product: a
recording is consistent, tireless, and repeatable. What it cannot be is
contingent. It does not follow your baby's gaze, wait for their turn, or answer
the sound they just made, and in that study the social channel turned out to be
the condition for learning rather than a pleasant addition to it. This is the
same mechanism that explains why educational baby videos, when actually tested,
produced no word learning.

**3.3 · B.** A is the marketing claim and it is widely believed, partly because
gesturing with a baby genuinely is enjoyable and pointing and reaching really are
important parts of communication. The randomized trial compared infants whose
caregivers were trained in symbolic gesturing against infants whose caregivers
were trained to use verbal labels equivalently, plus a control, and the
gesture-trained infants showed no language advantage. Sign with your baby if you
like it. Do not pay for it as an accelerator.

**3.4 · C.** A is what nearly all of us do, because an adult-length silence feels
like a failed exchange. Baby response times are much slower than adult ones, and
adults routinely fill the gap before the baby has finished processing, which ends
the rally by accident. Leave a genuinely uncomfortable pause. Anything that fills
the slot counts: a coo, a syllable, a mouth opening, a kick, an eyebrow.

**3.5 · C.** A is the most repeated number in early childhood policy and it comes
from a study that rested on a very small number of families and counted only
speech directed at the child by a primary caregiver at home. An attempted
replication across five communities did not reproduce it, and the method
systematically undercounts environments where many adults and older children
talk. B is the same error in gentler clothing. Contingency and direction are what
the evidence points at; volume is what got measured because it was easy to
measure.

**3.6 · C.** A, B, and D are all ordinary variation, and A in particular is the
comparison that keeps parents awake for no reason: vocal output varies hugely and
much of that variation is temperament. Loss of a skill that was previously
present is different, and it is the one item worth an appointment rather than a
mention at the next well visit. Also worth raising: no consonant-vowel babbling
such as "ba" or "da" by around nine to twelve months, and any apparent lack of
response to sound at any age. You are allowed to ask without a strong case.

## Quiz 4 · Connection and Security

Covers: what attachment means in the research, what the still-face procedure
shows, repair, separation distress, and non-parental care.

**4.1** In the research literature, what is attachment?

- A. A measure of how much a baby loves a caregiver
- B. A fixed trait a baby has, like eye colour
- C. The system a baby uses to get protection from a particular adult when
  frightened, hurt, sick, or overwhelmed
- D. A parenting philosophy involving carrying, bedsharing, and feeding choices

**4.2** The Strange Situation is the laboratory procedure attachment research is
built on. Which part of it carries the information?

- A. How upset the baby gets when the caregiver leaves
- B. What the baby does when the caregiver comes back
- C. How the baby responds to the stranger
- D. How long the baby plays independently

**4.3** You have seen the still-face video, usually captioned as a warning about
checking your phone. What does the procedure actually demonstrate?

- A. That divided parental attention distresses babies in the way the video
  implies
- B. That babies in the first months already expect interaction to be mutual and
  contingent, and detect and protest a break in it
- C. That babies cannot tolerate any period without adult attention
- D. That eye contact is the main channel of infant development

**4.4** Over five minutes of face-to-face play, you lean in as your baby looks
away, you offer a rattle when they wanted your face, and twice you misread them
entirely. Then each time one of you adjusts and you come back into sync. What is
that?

- A. A below-average interaction that you should try to make smoother
- B. Evidence you are missing cues and should slow down
- C. The normal structure of parent and infant interaction, in which repair rather
  than accuracy is the part that does the work
- D. Neutral, since only the total amount of interaction time matters

**4.5** Your eight-month-old, who used to be handed happily around family
gatherings, now stiffens at an aunt and protests the moment you leave the room.
What is happening?

- A. A regression, probably caused by a recent change in routine
- B. A new ability arriving disguised as a problem: they can now reliably tell
  familiar from unfamiliar and hold you in mind when you are gone
- C. A sign the attachment relationship is insecure
- D. A phase that should be discouraged so it does not become a habit

**4.6** What does the large longitudinal research on childcare and attachment
support?

- A. Non-parental care in itself reduces attachment security
- B. Non-parental care in itself did not determine attachment security, and family
  and parenting quality were the stronger predictors, though the design is
  correlational
- C. Childcare improves attachment security
- D. The question has never been studied at scale

### Answers and why

**4.1 · C.** A is what the word means in ordinary conversation, which is exactly
why the term has been stretched past usefulness. Attachment is a safety system,
not a love meter, and B misses a second point worth holding onto: it is a
property of a relationship, not a trait of a baby. The same baby can have
different relationships with different caregivers, so there is no such thing as
an insecure baby. D shares a word with the research construct and very little
else.

**4.2 · B.** A is what everyone assumes, and it is the assumption that makes
parents watch their own baby's goodbyes anxiously. How much a baby cries at
separation tells you more about temperament than about the relationship. The
reunion is what gets scored: whether the baby seeks the caregiver, whether
contact actually settles them, and whether they can then go back to playing. It
is also worth saying plainly that this is a standardised laboratory procedure
scored by trained coders against a manual, and it is not something to run or
interpret at home.

**4.3 · B.** A is how the clip is almost always presented, and it is not a fair
reading. The still-face condition is deliberately strange: a parent physically
present, close, staring directly at their baby, giving nothing at all. That is a
violation, not a simulation of ordinary divided attention. What the babies'
protest reveals is how sophisticated they already are, which is a statement about
your baby rather than an indictment of you.

**4.4 · C.** A and B are what most parents conclude, because if attunement were
about accuracy then parenting would be a test you fail hundreds of times a day.
Frame-by-frame study of ordinary interaction finds pairs out of sync a great deal
of the time, with repair happening constantly. What your baby accumulates is not
"this person always reads me correctly" but "when this goes wrong, it gets
fixed," which is a considerably more useful thing to carry through a life. The
moments you feel worst about are not deposits in a deficit account; what matters
is that you come back.

**4.5 · B.** A is what someone will tell you, and C is the fear underneath the
question. Three things are converging: your baby can now hold a stable
representation of known faces and compare new ones against it, their memory for
people has improved enough that you continue to exist when you are elsewhere, and
most babies become mobile around this stretch, which creates a real need for a
system that tracks how far away you are. Separation protest is the attachment
relationship becoming observable. The range is wide in both directions, and
little or no stranger wariness is not a warning sign either.

**4.6 · B.** A is the version that has been used to make working parents feel
guilty for decades. The large American longitudinal study found that routine
non-parental care did not in itself determine attachment security, and that
family and parenting variables were the stronger predictors of outcomes. Risk
accumulated in combination, where low parental sensitivity co-occurred with
poor-quality or unstable care, which is a statement about compounding
disadvantage rather than about the decision to work. The design is correlational
and country-specific, which is why B names that limit rather than hiding it.

## Quiz 5 · Bodies in Motion

Covers: motor milestones and their real width, safe sleep, sleep at nine months,
and how far the feeding evidence actually reaches.

**5.1** Your baby is nine months old and has never crawled on hands and knees.
They scoot, they roll with purpose, and they are pulling up. Someone tells you
that skipping crawling causes later reading and coordination problems. What is
true?

- A. They are right, and remedial crawling programs address it
- B. There is no good evidence for that claim, and a substantial minority of
  healthy children never crawl on hands and knees at all
- C. It is true only if the baby also skips cruising
- D. The evidence is too thin to say anything either way

**5.2** Babies who slept on their front used to reach some early motor milestones
slightly sooner on average. How should that finding be read?

- A. Back sleeping holds development back and the guidance is a compromise
- B. The finding is not real and should be ignored
- C. It is a difference in the timing of some early milestones, not a difference
  in developmental outcome, and it does not come close to changing the safe sleep
  recommendation
- D. It means tummy sleeping is fine once a baby can roll

**5.3** Your newly walking baby falls over dozens of times a day. What are you
watching?

- A. A coordination problem worth mentioning at the next visit
- B. An unusually large number of falls
- C. The ordinary texture of learning to walk, in which enormous volumes of
  imperfect practice are the mechanism
- D. A sign they started walking too early

**5.4** Your nine-month-old wakes twice most nights. Everyone is coping, more or
less. What does the evidence support?

- A. Night waking at this age indicates a sleep problem that needs correcting
- B. Night waking at six to twelve months is common in typically developing
  infants, and if it is not costing you anything there is nothing to fix
- C. You should sleep train, because the trials show it is required
- D. You should never sleep train, because the trials show harm

**5.5** You are considering a behavioral sleep intervention. What is the honest
summary of the evidence?

- A. It is proven safe
- B. It is proven harmful to attachment
- C. It works in the short term for the outcomes it targets, the trials that
  measured stress physiology and attachment did not detect adverse effects
  although they were small, and long-term follow-up shows neither lasting harm
  nor lasting benefit
- D. There is no evidence in any direction

**5.6** A trial randomized high-risk infants, meaning infants with severe eczema,
egg allergy, or both, to eat peanut or avoid it, and found a large reduction in
peanut allergy in the eating group. What does that license?

- A. Introduce all allergenic foods early to prevent allergies generally
- B. It is strong evidence about peanut in high-risk infants specifically, it does
  not generalize to allergenic foods as a class, and if your baby is high risk
  this is a conversation to have with your pediatrician before starting solids
- C. Nothing, because it was a single trial
- D. Delay allergenic foods, since the older advice was safer

### Answers and why

**5.1 · B.** A circulates widely and is often attached to programs selling
remediation, which is a good reason to be suspicious of it. The internationally
standardized reference study of infant motor development treated hands-and-knees
crawling differently from the other five gross motor milestones precisely because
it is not universal: a small but real percentage of the healthy children in it
were never observed to crawl that way, and crawling was also the only one of the
six that did not hold a fixed place in the sequence. D is the wrong kind of
humility here. This is not an open question so much as a claim that was never
established.

**5.2 · C.** A and B are the two dishonest readings, and both are common. The
finding is real: prone position gives babies practice, and practice matters. But
what was found was a difference in when some early milestones appeared, not a
difference in developmental outcome, and all the infants were within normal
ranges. Set a few weeks of timing against a large, replicated reduction in infant
deaths and it is not close. Back to sleep, every sleep, all year. The answer to
the practice question is awake, supervised tummy time.

**5.3 · C.** A is what the sheer quantity suggests, and it is worth knowing how
far off it is. Infants learning to walk take thousands of steps in an hour of
free play, travel remarkable distances across a day, and fall dozens of times
daily, and the falls do not predict anything bad. You are not watching failure
with occasional success mixed in. The imperfection is the mechanism.

**5.4 · B.** A is the assumption baked into most sleep content, and it makes a
normal pattern feel like a fault. Sleep consolidation is gradual, its trajectory
varies a lot between babies, and a large share of babies who had started sleeping
through go back to waking. It is also worth knowing that "sleeping through the
night" is defined differently in nearly every study you will encounter, so a
headline percentage means very little without its definition. If nothing is
costing you anything, you can stop reading sleep content.

**5.5 · C.** A is what supporters say and B is what opponents say, and neither is
what the trials show. The randomized trial that measured infant cortisol and
attachment at follow-up found no adverse effects, but it randomized only around
fourteen families per arm, which makes it absence of detected harm rather than
demonstrated safety. The five-year follow-up of a larger trial was null on child
outcomes in both directions, and the maternal mood benefit seen earlier had not
persisted. That combination makes this a values decision rather than a medical
requirement, and safe sleep practice does not change either way.

**5.6 · B.** A is the sentence this trial is most often used to support, and it
goes well past what was tested. The trial tested peanut, not allergenic foods as
a class, and egg trials have produced a genuinely mixed picture rather than a
matching result. It also enrolled high-risk infants only, so it does not measure
what happens in a general-population baby. What is clear is that delaying
allergenic foods is not protective, which is why D is wrong, and that peanut
introduction for high-risk infants is now actively recommended on a timeline your
pediatrician should set.

> **SCOPE FLAG:** No question was written on tummy time dose. The direction of
> the advice is sound and low risk, but the widely repeated hourly target is
> consensus guidance rather than a trial-derived threshold, and any question with
> a clean correct answer would have implied a precision the evidence does not
> have.

> **SCOPE FLAG:** No question was written on breastfeeding and cognitive
> outcomes. The honest answer is that the evidence does not settle it, which is a
> legitimate correct option elsewhere in these quizzes, but here every plausible
> distractor set risked reading as a verdict on a feeding decision the parent has
> already made and often could not freely choose. The module states the position
> in prose and that is the better place for it.

## Quiz 6 · Everyday Life

Covers: play, joint attention, routines and stress, screens, and the caregiver as
part of the system.

**6.1** You have a budget for one thing. Which is most likely to be developmentally
useful to a nine-month-old?

- A. A toy that lights up, plays a tune, and says a number when a button is
  pressed
- B. A subscription box of stage-matched developmental toys
- C. Nesting cups, a wooden spoon, a scarf, a cardboard box, and a clean empty tub
  with a lid
- D. An educational app designed for infants

**6.2** You hide a toy under cloth A a few times, your baby finds it each time,
and then you move it to cloth B while they watch. They reach for A. What does
that show?

- A. They do not yet understand that objects continue to exist out of sight
- B. A collision between a new location held in working memory and a motor habit
  that has already been rewarded, both of which are maturing fast in the second
  half of the year
- C. A memory problem worth mentioning to your pediatrician
- D. That they were not paying attention when you moved it

**6.6** You have been flat, anxious, and not yourself since your baby arrived.
What follows from the evidence in this course?

- A. Get through it, since your baby needs you present more than well
- B. Read more about infant development so you can compensate
- C. Perinatal depression and anxiety are common and highly treatable, and getting
  help acts directly on the input that matters most, which makes asking a
  caregiving act rather than a detour from one
- D. The damage is likely already done, so focus on the years ahead

### Answers and why

**6.1 · C.** A, B, and D are not harmful and babies do enjoy them, so nothing
here is a reason for guilt if they are already in your house. But the
developmental value of a toy is roughly inverse to how much it does on its own. A
toy that lights up on a button press has already made every interesting decision;
the baby's contribution is the button. Objects that do nothing until the baby
acts on them, and then do exactly what the action makes them do, supply the
contingency that is worth having, and most of them are already in your kitchen.

**6.2 · B.** A is the classic interpretation and it was the textbook story for
decades, which is why it still circulates. The modern account is more forgiving:
the baby has to hold a new location in mind across a delay while inhibiting a
reach that has already worked several times. Lengthen the delay and errors go up;
shorten it and they go down. You are not watching a conceptual gap. You are
watching a young braking system.

**6.3 · C.** B is what anxiety suggests, and it does not work: a baby who is
pointed at repeatedly by a worried adult does not develop joint attention faster.
There is nothing to drill. What supports it is responding when your baby looks at
something, following their gaze rather than always redirecting to yours, and
reacting with warmth when they show you something. The fluff is not interesting.
The offer is. Note also that joint attention is a thing to understand and enjoy,
not a checklist to administer to your own baby; timelines vary widely and a baby
not pointing at twelve months is not thereby a baby with a problem.

**6.4 · C.** A is the natural assumption, and it is why the term has been applied
to ordinary hard days in a way its own authors do not support. Toxic stress in
the framework means strong, frequent, or prolonged activation of the stress
response in the absence of adequate adult buffering, and the absence is the
operative clause. Your bad day is not toxic stress. Neither is crying in the car
seat, or daycare, or putting the baby down safely and going to breathe in the
hallway, which is if anything a good outcome. It is worth adding that these
categories are conceptual and there is no test that sorts a child into one.

**6.5 · B.** A is the guilt most parents carry, and it draws on observational
studies whose effects are small and heavily confounded by household income,
parental availability, family stress, and mental health, all of which
independently track child outcomes. What is well supported is the video deficit
effect: under about two years, babies learn markedly less from a video
demonstration than from a live one, largely because a screen cannot be
contingent. That is also exactly why video chat is carved out of the guidance,
since a grandparent on a call responds in real time to what your baby just did.
A caregiver at the absolute end of their reserves is a real risk to the thing
that actually matters, so if you need twenty minutes, take the twenty minutes.

**6.6 · C.** A is what a lot of parents tell themselves, and it gets the priority
exactly backwards. Perinatal mood and anxiety conditions are common, they are not
caused by insufficient love or effort, and they are among the more treatable
things in medicine and the least treated, because people do not present. D is
worth naming directly: the research linking parental depression to child outcomes
is correlational, describes small to moderate group averages, and is heavily
confounded, and most children of depressed parents do not develop the outcomes
studied. It is a reason to resource parental mental health quickly. It is not
evidence that anyone has already harmed their baby.
