## Module 5 · Bodies in Motion

This module is about your baby's body: how it learns to hold a head up, roll, sit, crawl, cruise, and walk, and why that sequence is far messier and far more forgiving than the charts suggest. It is also about the two topics that generate more guilt than any others in the first year, sleep and feeding, and it treats them the way the rest of this course treats everything else: with the actual evidence, the actual uncertainty, and no moralizing. Along the way we will make the case that movement is not a separate track from thinking. A baby who has just learned to crawl is not only a baby who can now cross a room. They are a baby whose whole relationship to space, memory, and you has been reorganized.

### Lesson 5.1 · Motor development is not a ladder

Almost every image you have seen of infant motor development is a staircase. A silhouette lies down, then props up, then sits, then crawls, then stands, then walks, each step neatly above the last, each with a month number stamped beneath it. It is a clean picture and it is wrong in a specific way that matters for how you feel at three in the morning when your baby is eight months old and showing no interest in crawling.

Here is what is closer to the truth. Motor development is not a sequence of levels a baby climbs. It is a continuous process of a particular body, with particular proportions and particular muscle strength, solving particular problems in a particular environment. Change any of those inputs and the solution changes. Esther Thelen's work made this concrete with one of the most satisfying findings in developmental science. Newborns held upright with their feet on a surface make stepping movements. That stepping "reflex" disappears at around two months, and for decades the textbook explanation was neurological: a maturing cortex was inhibiting a primitive brainstem reflex. Thelen and Fisher had already shown, in 1982, that upright stepping and the kicking a baby does lying on their back are the same underlying muscle synergism, which made the tidy "a reflex switches off" story harder to believe. Then in 1984, Thelen, Fisher and Ridley-Johnson tested the alternative directly, in three studies reported together.

In the first, they followed 40 infants at two, four, and six weeks. The babies who were gaining weight fastest were the ones stepping least by four weeks. In the second, they added small weights to the legs of babies who were still stepping, and the stepping dropped off, with weaker flexion at the joints. In the third, they submerged babies' legs in water, so buoyancy took some of the weight off, and the stepping rate went up, with larger joint flexions. Babies in those months gain fat faster than they gain muscle, so their legs get heavy relative to their strength. The authors' conclusion was that the disappearance of stepping is better explained by uneven, asynchronous physical growth than by any of the neurological accounts that preceded it. The behavior did not vanish from the brain. The physics changed.

That is the whole argument in miniature. A milestone is not a switch flipping in the brain. It is what happens when strength, body proportions, motivation, practice, and the surfaces and support available in a baby's daily life line up well enough for a solution to emerge.

Karen Adolph and colleagues have spent decades filling in what that looks like in practice, and the numbers are worth sitting with. In a study published in Psychological Science in 2012, Adolph, Cole, Komati and colleagues tracked infants who were learning to walk during natural free play. Newly walking infants took thousands of steps in an hour of play, travelled the length of multiple football fields in a day, and fell dozens of times a day. Not dozens of times in a difficult week. Dozens of times a day, as the ordinary texture of learning to walk. And crucially, the falls did not predict much of anything bad. Falling was not a sign of a problem with walking. Falling was walking, in its early form.

That reframes what you are watching when your baby topples over for the fortieth time before lunch. You are not watching failure with occasional success mixed in. You are watching an enormous volume of practice, distributed across the day, in which most attempts are imperfect and the imperfection is the mechanism. Adolph and Hoch's 2019 review in the Annual Review of Psychology gathers this literature and makes the general point: infant motor learning is variable, flexible, and situation-specific rather than staged. Babies do not learn "walking" as an abstract skill and then apply it everywhere. They learn to walk on this carpet, then have to partly relearn it on grass, on a slope, in shoes, while carrying something. Adolph and Robinson's work on variability makes the critique of stage models directly: the same infant, observed across days, does not show a tidy progression. They show a moving cloud of attempts, some more advanced than others, with the average drifting upward over weeks.

Which brings us to the practical question you actually care about, which is: is my baby on time?

The honest answer is that "on time" is a much wider window than the chart on the wall of the pediatrician's office implies. The WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study Group published windows of achievement for six gross motor milestones in 2006, based on longitudinal data from 816 healthy infants followed from four to twenty-four months across five countries: Ghana, India, Norway, Oman, and the United States. These are not averages with a decorative range attached. They are windows within which the great majority of healthy, typically developing children achieve the skill, and they are wide. Independent sitting spans roughly the second half of the first year. Hands-and-knees crawling spans a broad stretch of months. Walking alone has a window running from around the end of the first year well into the second. A baby at the late edge of a window is not a baby who is behind. They are a baby at the late edge of normal, which by definition a substantial number of perfectly healthy babies have to be.

The WHO study also found something that undercuts the ladder picture entirely. Not every child crawls on hands and knees. Thirty-five of the 816 children, 4.3 percent, were never observed to crawl on hands and knees at all. They moved from sitting to pulling up to walking, or got around by scooting on their bottom, or by commando-crawling on their belly, or by rolling with purpose. Because of this, the WHO excluded those children from that milestone's analysis and treated hands-and-knees crawling differently from the other five, precisely because it is not universal. It is also the only one of the six that does not hold a fixed position in the sequence: about ninety percent of children followed a fixed order for the other five, while crawling moved around. Let that land: an internationally standardized reference study for infant motor development declined to treat crawling as a required step, because plenty of healthy children do not take it.

You may have heard that skipping crawling causes later problems with reading, coordination, or "brain integration." There is no good evidence for that. It is a claim that circulates widely, often attached to programs selling remediation, and the developmental literature does not support it. We will say so again in the "not saying" section, because it deserves saying twice.

So what should you actually do with all this? Mostly, offer opportunity and get out of the way. Babies who spend time on the floor, on varied surfaces, with room to move and things worth moving toward, get more practice, and practice is the currency here. You are not teaching the skill. You are supplying the conditions and the motivation. The motivation is very often you: a baby will work harder to reach a face they love than a toy they do not.

And there is a real limit to reassurance, which we will state plainly. Wide normal variability is not the same as "never worry." Some patterns warrant a clinician's eyes rather than a chart. Loss of a motor skill your baby previously had is one. Persistent stiffness, or persistent floppiness, is another. Strong asymmetry, meaning your baby consistently uses one side of the body and neglects the other, or always turns the head one way, is another. Those are not "wait and see" observations. They are "mention this to your pediatrician" observations, and mentioning them early costs you nothing. The CDC's "Learn the Signs. Act Early." program maintains free milestone checklists and a tracking app that are genuinely useful for organizing what you are seeing before an appointment. This course does not replace a pediatrician, and nothing here should be used to talk yourself out of an appointment you were already considering.

### Lesson 5.2 · Tummy time, containers, and the back-to-sleep tradeoff

Let us start with the part that is not up for debate, and state it without softening.

Put your baby on their back for every sleep, naps included, for the entire first year. Use a firm, flat sleep surface, in a crib, bassinet, or play yard that meets current safety standards. Your baby sleeps alone on that surface: no pillows, no loose blankets, no crib bumpers, no positioners, no stuffed animals, no inclined sleepers. Share a room with your baby, ideally for at least the first six months, but not the same sleep surface. These are the American Academy of Pediatrics safe sleep recommendations, updated in 2022 in Pediatrics, and they exist because the back-to-sleep campaigns that began in the early 1990s were followed by a dramatic fall in sudden infant death syndrome. This is one of the clearest public health wins in modern pediatrics. It is not a matter of preference or philosophy, and it is not something this course will hedge on.

Now the part that gets discussed badly.

There is a real finding, from Davis, Moon, Sachs and Ottolini, published in Pediatrics in 1998, that infants who slept prone reached some early motor milestones slightly earlier on average than infants who slept supine. Rolling, sitting, and crawling showed small timing differences. This finding is real, and it gets used dishonestly in two directions. Some people use it to imply that back sleeping "damages" development. Others pretend the finding does not exist.

Both are wrong, and the correct reading is straightforward. What Davis and colleagues found was a difference in the timing of some early milestones, not a difference in developmental outcome. The supine-sleeping infants reached the same milestones. All of the infants in that study were within normal ranges. By the time you look at later motor competence, the gap is not the story. A few weeks' difference in when hands-and-knees crawling first appears is a timing difference, and timing differences of that size in the first year are not developmental advantages. Set that against a large, replicated reduction in infant deaths and the tradeoff is not close. Back sleeping, every time.

The finding does, however, tell you something useful about mechanism: prone position gives babies practice, and practice matters. Which is exactly why the answer is not to compromise on sleep position. The answer is awake tummy time.

Tummy time is simply time your baby spends on their front while awake and supervised. It gives them the thing prone sleeping incidentally gave, which is the chance to push against a surface with their arms, lift and turn a heavy head, strengthen the neck, shoulders, and trunk, and see the world from an angle that makes reaching worth attempting. The AAP recommends starting from the first days home, in very short bouts, and building up as your baby tolerates it. Common consensus guidance suggests working toward something on the order of an hour a day, broken into many short sessions, by around three months.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** The tummy time evidence base is largely observational. Reviews such as Hewitt, Kerr, Okely and colleagues found associations between greater tummy time and gross motor development, and also with lower risk of positional plagiocephaly, but the studies are predominantly correlational and heterogeneous in how tummy time was measured, usually by parent report. Specific dose recommendations, including the widely repeated "one hour a day by three months," are consensus guidance rather than trial-derived thresholds. The direction of the advice is sound and low risk. The precision of the number is not evidence. Reviewer should decide how firmly to state the dose.

There is a second reason tummy time earns its place, and it is not about milestones at all. Since babies now spend all their sleep on their backs, and often a great deal of their awake time in seats, positional plagiocephaly, meaning a flat spot on one part of the skull, became more common. It is a cosmetic and positional issue rather than a brain issue, and it is usually preventable and manageable with varied positioning: tummy time, alternating which end of the crib you lay your baby down at, alternating arms when you carry, and simply holding your baby more.

Many babies hate tummy time at first, and this is worth naming because parents interpret the protest as a verdict on themselves. It is not. Lifting a head that is a quarter of your body weight, using muscles you have barely used, is genuinely hard work. Things that help: start with very short bouts, thirty seconds is a real session; try chest-to-chest tummy time with you reclined, which is still tummy time; get down on the floor at eye level so there is a face worth lifting the head for; try it after a nap rather than after a feed; put a rolled towel under the chest and armpits for a little mechanical advantage. If your baby cries, pick them up. Ending on a manageable note builds tolerance faster than pushing through a meltdown.

Now, containers. "Container" is the shorthand for anything that holds a baby's body in a fixed position: bouncers, swings, infant seats, car seats used outside the car, jumpers, activity centers, and strollers for long stretches. These devices are not evil, and the safety case for the car seat in the car is absolute. The concern is cumulative time. A baby in a container is a baby who is not on the floor, not shifting their own weight, not discovering what happens when they lean, not doing any of the thousands of small self-directed trials that Adolph's work shows is how motor learning actually accumulates. Time in containers is time subtracted from practice.

Two specific notes. Infant seats and car seats are not safe sleep surfaces. If your baby falls asleep in the car seat, move them to a firm flat surface on their back when you get home. And walkers with wheels are actively unsafe: the AAP has called for a ban on them for years because of injury data, mostly falls down stairs, and they do not accelerate walking.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Container-use limits are consensus guidance, not trial evidence. There is no randomized trial establishing a threshold of daily container time above which motor development suffers. The mechanistic argument, that container time displaces self-directed practice, is well grounded in the motor learning literature, but the specific advice to "limit containers" is expert consensus. Recommend stating the reasoning rather than implying a proven dose-response relationship. Note separately that the walker injury data is strong and should not be softened.

The practical version of this lesson fits on a sticky note. Back to sleep, always. Tummy to play, often. Floor over seat, whenever it is realistic. And no guilt about the swing that buys you twenty minutes to eat.

### Lesson 5.3 · Motion and thinking are the same project

There is a moment, somewhere around seven to ten months for many babies, when a family says something like: "Everything changed at once." The baby started moving, and simultaneously stopped sleeping the way they had been, started protesting when a familiar adult left the room, became fascinated by cupboards, and developed opinions about being put down. It feels like several unrelated developments arriving on the same day.

It is not several. It is mostly one, viewed from different angles. When a baby becomes able to move themselves through space, the change is not just logistical. It is cognitive, emotional, and social, all at once.

Start with what happens to perception. A baby who is carried travels, but the travel is not theirs. The view changes without their intention producing it. A baby who crawls generates their own optic flow: the world streams past because they decided to move, and stops when they stop. That coupling, between an intention, a movement, and a change in what you see, is the raw material for a lot of learning. Depth, distance, and the layout of a room stop being a picture and become a set of consequences.

Campos, Anderson, Barbu-Roth and colleagues gathered this argument in a 2000 paper with a title that has stuck: "Travel broadens the mind." Their claim was that the onset of self-produced locomotion is a developmental milestone with wide psychological consequences, reorganizing spatial cognition, attention, memory for locations, wariness of heights, social referencing, and emotional communication with caregivers. As a framework for thinking about the first year, it is genuinely valuable, and it is why this lesson exists.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** The "travel broadens the mind" literature has design limits that should be stated. Much of the evidence is correlational: crawling infants differ from non-crawling infants of the same age in many ways besides crawling. The stronger tests used locomotor experience comparisons and artificial locomotion, for example giving pre-crawling infants powered walker experience, but these are typically small samples and are not always independently replicated. The direction, that locomotor experience contributes to spatial and social-emotional change, is reasonably supported. Strong causal statements of the form "crawling causes cognitive advance" outrun the design. Recommend the framework be presented as a well-motivated account rather than settled cause.

Reaching is the earlier chapter of the same story. Long before a baby can move their whole body, they are working on moving one arm to a specific place, which turns out to be a hard problem. Early reaches, from around three to five months, are ballistic and imprecise: an arm launches and often misses. Over the following months reaching becomes smoother, better guided by vision, and eventually anticipatory, so a baby shapes the hand before contact according to whether the target is a ball or an edge. Grasp refines too, from a whole-hand rake toward the pincer grip using thumb and forefinger that typically appears somewhere in the second half of the year. Each of these gives your baby a new class of experience. A baby who can pick up a small object can now bang it, drop it, mouth it, and discover that objects have properties. Reaching is how the world becomes examinable.

Now memory, because there is a lovely and often misremembered line of research here. Carolyn Rovee-Collier developed a method called mobile conjugate reinforcement. A ribbon runs from the baby's ankle to a mobile above the crib. When the baby kicks, the mobile moves, proportionally to the kick. Babies as young as two to three months learn this within minutes, and their kick rate rises well above their own baseline. Then, crucially, you can come back days or weeks later, hang the mobile without the ribbon connected, and see whether the baby kicks. If they do, they remembered.

What that work established is that infant memory is real and much earlier than the field had assumed. What it also established is that infant memory is exquisitely tied to context. Change the mobile, change the pattern on the crib bumper, change the room, and retention drops sharply. Young infants remembered "kicking makes the mobile with the blue blocks move, in this crib, in this room," not an abstract rule about kicking. Retention intervals also lengthened steadily with age across the first year, and a reminder, a brief re-exposure to the moving mobile, could reactivate a memory that had apparently faded.

Two things follow for you. First, your baby is learning contingencies constantly, and the thing they are learning is that their own action produces an effect. That is the same lesson underneath the responsive interactions in Module 2 and Module 4. It is also why toys that respond to what a baby actually did are more interesting to a baby than toys that perform on their own schedule. Second, repetition in the same context is not boring to your baby. It is how retention works. The bath song sung in the bath, the same book at the same time, the same peekaboo with the same cloth: the sameness is doing work.

Now back to that "everything changed at once" family. Consider what new mobility actually does to the rest of life.

Sleep often gets worse, or at least different, when a baby learns to roll, sit, crawl, or pull to stand. A skill under active construction gets practiced everywhere, including at two in the morning in the dark. Babies pull to stand in the crib and then cannot work out how to get down. This is a normal and usually temporary disruption. Practicing the new skill deliberately during the day, including the getting-down half of it, tends to shorten it.

Separation distress often intensifies in the same window. This is not a coincidence and it is not regression. A baby who can leave is a baby for whom your leaving has become meaningful, and a baby who is now spending real time at a distance from you needs a reliable way to check in. That is why social referencing, the habit of looking back at your face to find out whether an ambiguous situation is fine, shows up so strongly around the onset of locomotion. Your face becomes a consulted instrument. Module 4 covers this in depth; the point here is that the emotional change and the motor change are the same event.

And your own life changes, which is worth saying without pretending it is purely delightful. A mobile baby is more work. The floor needs a different level of attention, outlets and stairs and cords become live issues, and you cannot put the baby down and expect them to be where you left them. Baby-proofing before you need it is a kindness to yourself, because it converts a day of constant "no" into a day where the environment does the enforcement and you get to be the person who plays.

### Lesson 5.4 · Sleep without the guilt

Sleep and feeding, which is the next lesson, generate more shame per square inch than anything else in the first year, and most of that shame is manufactured by confident advice that outruns its evidence. So here is the evidence, and here is where it stops. Sleep first, because it is the one costing you the most right now.

**What infant sleep actually does in the first year.** Newborn sleep is distributed across the twenty-four hour cycle in short bouts because newborn circadian rhythm is not yet established and stomachs are small. Over the first months, sleep gradually consolidates: longer stretches at night, more organized daytime naps, more sleep occurring during the dark hours. Henderson, France, Owens and Blampied, publishing in Pediatrics in 2010, tracked this longitudinally and described the normative course of consolidation across the first year. Two findings from that literature deserve to be much better known than they are.

First, consolidation is gradual and its trajectory varies a lot between babies. Second, and this is the one to hold onto, a large proportion of babies who have started sleeping through the night go back to waking, and night waking at six to twelve months is common in typically developing infants. Depending on the study and the definition used, somewhere in the range of a quarter to a third of infants in the second half of the first year are still waking at night regularly. Waking at night at nine months is not a failure of your baby, your routine, or you. It is within the ordinary range of infant biology.

That range needs its definition attached, though, because "sleeping through the night" turns out to mean whatever the researchers decided it meant, and the choice moves the answer enormously. Henderson and colleagues followed 75 infants monthly across the whole first year and scored the same babies against three different criteria. By a "midnight to five in the morning" criterion, half the babies were there by three months, and at twelve months 11 of the 75, which is 15 percent, still were not. Requiring any eight consecutive hours gave the same picture. But by a "ten at night to six in the morning" criterion, half the babies got there only by five months, and at twelve months 21 of the 75, which is 28 percent, still had not. Same babies, same year, same data. Fifteen percent or twenty-eight percent, depending entirely on where you put the clock.

So when you meet a reassuring statistic, the first question is which criterion it used. A study counting five hours from midnight, or any eight consecutive hours wherever they happen to fall, will produce comfortable numbers. A criterion built around the hours you are actually trying to be asleep produces considerably higher rates of still-waking. Henderson and colleagues argued that the ten to six window is the most developmentally and socially valid of the three, precisely because it is the one that corresponds to whether anybody in the house got a night.

Weinraub and colleagues reached a similar place from a different direction, following more than 1,200 infants. They found two groups rather than one. About 66 percent were "sleepers," waking roughly one night a week from six months right through to thirty-six months. The other 34 percent were "transitional sleepers," waking around seven nights a week at six months and settling to about two nights a week by fifteen months. A third of babies on the slower path is not a third of babies with a problem. It is what the distribution actually looks like.

**Safe sleep, restated.** Because this lesson is about sleep, we state it again, plainly, per the AAP 2022 recommendations. Back to sleep for every sleep. Firm, flat, separate surface designed for infant sleep. No soft bedding, no pillows, no loose blankets, no bumpers, no positioners, no inclined sleepers. Room-sharing without bed-sharing, ideally at least six months. Avoid smoke exposure. Offer a pacifier at sleep onset once feeding is going well. Do not use products marketed for sleep that are not designed and regulated for infant sleep, including many popular inclined and cushioned devices. If you are exhausted and feeding at night, the safest thing is to feed in a chair or bed cleared of pillows and blankets rather than on a sofa or armchair, because falling asleep with a baby on a sofa or armchair carries a particularly high risk. If your circumstances make any of this hard, that is a conversation for your pediatrician, who would much rather help you plan than have you improvise.

**Sleep training, honestly.** Here is what the trials actually show.

Hiscock and Wake, in a BMJ randomized controlled trial published in 2002, randomized families of infants with parent-reported sleep problems at around six to twelve months to a behavioural sleep intervention or usual care. The intervention group showed improved infant sleep by parent report, and mothers in the intervention group had lower depression scores at the short-term follow-up. That maternal outcome matters and is often left out of the argument. It should be reported with its expiry date, though: by the time this cohort was followed up at child age six, the maternal depression difference had not persisted.

Gradisar and colleagues, in Pediatrics in 2016, ran a randomized trial comparing graduated extinction, bedtime fading, and a sleep education control in infants around six to sixteen months. Both active interventions reduced time to sleep onset, graduated extinction reduced night wakings, and, importantly, the study measured infant salivary cortisol and maternal-reported stress, and assessed attachment and emotional/behavioural outcomes at twelve months. It found no evidence of adverse effects on infant cortisol, attachment security, or behaviour at that follow-up.

Mindell and colleagues' review of behavioral sleep interventions in infants and young children found that these methods have consistent short-term efficacy for sleep onset and night waking across a substantial number of studies.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Two limits on the reassurance above. First, the Gradisar et al. 2016 trial is small, with roughly forty-three families randomized across three arms, and the twelve month follow-up retained fewer. It is meaningful evidence against the strongest "sleep training is harmful" claims, but a study of that size cannot rule out small effects, and it should not be cited as if it settles the question. Second, long-term outcome data is thin in both directions. The most-cited long-term follow-up, Price and colleagues' five year follow-up of the Hiscock and Wake cohort, found no differences between groups on child emotional or behavioural outcomes, child-parent relationship, or child cortisol. That is genuine evidence of no lasting harm, and it is also evidence of no lasting benefit. Reviewer should ensure the section does not read as either an endorsement or a warning.

So what should the course say? This: behavioral sleep interventions work in the short term for the outcomes they target, the best available evidence does not show harm to attachment or stress physiology, and the long-term evidence does not show large lasting effects in either direction. That combination makes this a values decision, not a medical requirement. Families who sleep train are not damaging their babies. Families who do not are not failing them. If you and your baby are doing fine, there is nothing to fix. If you are not fine, and parental exhaustion is a real health issue rather than a character test, this is a legitimate tool and your pediatrician can help you use it. Whatever you choose, safe sleep practice does not change.

### Lesson 5.5 · Feeding without the guilt

Feeding gets the same treatment sleep just got. Here is what is actually established, here is what is contested, and here is the large territory where the honest answer is that it is your call and the evidence does not care.

**The established part.** It is shorter than the internet suggests.

Milk, whether human milk, formula, or a combination, is the primary source of nutrition for roughly the first six months and remains a major source through the first year. Complementary foods are generally introduced at around six months, which is the WHO recommendation and is consistent with AAP guidance, and which is also roughly when most babies show the relevant readiness: sitting with support and good head control, a reduced tongue-thrust reflex, and interest in food. Iron-rich foods matter early because iron stores from birth deplete over the first half year. Older advice told parents to delay allergenic foods. That advice has been reversed, and it is worth being precise about how far the reversal has actually been demonstrated. The strongest single piece of evidence is the LEAP trial (Du Toit et al., 2015, New England Journal of Medicine), which randomized 640 infants aged four to eleven months to eat peanut or avoid it. Among infants not already sensitized, peanut allergy at five years was 13.7 percent in the avoidance group and 1.9 percent in the consumption group. That is a large effect. Two limits matter. LEAP tested peanut, not allergenic foods in general, and the trials on egg have produced a genuinely mixed picture rather than a matching result. And LEAP enrolled infants at high risk specifically, meaning infants with severe eczema, egg allergy, or both, so it does not measure what happens in a general-population baby. The practical upshot is that delaying allergenic foods is not protective, and that peanut introduction in particular is now actively recommended for high-risk infants on a timeline your pediatrician should set. If your baby has severe eczema or a known food allergy, this is a conversation to have before you start solids, not after. Vitamin D supplementation is recommended for exclusively and partially breastfed infants. Honey should not be given before twelve months because of infant botulism. Cow's milk as a main drink waits until twelve months, though dairy in foods is fine earlier.

That is close to the whole list of things that are actually established. Everything past it is preference, culture, and circumstance.

**Responsive feeding** is the part with a real developmental logic behind it, and it maps directly onto everything else in this course. Responsive feeding means feeding in response to your baby's cues rather than to the clock or the volume markings on a bottle. Watch for hunger signals, rooting, hand-to-mouth, stirring and fussing before full crying, and for fullness signals, slowing down, turning away, closing the mouth, losing interest, relaxing the hands. Then respond to what you saw. Do not finish the bottle because there is an ounce left. Do not chase a closed mouth with a spoon. The value is not just nutritional. Feeding is one of the highest-frequency interactions of the first year, which makes it one of the largest available arenas for a baby to learn that their signals are read and answered. That is the same contingency lesson from Lesson 5.3, delivered eight times a day.

Some practical honesty about this. Babies vary in appetite day to day and week to week, and growth is not linear. Weight tracked along a percentile curve over time tells you far more than any single number. Bottle-fed babies can be paced, held more upright, with breaks, so they can regulate rather than being overwhelmed by flow. Solids in the first year are largely about learning: textures, self-feeding, the mechanics of a mouth. Milk is still carrying the nutrition. And feeding does not have to be efficient to be going well.

We are not going to prescribe a feeding method, a schedule, a philosophy, or a food list beyond the safety items above, because the evidence does not support that level of prescription and because families' circumstances differ in ways no course can see. Whether you breastfeed, formula feed, combination feed, pump exclusively, do baby-led weaning, spoon feed purees, or some mix that has no name, none of those choices is the variable that determines how your baby turns out.

Which brings us to the claim this course will not make, and will explicitly refuse.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Breastfeeding and cognitive outcomes is contested and should stay contested in the final text. The PROBIT cluster-randomized trial in Belarus, which randomized a breastfeeding promotion intervention rather than breastfeeding itself, reported higher IQ scores in the intervention arm at follow-up, with the effect concentrated in verbal measures and with the assessment structure drawing methodological criticism. Against that, sibling-comparison designs, which control for stable family characteristics by comparing differently-fed siblings within the same family, largely do not find the effect. Colen and Ramey (2014), in Social Science and Medicine, is the most-cited example and found that most differences attributed to breastfeeding attenuated substantially or vanished in within-family comparisons. Breastfeeding has well-established benefits, including a reduction in gastrointestinal and respiratory infection and an association with reduced SIDS risk, and this course should say so. The IQ claim specifically should not be asserted in either direction. Reviewer to confirm final wording.

The short version for a parent: how you feed your baby is not how you determine your baby's intelligence. The evidence for a real, causal breastfeeding effect on IQ does not hold up once family background is properly accounted for, and anyone who tells you with total confidence that it does, or that it definitely does not, is going past what the studies support. Feed your baby in whatever way works for your body, your circumstances, and your family, and spend the energy you save on the responsive back-and-forth that the rest of this course is about.

### Try this week

**1. Floor time audit.**
*What to do:* For two ordinary days, keep a rough tally of how much awake time your baby spends on a flat surface, floor or firm mat, with room to move, versus in a seat, swing, bouncer, carrier, or car seat outside the car. Do not change anything on day one. Just count.
*What you are looking for:* The ratio, and where the container time is coming from. Most families find one or two specific slots, often the stretch while you make dinner, doing most of the work.
*What it means if nothing happens:* If the ratio already looks fine to you, you are done. There is no target number to hit. If container time is high, the fix is not elimination, it is finding one predictable slot to convert to floor time. One slot is a real change.

**2. Tummy time, five short bouts.**
*What to do:* Instead of one long session, try five short ones spread across a day. Thirty seconds to two minutes each. Go down to eye level with your baby, or do it chest-to-chest with you reclined. Try after a nap, not after a big feed.
*What you are looking for:* Head lifting, weight shifting onto the forearms, turning to track your face or a sound, and pushing up. Also watch tolerance across the week rather than within a session.
*What it means if nothing happens:* If your baby protests every time, pick them up and try again later. Protest is not a sign you have done it wrong. If after a couple of weeks of short bouts your baby still cannot lift and hold their head at all in a position where you would expect it, or if their head control seems to be going backwards, that is worth mentioning to your pediatrician rather than working around.

**3. Motivate a reach, do not deliver it.**
*What to do:* Put something genuinely interesting just outside your baby's current reach. A toy, a safe household object, your own face. Then wait. Give it several seconds of silence rather than narrating or moving the object closer.
*What you are looking for:* The attempt. An arm launching, a shoulder rotating, a body leaning, a leg pushing, a roll initiated. The attempt is the whole point, not the acquisition.
*What it means if nothing happens:* If your baby loses interest, the object was not interesting enough or was too far. Move it slightly closer and try again, or use your face, which usually outperforms toys. If your baby consistently reaches with only one hand and never the other, note that specifically and mention it at your next visit. Strong, persistent one-sidedness is one of the few motor observations worth flagging early.

**4. Contingency check.**
*What to do:* Find something your baby can cause. A rattle they hold, a crinkle book, a toy that moves when kicked, or simply you responding every single time they vocalize. Do the same thing in the same spot for a few days running.
*What you are looking for:* Escalation. Does the kicking or batting increase above their usual rate once they work out the connection? Does the behavior come back faster on day three than day one? That is the Rovee-Collier effect in your living room.
*What it means if nothing happens:* Very young babies need many repetitions and the same context each time, so try holding the setting constant for several days before concluding anything. If your baby seems not to respond to your responses at all, across contexts and over weeks, that is worth raising with your pediatrician. It is much more often a matter of state, timing, and difficulty than a signal of anything.

**5. Write your own sleep question down.**
*What to do:* Before your next pediatric visit, write two sentences: what your baby's sleep actually looks like over a typical week, and what specifically is not working for you. Not what you think you are supposed to want. What is not working.
*What you are looking for:* Clarity about whether you have a problem to solve or a normal pattern you have been told to be ashamed of. Night waking at six to twelve months is common and normal. If it is not costing you anything, there is nothing to fix.
*What it means if nothing happens:* If writing it down reveals that everyone is coping, that is a real result and you can stop reading sleep content. If it reveals that you are not coping, that is a legitimate medical conversation to have, and exhaustion is a good enough reason on its own to ask for help.

### What this module is not saying

- **Not saying that early walkers are smarter or more advanced.** Age of walking within the normal range does not predict later cognitive ability in any way that should influence how you feel about your baby. The WHO windows are wide because healthy variation is wide.
- **Not saying that skipping crawling causes later problems.** There is no good evidence that children who skip hands-and-knees crawling have later difficulties with reading, coordination, or "neurological integration." The WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study treated crawling differently from the other gross motor milestones precisely because a substantial minority of healthy children never do it. Programs that sell remediation for skipped crawling are selling a fix for a problem that has not been established.
- **Not saying that prone sleeping is developmentally better.** Davis et al. (1998) found small differences in the timing of some early milestones, not differences in developmental outcome. Timing is not advantage, and the safe sleep evidence is not close.
- **Not saying your baby must sleep through the night by any particular month.** Night waking at six to twelve months is common in typically developing infants, and "sleeping through the night" is defined inconsistently across the very studies people quote at you. In Henderson et al. (2010), the same 75 babies were 15 percent or 28 percent "still waking" at twelve months depending only on whether the criterion was midnight to five or ten to six.
- **Not saying sleep training is required, and not saying it is harmful.** Behavioral sleep interventions have short-term randomized support and no demonstrated harm to attachment or infant cortisol in the trials that measured them, and the long-term follow-up data shows neither lasting harm nor lasting benefit. This is a values decision.
- **Not saying any specific feeding choice determines outcomes.** Not breast, not formula, not combination, not purees, not baby-led weaning. None of these is the variable that determines how your child develops.
- **Not saying breastfeeding causes higher IQ.** This is contested. The PROBIT cluster-randomized trial reported cognitive differences favouring the breastfeeding promotion arm, with methodological criticism attached; sibling-comparison studies such as Colen and Ramey (2014) found those differences largely disappear once stable family characteristics are controlled. Breastfeeding has other well-supported benefits. An IQ effect is not one this course will assert.
- **Not saying tummy time has a proven dose.** The recommendation is sensible, low risk, and supported by correlational evidence. The specific hourly targets are consensus, not trial results.
- **Not saying that self-produced locomotion causes cognitive development in a simple, established way.** "Travel broadens the mind" is a well-motivated framework with real supporting evidence and genuine design limits. Movement and cognition are entangled. That is a weaker and more honest claim than causation.
- **Not saying wide normal variation means never worry.** Loss of previously acquired motor skills, persistent stiffness or floppiness, or strong asymmetry of movement are reasons to see a clinician, not reasons to wait. This course does not replace a pediatrician.

### Module summary

- Motor development is not a ladder. It is a body solving problems with the strength, proportions, motivation, and surfaces available, which is why Thelen, Fisher and Ridley-Johnson (1984) could bring back the "disappeared" stepping reflex just by taking the weight off a baby's legs in water.
- Newly walking infants take thousands of steps and fall dozens of times a day (Adolph et al., 2012). Falling is not failure. It is the practice mechanism.
- Learning is variable and situation-specific, not staged. Babies relearn skills across surfaces and contexts rather than acquiring an abstract skill once.
- The WHO windows of achievement for six gross motor milestones are wide, and hands-and-knees crawling is not universal. Plenty of healthy babies never crawl, and there is no good evidence this causes later problems.
- Back to sleep, every sleep, all year. Firm flat separate surface. No soft bedding, bumpers, positioners, or inclined sleepers. Room-share without bed-sharing. This is not negotiable and it has saved lives.
- Prone sleeping was associated with slightly earlier timing on some early milestones. That is a timing difference, not a developmental advantage, and it does not change the recommendation.
- Awake, supervised tummy time is the answer to the practice question. Short frequent bouts, at eye level, work better than long ones. Protest is normal and is not a verdict on you.
- Container time, meaning seats, swings, bouncers, and jumpers, is time subtracted from self-directed practice. Car seats are for cars and are not sleep surfaces. Wheeled walkers are unsafe.
- Movement and thinking are the same project. Self-produced locomotion changes what a baby perceives, remembers, and needs from you, which is why mobility, sleep disruption, and separation distress so often arrive together.
- Rovee-Collier's mobile studies showed infant memory is real and early, and also highly context-bound. Repetition in the same setting is not boring to your baby. It is how retention works.
- Sleep consolidation is gradual and night waking at six to twelve months is common and normal (Henderson et al., 2010). Always ask which criterion a "sleeps through" statistic used: the same 75 babies were 15 percent still waking at twelve months by a midnight-to-five rule and 28 percent by the ten-to-six rule the authors considered most valid. Weinraub et al. (2012) found 34 percent of more than 1,200 infants on a slower waking trajectory that was still entirely ordinary. Sleep training works short term, shows no demonstrated harm in trials that measured attachment and cortisol, and has thin long-term data in both directions. It is a values choice.
- Feed responsively: read hunger and fullness cues rather than the clock or the bottle markings. Solids around six months, iron-rich foods early, no honey before one year, vitamin D for breastfed infants. Beyond the safety items, this course does not prescribe a feeding method.
