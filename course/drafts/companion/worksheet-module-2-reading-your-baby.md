# Worksheet · Module 2 · Reading Your Baby

**What this is for:** a place to record what you actually see while you practice
the five "Try this week" activities from Module 2, so that states and cues stop
being a list you read and become a thing you can spot.

Your baby is communicating constantly, in shifts of alertness, small movements of
the hands and face, changes in breathing and color, and the timing of a cry.
Nobody reads it perfectly, including people who study it for a living, so treat
everything below as noticing practice rather than a test you can fail.

---

## 1. Run a state watch

**What to do.** Once, for about twenty minutes, watch your baby without trying to
change anything. Note which of the six states they are in and when it shifts. Do
it during a normally busy part of the day rather than a calm one.

The six states: quiet sleep, active sleep, drowsy, quiet alert, active alert,
crying.

**Write it down.**

Time started: __________   Time ended: __________

| Roughly when | State | What changed just before the shift |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ________ | ________________ | ______________________________ |
| ________ | ________________ | ______________________________ |
| ________ | ________________ | ______________________________ |
| ________ | ________________ | ______________________________ |

**What you are looking for:** the transitions. Specifically, what happens right
before a shift: the change in breathing, the movement picking up, the gaze going
unfocused.

**What it means if nothing happens:** if your baby stays in one state the entire
time, you have learned something useful about their rhythms and you can try again
at a different hour. If you cannot tell the states apart yet, watch breathing
regularity first. It is the easiest single indicator and it separates quiet sleep
from active sleep immediately.

---

## 2. Find quiet alert, and use it

**What to do.** When you catch your baby eyes open, body still, breathing steady,
drop what you are doing. Bring your face close, near enough that it fills most of
what they can see, and just look at them. Talk quietly, or say nothing. Let them look back.

**Write it down.**

Day and rough time: ______________________________________________

Roughly how long the window lasted: ______________________________

What ended it: ___________________________________________________

What they did while it lasted: ___________________________________

__________________________________________________________________

**What you are looking for:** sustained gaze at your face, eyebrows lifting, a
stilling of the body, maybe a small sound. Note roughly how long it lasts before
they look away.

**What it means if nothing happens:** in the early weeks these windows can be
very brief and may not appear at a convenient time for days. A baby who does not
lock on is often just not in that state, or is younger than the visual system
needs. Try again tomorrow. There is nothing to make up for.

---

## 3. Practice the back off and wait

**What to do.** During any face-to-face interaction, the moment your baby looks
away, stop. Stop talking, stop moving, lean back slightly, keep your hands
supporting them. Say nothing for ten to twenty seconds.

**Write it down.**

What they did to signal the break: _______________________________

Roughly how many seconds until they turned back: _________________

Did they turn back at all? [ ] yes  [ ] no  [ ] escalated to crying

How it felt to do nothing: _______________________________________

**What you are looking for:** whether they turn back to you on their own, and how
long it takes. Most parents are surprised how short the break needs to be.

**What it means if nothing happens:** if they do not turn back within thirty
seconds or so, the interaction is over for now rather than paused, and ending it
is the correct read. If they escalate to crying, you were later than you thought,
and the cue you missed came earlier. Look for it next time.

---

## 4. Build your two-column cue list

**What to do.** Keep a running note, on your phone or on this page, with two
headings: "more" and "enough." Over several days, add the specific things your
baby actually does, in your words. Not the textbook list. Theirs.

**Write it down.**

| "More" (keep going) | "Enough" (I need a break) |
| --- | --- |
| ____________________________ | ____________________________ |
| ____________________________ | ____________________________ |
| ____________________________ | ____________________________ |
| ____________________________ | ____________________________ |
| ____________________________ | ____________________________ |

The two or three that repeat most: _______________________________

__________________________________________________________________

**What you are looking for:** repeats. After a few days you will notice that your
baby has two or three signature disengagement cues they use consistently, and
knowing those is worth more than knowing all twelve generic ones.

**What it means if nothing happens:** some babies genuinely signal late and
subtly, and a thin list after a week is real information, not a failure of
observation. If that is your baby, shift your attention to timing and context
instead: how long they usually last before they are done, and which situations
use them up fastest.

---

## 5. Have the step-away conversation before you need it

**What to do.** Today, while nobody is crying, say out loud to your partner or
whoever else cares for your baby: if either of us feels close to losing it, we
put the baby down on their back in the empty crib and walk out for a few minutes,
and nobody gets judged for it. Decide now where the safe spot is.

The instruction the plan is built on, from Module 2, unchanged:

> **If you feel yourself getting close to losing control, put your baby down on
> their back in an empty crib or bassinet, walk out of the room, and close the
> door. Set a timer for five or ten minutes. Let your baby cry. Breathe, call
> someone, splash water on your face, sit on the floor. Then go back.**
>
> A baby crying alone on their back in a safe, empty sleep space for ten minutes
> is not being harmed. Not at all.
>
> **Never shake a baby. Not once, not gently, not to get their attention.** An
> infant's head is heavy relative to their neck strength, and their brain is
> fragile in a way that makes shaking capable of causing bleeding, permanent
> disability, and death, in seconds, with no visible injury on the outside.

**Write it down.**

The safe spot is: ________________________________________________

I said this out loud to: _________________________________________

Date: ____________

One person I can call at a bad hour: _____________________________

Their number: ____________________________________________________

I asked them this week if that is okay: [ ] yes  [ ] not yet

**What you are looking for:** agreement, spoken, in advance. That is the entire
goal. Plans made calmly are the ones that get used at three in the morning.

**What it means if nothing happens:** if you are doing this alone with no one to
have the conversation with, say it to yourself and write it down somewhere you
will see it. Then, separately, find one person you can call at a bad hour, and
ask them this week whether you can. Having the number matters more than using it.
