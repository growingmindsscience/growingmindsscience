## 1. Welcome

**Send:** Day 0, immediately on signup
**Subject:** Welcome to The First Year
**Preview text:** Including the parts where the evidence is thin.

Welcome. You are in.

Here is what this course is. Six modules on the first twelve months: what is
actually being built in your baby's head, how to read what they are telling you,
how language arrives, how security forms, how bodies learn to move, and how the
ordinary day holds all of it together.

Here is what makes it different, and it is the only promise worth making. This
course tells you when the science is thin.

You have probably been handed at least three confident, contradictory claims
already. Some of what you have been told is true. Some is true but much smaller
than it sounded. A few things are simply wrong, and those are usually the ones
that made you feel behind. Every module in this course ends with a section
called "What this module is not saying," where we list the popular claims we
refuse to make and explain why. The thirty million word gap is in there. So is
the Mozart effect. So is a claim you may find genuinely surprising: we will not
tell you, in either direction, whether your newborn imitates you, because the
research is contested and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

You will not find a closing window in here. You will not find a race.

You do not have to start today, and there is no schedule to fall behind on. The
modules will be there when you have a hand free.

**Next step:** open Module 1, Lesson 1.1, when you have ten quiet minutes. It is
about what your baby arrived already able to do.

## 2. The one idea

**Send:** Day 2
**Subject:** The active ingredient is contingency
**Preview text:** Not volume. Not enrichment. Timing.

If you take one idea from this whole course, take this one.

The framework is called serve and return, from the Center on the Developing
Child at Harvard. Your baby serves: a look, a coo, a reach, a change of
expression, a cry. You return: you look back, you answer, you name the thing
they are looking at, you pick them up. They serve again.

Underneath that sits the finding that does the most work. The active ingredient
is contingency. Contingency means what you do depends on what your baby just
did, closely enough in time that they can tell.

That is the difference between talking near your baby and talking with your
baby. Both involve words. Only one gives your baby evidence that their own
behaviour changes the world. That evidence is how they learn they are an agent
at all, and it is the foundation under communication, persistence, and the
expectation that other people are reachable.

Three things follow. Timing beats volume, which is why a screen or a radio does
not substitute: nothing you say to a screen changes what it does next. Following
beats directing, so the rich move is to talk about the ceiling fan your baby is
already staring at. And ordinary caregiving is already dense with these
exchanges. Feeding, changing, bathing, dressing. You do not need to add a
session to your day.

You also do not need to be contingent all the time. Nobody is. Missing a serve
is not damage, and the return to connection after a mismatch is itself part of
the process.

**Next step:** read Module 1, Lesson 1.3, which is where all of this lives.

## 3. Module 1 and the worksheet

**Send:** Day 5
**Subject:** Module 1, and one thing you can put down
**Preview text:** Plasticity is on your side, not against you.

Module 1 is about what is actually being built. It is also the module where we
take something off your plate, so if you read only one lesson this week, make it
Lesson 1.4.

The claim that the first three years determine everything is an overclaim, and
this course will not make it. Sensitive periods are real for specific systems on
specific timelines. Hubel and Wiesel established one definitively in the visual
system. Werker and Tees (1984) showed speech sound discrimination narrowing
between roughly six and twelve months. But look at the shape of every one of
those cases. Each concerns whether basic, universally available input was
present at all. None concerns whether input was optimised or maximised.
Sensitive period research is about floors, not ceilings.

Plasticity is usually sold to you as a threat: the brain is plastic now, so act
before it stops. Read it the other way, which is the way the evidence supports.
Plasticity is why recovery is possible. It is why a hard month, a period of
illness, or a return to work is not a verdict on a child.

The Module 1 worksheet walks you through the five "Try this week" activities.
The last one is a two-question test for a developmental worry you are currently
carrying: is this about the absence of something basic, or the absence of
something optimal? And is there an actual clinical question inside it? Most
worries turn out to be the second kind, and most of those you can put down.

**Next step:** print the Module 1 worksheet and do activity five, the one about
the worry.

## 4. States and cues

**Send:** Day 8
**Subject:** Your baby has six states, not two
**Preview text:** And it explains why the technique that worked yesterday failed today.

Your baby is not simply awake or asleep.

Peter Wolff, watching newborns for long stretches in the late 1950s and 1960s,
described six recognisable behavioural states: quiet sleep, active sleep,
drowsy, quiet alert, active alert, and crying. His descriptions were later built
into the Brazelton Neonatal Behavioral Assessment Scale, which is the framework
a lot of newborn nurses and pediatricians are working from when they handle your
baby in a way that looks almost like a conversation.

Which state your baby is in determines almost everything about what they can do
and how they will respond to anything you try. This is why a technique that
worked brilliantly yesterday fails today. The technique did not stop working. It
stopped matching the state.

Quiet alert is the one to know. Eyes open and bright, body still, breathing
regular, attention pointed outward. It is the state where social interaction
actually works. In the early weeks it is a small fraction of the day and the
windows may last a few minutes. That is normal, and it is not a missed
opportunity. The instruction is not to manufacture more of it. It is to notice
it when it happens.

The second layer is cues, and here is the reframe that tends to rearrange
people's week: gaze aversion is not rejection, it is regulation. Faces are
intense. Your baby cannot turn their arousal down internally yet, so they look
away, come down, and look back. Working harder at that moment raises the input
your baby was trying to lower.

**Next step:** read Module 2, Lesson 2.2, then try the back off and wait. Next
time your baby looks away, stop, lean back slightly, keep your hands supporting
them, and say nothing for ten to twenty seconds.

## 5. Crying, honestly

**Send:** Day 11
**Subject:** What the crying research actually found
**Preview text:** The early weeks are loud. That is not caused by you.

This is the email where we correct something you have almost certainly been
told, and the corrected version is less tidy than the popular one.

You may have met the claim that infant crying follows a curve peaking reliably
at around six weeks. That framing comes from Ronald Barr's work and underlies
the "period of PURPLE crying" materials handed out in a lot of hospitals. The
numbers do not support it as tidily as the framing implies.

Wolke, Bilgin and Samara published a meta-analysis in the Journal of Pediatrics
in 2017, pooling parent-diary data from 28 studies and 8,690 infants across a
range of countries. What they measured is fussing and crying combined, which
matters, because that number sounds roughly double what most parents mean by
crying. The averages per twenty-four hours: about 117 minutes at one to two
weeks, about 133 minutes at five to six weeks, about 68 minutes by ten to twelve
weeks.

Read those again and notice what they do and do not show. The decline by the
third month is clear and large. The rise to a peak is not. Durations across the
first six weeks were not statistically distinguishable from one another, and the
authors report finding no statistical evidence for a universal crying peak.

So here is the honest version. The early weeks are the loud ones. The loudness
is not caused by you: it shows up in babies with very different parents in very
different countries. And it gets better by around three months.

One more thing, and it is the most important sentence in Module 2. If you ever
feel close to losing control, put your baby down on their back in an empty crib,
walk out, and take five or ten minutes. A baby crying alone on their back in a
safe empty sleep space for ten minutes is not being harmed. Make that decision
without guilt. Never shake a baby, not once, not gently.

**Next step:** read Module 2, Lesson 2.3, and have the step-away conversation
out loud today, while nobody is crying.

## 6. Language, and the claim we refuse

**Send:** Day 14
**Subject:** We do not teach the thirty million word gap
**Preview text:** Nobody in Module 3 is going to ask you to talk more.

Hart and Risley (1995) reported a large difference in cumulative words heard by
age three between children in professional and welfare-recipient families. That
figure became one of the most repeated numbers in early childhood policy. Sperry,
Sperry and Miller (2019), in Child Development, attempted to replicate it across
five communities and did not.

The original study rested on a very small number of families and counted only
speech directed at the child by a primary caregiver in the home, then used that
count as though it measured the child's whole language environment. In
communities where children are talked to by many adults and older children, and
where overheard and multiparty speech carries a large share of the load, that
method systematically undercounts. The deficit framing that followed misread
those language environments as impoverished when what was actually impoverished
was the measurement.

So word count is not the target. There is no daily quota and no app that should
be counting for you.

What the evidence does point at is direction and contingency. Weisleder and
Fernald (2013) found that speech directed at the child, not speech merely
audible in the room, tracked later processing efficiency and vocabulary. That
study is correlational and cannot license a promise, but it is a good reason to
aim your speech at your baby when you are already talking.

And Goldstein, King and West (2003) ran the experiment that pins the mechanism
down. Mothers responded to nine-month-olds' vocalisations either immediately or
at unrelated times, with the same warmth and the same amount. Within a single
session, the babies getting contingent responses babbled in a more mature way.
The equally warm noncontingent group did not shift.

**Next step:** try the three-second pause. Say one short thing to your baby,
then stop, keep looking, and silently count to three. Baby response times are
slower than yours.

## 7. Attachment without the pressure

**Send:** Day 17
**Subject:** Attachment is a safety system, not a love meter
**Preview text:** And the effect size is smaller than you have been told.

Attachment is probably the most talked-about idea in parenting and the one most
often sold back to you as a technique or a warning. Module 4 does two jobs: it
explains what sixty years of research found, and it draws a line around what
that research cannot tell you.

Three things worth knowing now.

Attachment is a property of a relationship, not a trait of a baby. There is no
such thing as an insecure baby. A baby has an attachment relationship with each
specific caregiver, those relationships can differ inside the same family, and
they change.

Ainsworth's Strange Situation scores the reunion, not the separation. How much
your baby cries when you leave tells you more about temperament than about the
relationship. What is scored is what happens when you come back.

And here is the number most attachment content leaves out. De Wolff and van
IJzendoorn (1997) meta-analysed the studies linking parental sensitivity to
attachment security. The association is real and replicated, and it is modest,
around r = .24. Sensitivity is one meaningful contributor among many and is
nowhere near sufficient to determine the outcome.

That modest number is the most reassuring finding in the module. It means the
relationship is not a precision instrument sitting on a knife edge waiting for
you to mistime a response. There is enormous room in it for bad days, missed
cues, and being a person with limits.

Read it alongside Bakermans-Kranenburg and colleagues (2003), who found that
brief interventions narrowly focused on behaviour outperformed long
comprehensive ones. More effort is not the mechanism.

**Next step:** read Module 4, Lesson 4.2, on mismatch and repair. Repair is not
damage control. It is the curriculum.

## 8. How wide normal actually is

**Send:** Day 20
**Subject:** The WHO milestone windows are wider than the chart on the wall
**Preview text:** Including one milestone plenty of healthy babies skip entirely.

Almost every image of infant motor development is a staircase with a month
number stamped under each step. It is a clean picture and it is wrong in a way
that matters at three in the morning.

The WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study Group published windows of
achievement for six gross motor milestones in 2006, based on 816 healthy infants
followed from four to twenty-four months across Ghana, India, Norway, Oman and
the United States. These are not averages with a decorative range attached. They
are windows within which the great majority of healthy children achieve the
skill, and they are wide. Walking alone runs from around eight months to around
seventeen. The windows were published with no internal markings, deliberately,
to signal that all the variation inside them is normal.

A baby at the late edge of a window is not behind. They are at the late edge of
normal, which by definition a substantial number of healthy babies have to be.

The study also found something that undoes the staircase entirely. Thirty-five
of the 816 children, 4.3 percent, were never observed to crawl on hands and
knees at all. The WHO treated that milestone differently from the other five
precisely because it is not universal. If you have heard that skipping crawling
causes later problems with reading or coordination, there is no good evidence
for it, and the programmes selling remediation are selling a fix for a problem
that has not been established.

Wide normal is not the same as never worry. Loss of a motor skill your baby
previously had, persistent stiffness or floppiness, or strong one-sidedness are
reasons to mention something to your pediatrician rather than wait.

**Next step:** read Module 5, Lesson 5.1, then try the floor time audit. Count
for two days before changing anything.

## 9. Sleep, and what the trials show

**Send:** Day 23
**Subject:** "No adverse effects detected" is not "proven safe"
**Preview text:** The honest state of the sleep training evidence.

Sleep generates more shame per square inch than almost anything in the first
year, and most of it is manufactured by advice that outruns its evidence. So
here is the evidence and here is where it stops.

First, the thing nobody tells you clearly. Night waking at six to twelve months
is common in typically developing infants. Depending on the study and the
definition used, somewhere in the range of a quarter to a third of babies in the
second half of the first year are still waking regularly. Also worth knowing:
"sleeping through the night" is defined differently in nearly every study you
will encounter, and rarely as the thing you are imagining at four in the
morning.

On sleep training. Gradisar and colleagues (2016, Pediatrics) randomised infants
to graduated extinction, bedtime fading, or a sleep education control, and
measured infant salivary cortisol plus attachment and behavioural outcomes at
twelve months. They found no evidence of adverse effects.

That is a genuinely useful result, and the precise wording matters. The trial
randomised roughly forty-three families across three arms, which is about
fourteen per arm. A study that size cannot rule out small effects. So the
accurate statement is that no adverse effects were detected, not that safety has
been demonstrated. Anyone who tells you sleep training is proven safe is going
past the data.

Long-term, Price and colleagues followed the Hiscock and Wake cohort to child
age six and found nothing on either side: no lasting harm, and no lasting
benefit. The maternal mood improvement seen earlier had not persisted.

Which makes this a values decision, not a medical requirement. Families who
sleep train are not damaging their babies. Families who do not are not failing
them. Safe sleep practice does not change either way: back to sleep, every
sleep, firm flat separate surface, no soft bedding.

**Next step:** before your next pediatric visit, write two sentences. What your
baby's sleep actually looks like over a typical week, and what specifically is
not working for you.

## 10. Play and joint attention

**Send:** Day 26
**Subject:** The wooden spoon beats the toy that lights up
**Preview text:** And a milestone almost no list mentions.

The developmental value of a toy is roughly inverse to how much it does on its
own. A toy that lights up, plays a tune, and says a number when a button is
pressed has already made every interesting decision. The baby's contribution is
the button. A set of nesting cups, a colander, a scarf, a cardboard box: these
do nothing until the baby acts, and then they do exactly what the action makes
them do. That is contingency again, and it is free.

This is not a rule against buying toys. Expensive toys are fine and babies enjoy
them. The claim the course refuses is the marketing one, that a purchase adds
developmental value your kitchen drawer cannot.

Then there is the milestone that almost never makes the lists. For the early
months your baby's social life is two-way: you and them. Over the second half of
the year a third element enters. Your baby attends to an object and to you at
the same time, and seems to understand you are attending to it too. That is
joint attention, and it usually arrives as gaze following, then following a
point, then, often around nine to twelve months, a point of their own.

Liszkowski and colleagues (2004) found that twelve-month-olds point to share
attention and interest, not merely to get the object. When the adult attended to
the thing and shared some feeling about it, infants pointed more. They were
after the shared experience.

There is nothing to drill here. What supports it is following your baby's gaze
instead of redirecting it, naming what they are already interested in, and
reacting warmly when they show you a piece of fluff off the rug. The fluff is
not interesting. The offer is.

**Next step:** for a few minutes tomorrow, comment only on what your baby is
already looking at. Do not introduce anything.

## 11. You are part of the system

**Send:** Day 29
**Subject:** Your own steadiness is a developmental input
**Preview text:** Written to reduce guilt, not add any.

Every module of this course has said the same thing in a different way: the
mechanism of infant development is a responsive relationship. Which means the
state of the responsive adult is not a self-care aside bolted onto the end. It
is part of the mechanism.

Perinatal mood and anxiety conditions are common. In the United States, about
one in eight women with a recent live birth reported postpartum depressive
symptoms, 13.2 percent across thirty-one surveillance sites (Bauman et al.,
2020, MMWR). Anxiety occurs at a broadly comparable rate and often alongside.
Non-birthing parents, including fathers and adoptive parents, experience
perinatal depression at meaningful rates too, and that is under-recognised
almost everywhere.

Three things, in order of importance.

These conditions are highly treatable. They respond to specific psychotherapies,
to medication where appropriate, including options a prescriber can discuss with
you if you are breastfeeding, and to practical support. This is one of the more
treatable things in medicine and one of the least treated, because people do not
present.

This is not a character failure. Perinatal mood conditions occur in people who
desperately wanted to be parents and are working extremely hard. Feeling numb,
or frightened, or nothing at all, is a symptom.

And to be explicit, because Module 6 is explicit about it: the research linking
parental depression to child outcomes is correlational, describes small to
moderate group-level averages, and is heavily confounded by poverty, isolation,
and chronic illness. Most children of depressed parents do not develop the
outcomes studied. None of it is evidence that you have already harmed your baby.
It is a reason to treat this promptly, and that is the whole conclusion.

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, that is not
something to sit with alone. Contact your doctor or midwife, or go to an
emergency department. In the US the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline is available
by call or text at any hour.

**Next step:** ask one person for one specific, concrete thing this week. Two
hours on Saturday. One shop run. Something with edges.

## 12. Closing

**Send:** Day 32
**Subject:** What to keep, and what to put down
**Preview text:** The last email. There is no upsell in it.

You have reached the end of the sequence. Here is the short version of
everything.

What to keep. Contingency: your baby does something, you notice and respond,
they notice that you noticed. Following rather than directing, because a word
attached to the thing they already chose to look at lands somewhere. Repair,
which is not damage control but the actual mechanism, and which you already do
routinely without labelling it. And the specific clinical signposts, because
this course does not diagnose anything: loss of a skill your baby previously
had, no response to sound at any age, persistent stiffness or floppiness, strong
one-sidedness, and "something feels wrong and I cannot say why," which is a
legitimate reason to call.

What to put down. Word counts. Enrichment products, none of which have evidence
of improving developmental outcomes. The idea that a window is closing at one,
or at three. Guilt about twenty minutes of television. The belief that ordinary
caregiving is insufficient without a technique, which is a marketing position
rather than a finding.

Where to go next. Two things, because you do not need a reading list. The CDC's
"Learn the Signs. Act Early." programme has free milestone checklists by age, a
tracker app, and plain guidance on what to do with a concern. And your
pediatrician or family doctor, who is not an emergency resource but a
relationship over time. Bringing a worry early and being told it is nothing is
not a wasted appointment. That is the job.

Development is not a race with a finish line in the first year. Babies develop
in relationships that are responsive most of the time. Not all of the time.
Most of it.

You can put this down now. Your baby does not need you to have finished a
course. They need you to be there, mostly, imperfectly, tomorrow.

**Next step:** close this and go and be tired somewhere comfortable.
