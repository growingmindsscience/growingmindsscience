## Module 1 · What Is Actually Being Built

This module is about what is happening inside your baby's head during the first twelve months, and, just as importantly, what is not happening. You are going to hear a lot of claims this year. Some of them are true. Some of them are true but much smaller than they sound. A few of them are simply wrong, and they are the ones most likely to make you feel like you are already behind. The purpose of this module is to hand you an accurate picture of infant brain development so that the rest of the course, and the rest of your year, rests on something solid. By the end you should understand what your baby arrived already able to do, how experience turns into physical brain structure, what the actual active ingredient in that process is, and why the pressure you have probably been carrying about "windows closing" is heavier than the evidence justifies.

### Lesson 1.1 · The brain your baby was born with

Let us start by correcting an impression that almost everyone picks up somewhere, usually without noticing: the idea that a newborn is a blank, mostly empty system waiting to be filled in. That picture is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that matters, because it turns the first months into a race to install things. Your baby did not arrive empty. Your baby arrived with a set of systems that are already running, already gathering information, and already tuned toward exactly the kind of input a human infant reliably encounters. What looks like helplessness from the outside is, on the inside, an extraordinarily active intake operation.

Consider hearing. Hearing is functional well before birth. The auditory system is structurally in place during the third trimester, and by the time your baby is born they have already had weeks of exposure to sound filtered through the body: the rhythm and pitch contour of the voices around them, the beat of a heart, the muffled patterns of a language. Newborns show preference for the voice they heard most before birth and for the rhythmic shape of the language they were surrounded by. They are not understanding words. They are tracking rhythm, pitch, and melody, which is exactly the raw material a brain needs to eventually find word boundaries in a stream of speech. When you talk to your baby in the first week and it feels absurd because they cannot possibly understand you, remember that comprehension is not the point yet. The pattern is the point, and the pattern is landing.

Smell and taste are also working, and working well. Newborns orient toward the scent of their own caregiver's skin and milk. They show clear behavioral responses to sweet, and clear rejection responses to bitter. These are not decorative abilities. Smell in particular is one of the earliest routes by which a baby distinguishes the familiar from the unfamiliar, which is to say it is one of the earliest routes into relationship.

Touch is arguably the dominant sense of the newborn period. The skin is the largest sensory organ and it is mature enough at birth to carry an enormous amount of information: pressure, temperature, texture, movement, containment. Being held is not a comfort measure layered on top of the real developmental work. Being held is a stream of organized sensory input arriving at a system built to receive it, and it also regulates heart rate, temperature, and stress response. When people say that holding your baby is "doing something," this is the something.

Vision is the outlier, and it is the one worth understanding in detail because it is where the "blank slate" impression comes from. Newborn vision is genuinely poor by adult standards. Visual acuity is low, contrast sensitivity is limited, and the ability to focus at variable distances is not yet developed. The usual figure quoted is that a newborn sees most clearly at roughly eight to twelve inches. Treat that number as a rough description rather than a precise measurement, because it varies and because focal ability improves quickly over the first months. But the general point holds and is worth sitting with: the distance at which a newborn sees most clearly is approximately the distance from a feeding baby's eyes to the face of the person holding them. That is not a coincidence in any mystical sense. It is a system whose early limitations happen to point it directly at the most informative object in its environment, which is a human face.

Poor vision is not a deficiency to be corrected. It is a filter. A newborn who could see the whole room in sharp detail would be flooded. Instead, the visual system delivers a narrow, high-value slice: the face in front of them, high-contrast edges, movement. Over the first six months, acuity improves substantially, colour vision comes online, depth perception develops, and the baby graduates to taking in the wider world. The sequence is not a defect being repaired. It is a curriculum.

Then there are the reflexes. Rooting, sucking, grasping, the startle response, stepping. These are prewired motor programs that require no learning and, in most cases, fade over the first several months as voluntary control develops. It is easy to dismiss them as primitive leftovers. But look at what they accomplish. Rooting and sucking make feeding possible on day one. Grasping keeps a baby attached to a caregiver. The startle response is a rapid protective system. They are the bridge that carries a baby through the weeks before voluntary control arrives.

Here is the framing I want you to take from this lesson, and it will carry through the whole course. Your newborn's brain is not deficient. It is maximally receptive. It is built for intake rather than output. It has far more capacity to absorb than to act, and the asymmetry is deliberate. A system designed to learn an unknown language, an unknown face set, an unknown physical environment, and an unknown social world cannot arrive with those things preinstalled. It has to arrive open.

That reframe has a practical consequence. If your baby is a receiving system rather than an empty one, then your job in the early weeks is not to install content. It is to provide the ordinary human input the system is already expecting: your face, your voice, your touch, your responses. That is not a lowering of the bar. It is an accurate description of where the bar actually is, and it happens to be a place that exhausted people holding a newborn are already standing.

One more thing, because it comes up constantly. Newborns spend a great deal of time asleep or in states that are not alert. That is not lost time. Sleep is when a great deal of the consolidating and organizing happens. You do not need to wake a baby to stimulate them. The intake and the processing are both part of the same job, and your baby is doing both without instruction.

### Lesson 1.2 · How experience builds architecture

Now we get to the mechanism. How does something that happens in a room, a voice or a face or a touch, become physical structure inside a skull?

The unit to know is the synapse: the junction where one neuron passes a signal to the next. Thought, memory, perception, and movement all run across synapses. What is remarkable about early development is not that synapses form, but the pace and the excess.

Peter Huttenlocher's work, beginning in the late 1970s, examined synaptic density in human cortex across the lifespan using postmortem tissue. What he found reshaped the field. Synapse formation in infancy is not gradual. It is explosive, and it substantially overshoots the adult level. Different cortical regions peak at different times, which turns out to be one of the most important findings in the whole literature. Visual cortex reaches peak synaptic density early, within roughly the first year. The prefrontal cortex, which handles planning, inhibition, and self regulation, peaks considerably later and takes far longer to settle toward adult density. The brain does not mature all at once. It matures region by region, on a schedule.

So the first year is a period of massive synaptogenesis. The brain is producing far more connections than it will ultimately keep. Then, over the years that follow, comes pruning: connections that are used repeatedly are stabilized and strengthened, and connections that go unused are eliminated. The forest path analogy is the usual one and it is a good one. A route walked daily becomes a trail. A route nobody walks grows over. Pruning is not damage. It is how a general purpose system becomes an efficient specific one, tuned to the actual world it landed in.

Running alongside this is myelination. Myelin is a fatty sheath that wraps neural fibers and dramatically increases the speed and reliability of signal transmission. Think of the insulation on a wire. Myelination proceeds on its own long timeline, beginning before birth and continuing into the mid twenties, and, crucially, it follows the same regional order. Sensory and motor pathways myelinate early. The prefrontal cortex is among the last.

That regional ordering gives us the two rules that explain most of what you will observe this year. Development goes bottom up, from the older subcortical and brainstem systems that handle arousal, state, and basic regulation, upward toward cortex. And it goes back to front, from sensory and motor areas toward the prefrontal cortex at the very front, which finishes last. Your baby will be able to see, hear, reach, roll, and feel intensely long before they can wait, inhibit, or self soothe. That is not a temperament problem or a discipline problem. It is the construction sequence.

Now the distinction that does the most work in this module: experience-expectant versus experience-dependent development.

Experience-expectant development covers circuits that evolution has effectively bet on. The brain arrives expecting a particular class of input because every normally situated human infant reliably encounters it: patterned light, sound, touch, movement, human faces. These systems are built to be finished by ordinary experience, and because the input is so reliably available, evolution did not bother building the wiring in advance. It built the expectation instead.

The classic evidence here comes from David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel, working with kittens across the 1960s and 1970s in research that later earned a Nobel Prize. They found that if one eye was deprived of patterned visual input during a specific early period, the cortical territory that would normally have been devoted to that eye was substantially taken over by the other eye, and the deprivation produced lasting functional impairment. Restoring input later did not fully undo it. Deprivation outside that early period did not produce the same effect. This is the strongest and cleanest demonstration of a sensitive period we have: for that particular system, in that particular window, ordinary input was necessary, and its absence was not fully recoverable later.

I want to be precise about what that does and does not license. Hubel and Wiesel demonstrated that the absence of basic patterned input damages the visual system. They did not demonstrate that extra visual input builds a better one. The finding is about a floor, not a ceiling. This distinction gets collapsed constantly in popular parenting material, and the collapse is where most of the pressure comes from.

Experience-dependent development is the other category, and it is where the individual life shows up. These are circuits shaped by the specific experiences of a specific baby: the particular language spoken around them, the particular faces they see, the particular routines and relationships and responses they live inside. Experience-dependent learning is not governed by a narrow window. It continues, in some form, across the entire lifespan. It is elevated in early childhood, certainly. But it does not shut down.

One well documented example sits right at the boundary between the two. Janet Werker and Richard Tees, in a 1984 study published in Infant Behavior and Development, showed that infants around six months could discriminate speech sound contrasts that do not exist in the language spoken around them, while by roughly ten to twelve months that ability had substantially narrowed toward the sounds of their own language. This is called perceptual narrowing, and it is a beautiful demonstration of the whole principle. The baby starts general and becomes specific. The specialization is a gain, not a loss: becoming an expert in the language you actually need is worth more than staying an unfocused generalist. Note also what drove it. Not instruction, not drilling, not a product. Ordinary exposure to ordinary speech.

So the architecture story is this. Overproduce, then shape by use. Insulate on a long schedule. Build from the bottom up and the back to the front. Expect basic human input to arrive, and use the specific input that does arrive to specialize. Nothing in that sequence requires you to accelerate anything. It requires input of a kind you are almost certainly already providing.

### Lesson 1.3 · Serve and return: the actual mechanism

If the previous lesson explained how experience becomes structure, this one is about which experiences carry the most weight, and the answer is narrower and more encouraging than most people expect.

The framework worth learning is the one developed by the Center on the Developing Child at Harvard, usually called serve and return. The metaphor is a rally in tennis. Your baby serves: a look, a coo, a reach, a babble, a change in expression, a cry. You return: you look back, you answer, you name the thing they are looking at, you pick them up, you make the sound back. They serve again. The exchange continues until one of you stops.

That is the whole model, and its plainness is the point. There is no technique to acquire and no equipment involved. But underneath it sits the finding that I think is the single most useful thing in this entire course: the active ingredient is contingency.

Contingency means that what you do depends on what your baby just did, and closely enough in time that they can tell. It is the difference between talking near your baby and talking with your baby. Both involve words. Only one gives the baby evidence that their own behavior has an effect on the world.

Why does contingency matter so much? Because it is the mechanism by which a baby learns causality, and causality is the foundation under almost everything else. An infant does not arrive knowing that they are an agent. They learn it by producing a signal and observing a reliable, timely consequence. Sound comes out, face appears. Reach happens, object arrives. Distress happens, comfort arrives. Repeated thousands of times across ordinary days, that pattern teaches something enormous: I act, and the world responds. That belief is the seed of communication, of persistence, of exploration, and of the expectation that other people are reachable.

There are three practical implications, and they are the reason this lesson exists.

The first is that timing beats volume. A prompt response to something your baby actually did carries more information than a large amount of input unconnected to anything they did. This is why background talk, television, and audio playing in the room do not substitute for interaction. They are not contingent. Nothing you say to a screen changes what it does next.

The second is that following beats directing. Contingency requires that you respond to your baby's focus, not redirect it to yours. If your baby is staring at the ceiling fan, the developmentally rich move is to talk about the ceiling fan, not to wave a flashcard in front of them. Attention is the limiting resource. When you name the thing they are already looking at, you hand them a word already attached to a meaning they had themselves selected. When you name the thing you want them to look at, you first have to fight for the attention, and the word arrives attached to nothing.

The third is that ordinary caregiving is already dense with these exchanges. Feeding, changing, bathing, and dressing all involve close faces, turn taking, and response. You do not need to add a serve and return session to your day. You need to notice that most of your day already is one.

Now the evidence, honestly stated.

Tamis-LeMonda, Bornstein and Baumwell, in a 2001 paper in Child Development, followed infants and measured maternal responsiveness, then tracked when children reached specific language milestones. Higher responsiveness predicted earlier achievement of those milestones. It is a well conducted study and the finding has held up in shape across the literature. But it is longitudinal and correlational.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** The responsiveness-to-language link is correlational. Responsive caregivers may differ from less responsive caregivers in many ways, including circumstances, resources, available time, stress load, mental health, and heritable characteristics shared with the child. The study cannot establish that responsiveness caused the earlier milestones. This must not be written as "responding to your baby makes them talk sooner."

What upgrades this from an interesting correlation to something you can act on is intervention evidence. Bakermans-Kranenburg, van IJzendoorn and Juffer published a meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin in 2003, pooling randomized intervention studies designed to increase caregiver sensitivity. Interventions that successfully increased sensitivity did produce measurable improvements in caregiving behavior, and improvements in sensitivity were associated with improvements in infant attachment security. That is experimental evidence, and it is the reason this is not simply an observation about which parents happen to have which babies. Sensitivity is trainable, and training it changes something real.

Two honest qualifications. Those interventions were often targeted at families in difficulty, so the effect sizes may not transfer directly to a family already doing well. And the meta-analysis found that shorter, more behaviorally focused interventions often outperformed longer, more elaborate ones, which is a quietly encouraging finding: what works is specific and modest, not total.

One last thing, and it matters more than any of the mechanism. You do not need to be contingent all the time. You cannot be. Nobody is, and the research does not ask for it. The relevant literature on caregiver responsiveness has repeatedly found that even in well functioning interactions, caregiver and infant are out of sync a substantial proportion of the time, and that the repair, the return to connection after the mismatch, is itself a meaningful part of the process. A ratio, not a record. Missing a serve is not damage. Missing every serve, over months, in a way that never recovers, is a different situation, and that is what the next lesson is about.

### Lesson 1.4 · Plasticity, windows, and the pressure you can put down

This is the lesson where we take something off your plate.

You have almost certainly encountered some version of the claim that the first three years determine everything, that windows are closing, that what you do now is permanent. It appears in marketing, in well meaning advice, and in genuinely well intentioned public health messaging. It has produced an enormous amount of parental anxiety. It is also an overclaim, and this course will not make it.

Let us separate what is true from what has been stretched.

Sensitive periods are real. They are real for specific systems, on specific timelines, and the evidence for them is good. Hubel and Wiesel's visual deprivation work established one definitively. If patterned visual input is absent during the early sensitive period, the visual cortex reorganizes in ways that later input does not fully reverse. In humans, the practical parallel is congenital cataract: outcomes depend meaningfully on how early it is treated. This is the reason newborn vision screening exists and the reason it matters.

Native phonology is another. Werker and Tees showed the narrowing of speech sound discrimination between roughly six and twelve months. Something real is being tuned on a schedule there.

So yes: for basic sensory systems and for some aspects of speech perception, timing genuinely matters. Note the shape of every one of those cases. Each concerns whether basic, universally available input was present at all. None of them concerns whether input was optimized, enriched, or maximized. Sensitive period research is about floors.

Now the overclaim. "The first three years determine everything" makes three separate errors at once.

It generalizes from sensory systems to everything else. Vision and native phonology have relatively tight timelines. Vocabulary, reasoning, emotional regulation, and social skill do not. People acquire second languages, recover from adversity, form new secure attachments, and develop executive function well into adulthood. Experience-dependent plasticity does not switch off at thirty-six months. There is no biological event at that age.

It confuses deprivation with variation. This is the important one, and it deserves the clearest example available.

The Bucharest Early Intervention Project, led by Charles Nelson, Charles Zeanah, Nathan Fox and colleagues, with major findings published in Science in 2007, studied children raised in Romanian institutions under conditions of severe deprivation: minimal individual caregiving, very high child to caregiver ratios, almost no contingent responsiveness. The design was randomized. Institutionalized children were randomly assigned either to continued institutional care or to a high quality foster care programme created for the study, and both groups were compared with never institutionalized community children.

The findings were stark. Institutionalized children showed substantially reduced cognitive functioning compared with community children. Children moved to foster care showed significant recovery relative to those who remained institutionalized. And age at placement mattered: children placed earlier generally did better, with the strongest effects for children placed before roughly two years of age, though recovery was not confined to that group and outcomes varied by domain. Some domains, notably attachment, showed considerable recovery. Others, including some measures of brain activity and attention, showed more persistent effects.

This is real, important, and often misused.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** The leap from institutional deprivation findings to ordinary parenting is the central overclaim of the entire early brain development literature, and it is made constantly. BEIP studied the near total absence of individualized care: a child with almost no contingent responsive interaction with any adult across years. That is not the low end of ordinary parenting. It is a different kind of condition entirely. Nothing in BEIP supports any inference about differences between a more attentive and a less attentive parent in an ordinary home, and no sentence in this course should imply otherwise.

The honest reading of BEIP runs the other way from how it is usually deployed. It shows that severe deprivation causes real harm, that placing a child into responsive individualized care produces substantial recovery, and that recovery is possible even after years of deprivation. It is an argument for intervention and for hope. It is not an argument that your ordinary Tuesday is high stakes.

The third error in "the first three years determine everything" is that it mistakes an early advantage for a permanent one. Early development matters because it is foundational, and foundations affect what is built on top. But "foundational" does not mean "fixed." Later experience continues to shape the system.

Where does that leave the toxic stress framework? Shonkoff and colleagues, in a 2012 policy statement in Pediatrics for the American Academy of Pediatrics, set out the model that severe, chronic, unbuffered stress in early childhood can affect developing stress response systems with downstream effects on health and learning. The distinction the framework itself draws is the one that matters. Positive stress is brief and manageable. Tolerable stress is more serious but buffered by a responsive caregiver. Toxic stress is severe, prolonged, and unbuffered. The caregiver relationship is what does the buffering, which means the framework's own logic is that the presence of a responsive adult is protective. Your baby crying while you finish something, or having a hard day, or being upset while you cannot immediately get to them, is nowhere near this category.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** ACEs scores are frequently attached to this framework and used as though they predict an individual child's outcome. They do not. The ACEs literature reports population-level associations between adversity counts and aggregate outcome rates, with wide individual variation and many people at high scores showing no adverse outcome. An ACE score is not a diagnosis, not a prognosis, and not a valid individual-level screening instrument. If ACEs are mentioned anywhere in this course, this limitation must be stated in the same breath.

So here is the pressure you can put down.

You are not in a race. The relevant question in the first year is almost never "am I doing enough of the right stimulation" and almost always "is my baby in a relationship with someone who notices them and responds." That is a much lower bar and a much more achievable one, and it is also the one the evidence actually supports.

Missed moments are not permanent. The system is designed for redundancy. A baby has thousands of interactions a week. No individual one is load bearing.

Days that go badly do not accumulate into damage. Repair is part of the process, not a correction to it.

And plasticity is on your side, not against you. It is usually presented as a threat: the brain is plastic now, so act before it stops. Read it the other way, which is the way the evidence supports. Plasticity is why recovery is possible. It is why the Bucharest children who were moved improved. It is why a hard month, a period of parental illness, a return to work, or a stretch of depression is not a verdict on a child. The same property that makes early experience matter is the property that makes later experience matter too.

If you are worried about something specific, that worry deserves a real answer from a real clinician, and this course will keep telling you when to seek one. But generalized dread about whether you are building the brain correctly is not a useful signal, and it is not one the science supports. Put it down.

### Try this week

**1. The eight inch face.**
*What to do:* Once a day, when your baby is calm and alert, hold them at roughly the distance from your chest to your face, around eight to twelve inches, and just look at them. Talk if you like. Do not perform.
*What you are looking for:* Sustained gaze, quieting of movement, changes in expression, possibly a widening of the eyes or a slowing of breathing. In older babies, smiles and vocalizations.
*If nothing happens:* Very likely a state issue rather than a skill issue. Newborns are only in a quiet alert state for short stretches, and a baby who is drowsy, hungry, or overstimulated will disengage. Try a different point in the day. Persistent lack of any visual response over weeks, or eyes that never seem to fix on anything, is worth mentioning to your pediatrician, which is a screening question, not an alarm.

**2. Count one rally.**
*What to do:* Once this week, when your baby makes any sound or gesture, respond, then wait. Count how many back and forth turns you get before it ends. That is all. You are not trying to extend it.
*What you are looking for:* Any turn taking at all. Two exchanges is a real rally. Newborns may manage only one. Babies around three to four months often manage several.
*If nothing happens:* Turn taking emerges gradually and is heavily state dependent. Also check your own pacing: adults routinely respond faster than an infant can process, which ends the rally by accident. Try leaving a genuinely uncomfortable pause, several seconds, before you fill the silence.

**3. Follow, do not lead.**
*What to do:* For five minutes, notice what your baby is already looking at, and talk about that thing. Do not introduce a toy. Do not redirect. Whatever has their attention is the topic.
*What you are looking for:* Longer attention to the object than usual, glances back at you, or increased vocalizing. You may notice they hold the gaze longer when you narrate it.
*If nothing happens:* Nothing is wrong. Young infants often have no sustained external focus at all, and this exercise gets much easier around four to six months. The point of the exercise is partly to retrain you, and that part works regardless of the response.

**4. Notice the input already there.**
*What to do:* Pick one routine you do every day, a feed, a nappy change, a bath, and pay attention to how much serve and return it already contains without any effort from you.
*What you are looking for:* Eye contact, your automatic narration, your responses to their sounds and movements, the timing of it.
*If nothing happens:* If a routine feels flat or purely functional, that is normal and it is not a failure. Some feeds are just feeds. Some days you are running on nothing. The routines are not a performance and they do not all need to be rich.

**5. Put one thing down.**
*What to do:* Identify one developmental worry you are currently carrying, write it in a sentence, and ask two questions of it. Is this about the absence of something basic, or about the absence of something optimal? And is there an actual clinical question inside it?
*What you are looking for:* Most worries turn out to be about optimization, and this module is the argument that optimization is not the relevant variable. Those you can put down.
*If nothing happens:* If the worry survives the two questions and there is a genuine clinical question inside it, hearing, vision, feeding, tone, responsiveness, that is exactly the kind of thing to raise with your pediatrician. A specific question answered by a professional is a better use of your energy than general dread.

### What this module is not saying

**It is not saying there is a thirty million word gap you need to close.** The Hart and Risley word gap claim is contested. Sperry, Sperry and Miller (2019) failed to replicate the original finding, and the original study was based on a very small sample. Nothing in this module asks you to count words or increase word volume. The evidence points to the quality and contingency of interaction, not to quantity.

**It is not saying classical music makes babies smarter.** The Mozart effect has no supporting evidence in infants. The original adult finding was a brief and small effect on a specific spatial task and did not concern babies at all. Play music because you want music in your house.

**It is not saying a critical window closes at three.** Sensitive periods are real for specific systems such as vision and native speech sound discrimination. They are not real as a general deadline for cognitive or emotional development. There is no biological event at thirty-six months, and plasticity continues throughout life.

**It is not saying enrichment products build better brains.** No baby video, flashcard set, black and white card pack, developmental toy subscription, or educational app has evidence of improving developmental outcomes. Educational baby videos in particular have been tested: DeLoache and colleagues (2010) found no word learning from them. The evidence base concerns responsive human interaction, and no product substitutes for it.

**It is not saying more stimulation is better.** Every sensitive period finding concerns the presence or absence of basic input, not its optimization. There is no demonstrated dose response relationship where more input produces a better brain, and there is good reason to think overstimulation is its own problem.

**It is not saying your ordinary parenting resembles the Bucharest conditions in any degree.** Severe institutional deprivation is different in kind, not in degree, from ordinary variation in parenting.

**It is not saying you have to get it right most of the time.** Mismatch and repair are normal components of interaction, not deviations from it.

### Module summary

- Your baby was born with hearing, smell, taste, and touch already functional, and with vision that is poor by adult standards but well suited to seeing a caregiver's face at close range.
- The newborn brain is maximally receptive rather than deficient. It is built for intake before output, and that asymmetry is deliberate.
- Reflexes such as rooting, sucking, and grasping are prewired programs that carry a baby through the period before voluntary control develops.
- The brain massively overproduces synapses in early life, then prunes based on use. Huttenlocher's work showed that different regions peak at different times.
- Myelination speeds up signal transmission and continues into the mid twenties, following the same regional order.
- Development runs bottom up and back to front. The prefrontal cortex, which handles planning and self regulation, finishes last by years.
- Experience-expectant development covers circuits waiting on universal input such as light and speech. Experience-dependent development covers circuits shaped by the particular life a baby lives, and it continues for life.
- Serve and return is the core mechanism, and the active ingredient is contingency: your response depending on what your baby just did, closely enough in time for them to notice.
- Timing and following your baby's focus matter more than volume of input or amount of enrichment.
- Sensitive periods are real for vision and native phonology. They concern whether basic input was present at all, not whether it was optimized.
- Severe deprivation, as studied in the Bucharest Early Intervention Project, is different in kind from ordinary parenting variation, and that study also showed substantial recovery when children were placed in responsive care.
- Plasticity is on your side. It is the reason recovery is possible, not a deadline you are racing.
