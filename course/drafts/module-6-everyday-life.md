## Module 6 · Everyday Life

This is the last module, and it is deliberately the most ordinary one. The first five modules looked at what is being built, how to read your baby, how language arrives, how security forms, and how bodies learn to move. This one is about the container all of that happens inside: play, attention, routines, screens, and you. There is no new technique here that you have been missing. The point of this module is closer to the opposite. It is to show you that the things you are already doing, badly and inconsistently and while tired, are the mechanism. And then, at the end, to put down the pressure.

### Lesson 6.1 · Play is the curriculum

If you have ever watched a baby take a wooden spoon, put it in their mouth, take it out, look at it, bang it on the floor, put it back in their mouth, and then drop it over the side of the high chair for the eleventh time, you have watched a research programme in progress. It does not look like one. It looks like a baby who has run out of ideas. But the repetition is the point, and understanding why changes how the whole first year looks.

Infant play in the first twelve months moves through a rough sequence, and the sequence tells you what the baby is currently working on. In the early months, play is mostly sensory and mostly oral. Babies mouth things. This is not a phase to be tolerated until it passes; the mouth is, at that age, one of the best instruments a baby has. It has extraordinary sensory resolution, better than the hands for a while, and mouthing an object gives back information about texture, temperature, hardness, and edges that a hand cannot yet gather. A baby mouthing a set of measuring cups is doing material science with the best tool available.

Then, somewhere in the middle of the first year as reaching and grasping consolidate, play becomes percussive. Babies bang. They bang things on the floor, on the tray, on each other. Banging is a beautifully efficient experiment: one action, and you learn about weight, resonance, rigidity, and what a given surface does when struck. A wooden block and a plastic lid make different sounds, and a baby who bangs both is testing whether the sound belongs to the object or to the action. Notice that they will often try the same object on two different surfaces. That is not perseveration. That is a controlled comparison.

Later in the year, play gets relational. Things go in other things and come back out. This is container play, and it tends to arrive alongside the motor control needed to release an object on purpose, which is a genuinely harder skill than grasping one. Dropping is the same discovery from the other end. The famous over-the-high-chair-side drop, repeated until an adult loses composure, is a baby testing whether the result holds: does it always fall, does it fall the same way, does it fall if I let go with a different hand, and (this part matters) does the adult always retrieve it. That last question is a social experiment running inside the physical one.

Underneath all of it sits one of the most famous ideas in developmental psychology. Piaget (1954) described the gradual construction of object permanence, the understanding that objects continue to exist when they are out of sight. His observations included a striking error. Hide a toy under cloth A, let the baby find it a few times, then, in full view of the baby, hide it under cloth B. Babies around eight to ten months will often reach back to A. This is the A-not-B error, and for decades it was told as evidence that the baby did not really have a concept of the object as a persisting thing.

The modern account is more interesting and, honestly, more forgiving. Diamond's work on this task argues that the A-not-B error is not a missing concept at all. It is a collision between memory and habit. The baby has to hold the new location in mind across a delay while also inhibiting a motor reach that has already been rewarded several times. Lengthen the delay and errors go up. Shorten it and they go down. Reduce how many times the baby succeeded at A and the pull toward A weakens. What is developing is not the idea that objects exist. It is the working memory to hold a location and the inhibitory control to override a well-practised movement, both of which are maturing rapidly across the second half of the first year. So when your nine-month-old watches you move something and then reaches for the old spot, you are not watching a conceptual gap. You are watching a young braking system.

There is a further and more contested layer. Baillargeon and colleagues developed violation-of-expectation methods, in which infants are shown physically impossible events (a screen rotating through the space where a solid block should be, for instance) and their looking times are measured. Infants reliably look longer at the impossible event, which has been interpreted as evidence that object knowledge is present far earlier than reaching tasks suggest, sometimes in the first few months.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Violation-of-expectation infant cognition is in active replication dispute. The looking-time phenomena are real and repeatedly observed, but the interpretation (that longer looking indicates conceptual knowledge rather than perceptual novelty or familiarity effects) is contested, effect sizes in replications have often been smaller than the originals, and several high-profile infant cognition findings have not survived large multi-lab replication. This paragraph should be presented as an open question and must not be written as settled fact about what babies "know."

What matters for you as a parent is that both accounts point the same direction: your baby is doing far more than they can show you, and the gap between what they understand and what they can execute is normal and temporary.

Which brings us to toys. The honest version is that the developmental value of a toy is roughly inverse to how much it does on its own. A toy that lights up, plays a tune, and says a number when a button is pressed has already made every interesting decision. The baby's contribution is the button. A wooden spoon, a set of nesting cups, a cardboard box, a scarf, a colander, a ball, an empty plastic bottle with the cap glued on: these do nothing until the baby acts on them, and then they do exactly what the baby's action makes them do. That contingency, action in and result out, is the thing worth buying, and it is free.

None of this means expensive toys are harmful. They are fine. Babies enjoy them. The claim this course refuses is the marketing one, that a purchase adds developmental value your kitchen drawer cannot. There is no good evidence for that. If a toy buys you fifteen minutes to eat a meal standing up, that is a real and legitimate benefit and you do not need to justify it developmentally.

The last thing to say about play in the first year is about your role in it. You are not required to be an activity director. A baby playing on the floor near you while you fold laundry is having a completely adequate experience, and periodically looking up, catching your eye, and getting a look back is a substantial part of what they are getting out of it. Which is, conveniently, exactly what the next lesson is about.

### Lesson 6.2 · Joint attention, the quiet milestone

Most first-year milestone lists are about the body. Rolling, sitting, crawling, cruising, first steps, first words. There is one milestone that almost never makes those lists, that has no obvious physical marker, that most parents notice without ever knowing it has a name, and that developmental scientists take extremely seriously. It is joint attention.

Here is the shift. For the early months, a baby's social life is essentially two-way. You look at them, they look at you, you make a face, they make one back, you talk, they coo. This is dyadic attention: two participants, and the object of attention is each other. It is warm and it is enormously important, and it is also not yet the full thing.

Then, over the second half of the first year, a third element enters. The baby starts attending to an object and to you at the same time, and, crucially, seems to understand that you are also attending to it. This is triadic attention: baby, adult, and thing, with the baby aware that the attention is shared. It sounds small. It is one of the most consequential reorganisations of the first year, because it is the moment a baby's world stops being a series of two-way exchanges and becomes a place where experiences can be pointed at, offered, and held in common.

You can watch it arrive in a rough sequence, though as always the range is wide.

Following a gaze comes first, generally somewhere in the middle of the year. You look away, deliberately, toward something across the room, and your baby's eyes track to where you looked rather than staying on your face. Early on this works best when the target is close, in front of the baby, and visually interesting on its own. Later it works for targets further away and behind the baby, which is a genuinely harder inference.

Following a point comes next. You extend your arm, and instead of looking at your hand (which is the fascinating thing at close range) your baby looks along the line to where you are indicating. Understanding that a finger is not the point but a vector toward the point is a real cognitive achievement.

Then, typically somewhere around nine to twelve months, and with substantial normal variation on either side, comes the one that tends to stop parents in their tracks: the baby produces a point of their own. And notably, the earliest points are frequently not requests. Liszkowski, Carpenter, Henning, Striano and Tomasello (2004) examined what twelve-month-olds are doing when they point, and found that infants pointed to share attention and interest with an adult, not merely to obtain the object. When the adult responded by attending to the object and sharing emotion about it, infants pointed more. When the adult attended only to the infant, or only to the object, or misread it, pointing dropped off. The infants were not after the thing. They were after the shared experience of the thing, and they could tell the difference.

Alongside pointing you will usually see showing (holding an object up toward you without releasing it), giving (releasing it into your hand, often repeatedly), and social referencing (encountering something ambiguous, a stranger, a loud noise, a new surface, and looking to your face to find out how to feel about it). All of these are the same underlying capacity wearing different clothes: my experience and your experience can be about the same thing.

Carpenter, Nagell and Tomasello (1998), in a longitudinal monograph tracking infants across the first two years, mapped how these joint attentional skills emerge and how they relate to early communicative and language development. Mundy and Newell (2007) later summarised why the field treats joint attention as a hinge: it sits at the intersection of attention regulation and social cognition, and it is the platform on which word learning is built. That last part is worth spelling out. To learn that a sound refers to a thing, a baby has to work out which thing the speaker means. Joint attention is precisely the machinery for that. Without it, every new word would be a guess against an infinite list of candidates.

This is where I want to be careful, because this is exactly the point at which parenting content usually goes wrong.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Joint attention's reputation as "one of the most predictive things in the first year" derives substantially from autism-risk research, including prospective infant-sibling designs where reduced or atypically developing joint attention is studied as an early marker. Those are group-level findings in enriched-risk samples. This lesson must not be written in a way that converts joint attention into a home screening instrument, and must not imply that a baby who points late is on a trajectory. Recommend the human reviewer check the framing of this section line by line for exactly that drift.

So let me say the practical part plainly. There is nothing to drill. Joint attention is not a skill you train through exercises, and a baby who is pointed at repeatedly by an anxious adult does not develop it faster. What supports it is the ordinary stuff: responding when your baby looks at something, following their gaze rather than always redirecting to yours, naming what they are already interested in instead of what you would prefer they were interested in, and reacting with some warmth when they show you a piece of fluff they found on the rug. The fluff is not interesting. The offer is. Take the offer.

Two more honest notes. First, the timeline genuinely varies, and a baby who is not pointing at twelve months is not thereby a baby with a problem. Plenty are pointing at fourteen months and everything is fine. Second, and this is different: if joint attention behaviours are not appearing at all by around fifteen to eighteen months, if your baby does not follow a point, does not show you things, does not check your face when something surprising happens, or if you have the sense that they have become less socially connected than they used to be, that is worth raising with your pediatrician. Not as an emergency, and not as a diagnosis. As information, given to the person whose job it is to have the full picture. Bringing a concern is always reasonable, and being told everything looks fine is a perfectly good outcome of a conversation, not a wasted appointment.

### Lesson 6.3 · Routines, stress, and buffering

Babies are not born with a working nervous-system thermostat. Adults have one, more or less: we can be startled, ramp up, and come back down without much help. A newborn cannot. Regulation, in the first year, is not something the baby does. It is something that happens between the baby and an adult, and the baby gradually takes over the parts they can manage.

This is why predictability matters, and it is worth being precise about why, because "routine" gets sold in ways that are both rigid and slightly menacing.

A predictable environment lowers the load on a system that is trying to make sense of a chaotic world. When roughly the same things happen in roughly the same order, a baby's brain starts to generate expectations, and expectations do two useful things. They reduce the amount of surprise that has to be processed moment to moment, and they let the baby begin to anticipate rather than only react. A baby who recognises the sequence of bath, pyjamas, feed, dark room is not just being managed. They are running a prediction, and a met prediction is calming in a way that has nothing to do with discipline.

Note what this does not require. It does not require a clock-based schedule, it does not require identical timings, and it does not fail because you went out and everything ran late. Predictability in the first year is mostly about sequence and quality, not precision. The order of events and the manner of the adult carry most of it. A day that happens ninety minutes later than usual but in the usual order, with the usual person doing it in the usual way, is a predictable day.

Now the harder material, and the term that has been most thoroughly mangled in the last decade.

Shonkoff, Garner and colleagues (2012), in an American Academy of Pediatrics policy statement, laid out a three-way framework for stress in early childhood that has been widely adopted, including by the Center on the Developing Child at Harvard.

**Positive stress** is brief, moderate physiological arousal in the presence of a supportive adult. A vaccination. A stranger leaning in too fast. The first bath. Heart rate rises, cortisol rises, the adult responds, the system comes back down. This is not merely tolerable. It is the ordinary way a stress response system gets calibrated. A baby who never experienced arousal and recovery would not thereby be better off.

**Tolerable stress** is a more serious and more sustained activation: a genuine adversity, a serious illness, a bereavement, a frightening event, a period of real family upheaval. The defining feature is not the severity of the event. It is that the response is buffered by a stable, responsive adult relationship and is time-limited enough for the system to recover. Hard things happen to babies and their families, and hard things happen inside a relationship that holds.

**Toxic stress** in this framework means something specific: strong, frequent, or prolonged activation of the stress response systems in the absence of adequate adult buffering. The absence is the operative clause. It is not a description of an event. It is a description of an event plus the failure of the protective relationship around it. The framework's canonical examples are chronic neglect, ongoing abuse, caregiver substance dependence or severe mental illness that is untreated and unsupported, and the accumulated grind of severe deprivation.

I want to be blunt about the implication, because this is one of the most common sources of unnecessary parental fear that exists.

Your bad day is not toxic stress. Your baby crying in the car seat for twenty minutes on the motorway because you cannot pull over is not toxic stress. Losing your temper, putting the baby down safely, and going to breathe in the hallway for two minutes is not toxic stress; it is, if anything, a fairly good outcome. Daycare is not toxic stress. Your baby being upset while you shower is not toxic stress. The term has been lifted out of its framework and applied to ordinary distress in a way that its own authors do not support, and the effect on parents has been to make normal difficulty feel like documented damage.

The active ingredient in the whole framework is the buffering adult. And here is what "buffering" actually looks like, because it is much less impressive than it sounds. It looks like a baby who is upset having a person who notices, comes, and stays. Not a person who prevents the distress. Not a person who resolves it instantly. Not a person who never contributes to it. A person who is reliably available on the other side of it. Regulation is transmitted through repetition of that sequence, thousands of times, most of them unremarkable.

Which is also why repair matters more than perfection, a point Module 4 covered in depth and that is worth restating here: the relevant unit is not the individual interaction. It is the pattern.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** The positive/tolerable/toxic framework is a synthesising model built on animal stress-physiology work, human observational studies, and adversity research. It is well supported as an organising framework and is the AAP's own language, but the boundaries between the three categories are conceptual rather than empirically measured thresholds, and there is no biomarker that sorts a given child into a category. Reviewer should confirm this module never implies the categories can be assessed at home.

One further correction belongs here, because it travels with the toxic-stress material and does more harm than almost anything else in this space. Adverse Childhood Experience (ACE) scores are frequently presented to parents as though a number predicts a child's future. They do not do that. The ACEs literature is a population-level epidemiological association: across large groups, higher cumulative adversity is associated with higher average rates of a range of later health and social outcomes. That association is real. But applied to an individual child, an ACE score performs poorly as a prediction. Most people with high scores do not develop the outcomes in question, and many people with low scores do. A score is not a prognosis, it is not a diagnosis, and no one should be handing you one as a statement about your baby's life.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** ACEs as individual-level prediction. This paragraph is deliberately strong. The underlying point (that population-level risk associations have poor individual-level discriminative accuracy) is well established methodologically, but the specific framing should be checked by the reviewer so it is not read as dismissing the reality of childhood adversity, which is not the intent.

### Lesson 6.4 · Screens, and the honest version

Almost every parent of a baby has had a version of the same evening. Something has to get done, or you have simply run out, and a screen is right there, and afterwards there is a low background hum of having done something you were told not to do. This lesson is an attempt to replace that hum with an actual mechanism, because the mechanism is genuinely interesting and, unlike the guilt, it is useful.

Start with what the guidance says. The American Academy of Pediatrics Council on Communications and Media (2016) recommends that children younger than 18 to 24 months avoid solo use of screen media, with an explicit exception for video chat, and that if parents of children 18 to 24 months want to introduce digital media, they choose high-quality programming and watch it with the child rather than leaving the child to watch alone. Note two things about that. It is a guideline, not an experimental result, and its central distinction is not screen versus no screen. It is alone versus with you.

That distinction is not arbitrary. It comes from one of the more robust findings in early media research, and it is called the video deficit effect.

The finding, developed in work by Anderson and Pempek (2005) and in a substantial body of research by Barr and colleagues, is this: infants and young toddlers learn markedly less from a video demonstration than from a live demonstration of the same content. Show a baby a live person performing a novel action with an object and the baby is much more likely to reproduce it than if the identical action is shown on a screen. The same gap appears for word learning, for imitation, and for object-retrieval tasks. Under roughly two years of age, the video version is a substantially weaker teacher, and the gap narrows across the third year.

Why? Several contributors, and they matter for what follows. Two-dimensional images give poorer depth and size information for a perceptual system still calibrating. Video lacks contingency: a screen does not respond to your baby's specific look, sound, or reach, and the first year runs heavily on contingency. And there is a transfer problem, in that connecting something seen on a flat, glowing rectangle to the real three-dimensional object in front of you is a non-trivial inference for a young brain.

Once you know that, the AAP's video chat exception stops looking like a loophole and starts looking like the exact consequence of the mechanism. A grandparent on a video call is contingent. They respond to your baby, by name, in real time, to what your baby just did. That is why it is carved out.

The mechanism also explains DeLoache et al. (2010), which remains one of the cleanest results in this area. Infants were assigned to conditions including watching a commercially marketed "educational" baby DVD over several weeks, with and without parent involvement, alongside a condition in which parents simply taught the same words in everyday interaction, plus a control. The infants who watched the DVD learned no more of the target words than infants who did not watch it. Parent teaching was the condition that produced learning. This is the source of this course's hard ban on the claim that educational baby videos teach words. They do not, and the finding is a null, not an ambiguity.

Now the part that requires more care: the observational literature on screen time and developmental outcomes.

Madigan, Browne, Racine, Mori and Tough (2019), published in JAMA Pediatrics, followed a cohort of young children and reported that greater screen time at 24 and 36 months was associated with poorer performance on developmental screening measures at subsequent time points, using a cross-lagged design intended to examine directionality. The study has been widely cited and widely over-reported.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Madigan et al. (2019) is correlational, the reported effects are small, and screen time in early childhood is heavily confounded with family factors including household income, parental education, parental mental health, caregiver availability, family stress, and the number of adults present. Cross-lagged panel designs strengthen the directional argument but do not remove unmeasured confounding, and the outcome is a screening instrument rather than a clinical diagnosis. This study cannot support a causal warning to parents and must not be written as one. Reviewer should confirm the surrounding prose keeps the effect size and the confounding explicit.

Put plainly: what observational screen-time research can show is that families who use more screens differ, on average, from families who use fewer, on measures taken later. What it cannot show is that the screen caused the difference, and the most obvious alternative explanation is not subtle. A household where the television runs all day is quite likely a household under more strain, with fewer adults, less time, and more competing demands, and every one of those things is independently associated with developmental measures. The screen may be a marker of circumstances rather than the agent.

So here is the honest version, all in one place.

Under about 18 months, screens are a weak teacher relative to you. That part is well supported and is a claim about learning efficiency, not about harm. The largest real cost of screen media at this age is probably displacement: time spent on a screen is time not spent in the contingent back-and-forth that the first year runs on, and the screen also pulls adult attention away, which is a documented effect in its own right. If you watch with your baby, narrate, and connect what is on the screen to real objects in the room, you close a good part of the gap, because you have restored contingency to the situation.

And: if you need twenty minutes, take the twenty minutes.

I mean that without hedging. There is no evidence that occasional screen use in an otherwise responsive household damages a baby. There is a great deal of reason to think that a caregiver who is at the absolute end of their reserves, with no break available, is a real risk factor for the thing that actually matters, which is the quality and consistency of the relationship. A twenty-minute buffer that lets you eat, shower, make a phone call, or simply stop is not a failure of parenting. It is maintenance on the part of the system that does most of the work. The parent who puts a show on so they can be a functional human for the next three hours has, on the available evidence, made a good trade.

### Lesson 6.5 · The caregiver is part of the system

Every module of this course has, in some form, said the same thing: the mechanism of infant development is a responsive relationship. Which means that the state of the responsive adult is not a side issue, a nice-to-have, or a self-care aside to be tacked on at the end. It is a developmental input in exactly the same sense that everything else in this course has been.

That is the honest reason this lesson exists, and it is also why it is the last one.

Perinatal mood and anxiety disorders are common. Depression and anxiety occurring during pregnancy or in the year after birth affect a substantial minority of parents. The frequently cited figures put perinatal depression in the range of roughly one in seven to one in eight birthing parents, with anxiety disorders at a broadly comparable rate and often co-occurring, and with rates higher in some populations, including parents facing financial hardship, parents without support, parents of babies with medical complications, and parents with a prior history of depression or anxiety. Non-birthing parents, including fathers and adoptive parents, also experience perinatal depression at meaningful rates, which is under-recognised almost everywhere.

Three things follow, and I want to state them in order of importance.

**First: these conditions are highly treatable.** This is the part that gets lost. Perinatal depression and anxiety respond to treatment, including specific psychotherapies, medication where appropriate (including options compatible with breastfeeding, which a prescriber can discuss with you), and combinations of the two, alongside practical support. This is one of the more treatable things in medicine and one of the least treated, because people do not present.

**Second: this is not a character failure and it is not about how much you love your baby.** Perinatal mood disorders are not caused by insufficient devotion, insufficient gratitude, insufficient effort, or having wanted the baby less than you were supposed to. They occur in people who desperately wanted to be parents, who adore their babies, and who are working extremely hard. Feeling numb, or frightened, or trapped, or nothing at all, is a symptom, not a verdict on you.

**Third: asking for help is a caregiving act.** Not an indulgence, not a failure, not a detour from parenting. It is one of the highest-leverage things a caregiver can do for a baby, because it acts directly on the input that matters most.

There is a research literature behind this and I am going to describe it carefully, because it is easy to write in a way that harms the exact person it should be helping.

Goodman, Rouse, Connell and colleagues (2011) published a meta-analysis in Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review examining associations between maternal depression and child psychopathology, finding small to moderate associations across a large body of studies. Murray and Cooper's programme of work on postnatal depression and infant development documented associations with aspects of early interaction and later child outcomes.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** The maternal-depression-to-child-outcome literature is correlational. Depression is not randomly assigned; it co-occurs with poverty, partner absence, relationship conflict, social isolation, trauma history, and chronic illness, all of which independently predict child outcomes. Reported associations are small to moderate in size and describe group averages, not individual trajectories, and most children of depressed parents do not develop the outcomes studied. Reviewer must confirm that no sentence in this lesson can be read by a currently depressed parent as evidence that they have already damaged their baby. If any sentence is ambiguous on that point, cut it. The purpose of citing this literature is exclusively to justify treating parental mental health as worth resourcing, never to assign blame.

So the correct reading is this. Parental mental health matters enough to treat, promptly and properly, and that is the entire conclusion. It is not evidence that a depressed parent has harmed their child. A large part of the association appears to run through interaction and availability, which is precisely the thing that treatment restores. If you are unwell right now, the useful fact is not that this matters. You know it matters. The useful fact is that it is treatable and that treatment is the response.

**What to actually do.**

If any of this is landing close to home, the route is a clinician: your own doctor, the pediatrician at your baby's next visit (they will not be surprised, and many will ask you directly), a midwife or health visitor if you are still in that system, or a mental health professional directly.

You may encounter a short questionnaire in that process. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, developed by Cox, Holden and Sagovsky (1987), is a ten-item screening instrument used very widely in perinatal care. It is worth knowing two things about it. It is a screen, not a diagnosis: an elevated score means a conversation is warranted, not that anything is settled. And it should be administered and interpreted through a clinician rather than self-scored off a website, because the interpretation, and specifically the follow-up on the item about self-harm, is a clinical act.

Some plain signposting, without euphemism.

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, or thoughts of harming your baby, that is not a thing to sit with alone and it is not evidence that you are dangerous or a bad parent. Intrusive, unwanted, frightening thoughts about harm coming to the baby are extremely common in the postpartum period and are usually the opposite of intent, but they are still a reason to tell someone, because the right support exists and is effective. Contact your doctor or midwife, or go to an emergency department. In the US, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline is available by call or text, at any hour, and it is an appropriate route for a parent in crisis. If you are outside the US, your local emergency number and your health service's crisis line serve the same function. If you are ever afraid you might act, treat it as urgent and get to emergency care.

And to be completely clear about what this course is: it is educational material. It does not diagnose, it does not treat, and it is not a substitute for a clinician who knows you and your baby.

**Where to go next.**

Two things, and only two, because you do not need a reading list.

The CDC's "Learn the Signs. Act Early." programme provides free milestone checklists by age, a milestone tracker app, and clear guidance on what to do and who to contact if you have a concern. It is the best plain-language, no-cost, non-commercial developmental resource available and it is designed for exactly this purpose.

And your pediatrician or family doctor is the standing relationship. Not a resource for emergencies, a relationship over time. They see your baby repeatedly, they hold the whole picture, and the single most useful thing you can do with a worry is bring it to them early and let them tell you it is nothing. That is not a waste of their time. That is the job.

**The send-off.**

I want to finish by naming what this course has been trying not to do.

Almost all first-year parenting content is built on a quiet threat. There is a window, and it is closing. There are things you should be doing, and you are probably not doing enough of them. The critical period is now, the brain is being wired as you read, and every day you fail to optimise is a day you cannot get back. That framing sells extremely well, and it is not supported by the science it claims to be based on. Plasticity does not shut off at one, or at three. Development is not a race with a finish line in the first year. The idea that ordinary caregiving is insufficient without a technique is a marketing position, not a finding.

Here is what the evidence in this course actually converges on, and it is much smaller and much more forgiving than the internet suggests. Babies develop in the context of relationships that are responsive most of the time. Not all of the time. Most of it. The mechanism is unremarkable and constant: your baby does something, you notice and respond, they notice that you noticed. Repeated across thousands of ordinary moments, most of which nobody remembers, including you. Ruptures are normal. Repair is the developmentally significant part and you do it routinely without labelling it. The specific brand of the toy, the exact month of the milestone, the twenty minutes of television, the day you were too flattened to play: these are not the load-bearing parts.

You are the load-bearing part. And you already are one, which means the most important thing you can do is stay well enough to keep going.

So: you can put this down now. Your baby does not need you to have finished a course. They need you to be there, mostly, imperfectly, tomorrow. That is a much lower bar than you have been told, and you are already clearing it.

Thank you for spending this time with us. Go and be tired somewhere comfortable.

### Try this week

**1. The kitchen drawer swap.**
*What to do:* Put the battery-operated toys away for a few days. Offer, on the floor, a small collection of ordinary household objects: nesting measuring cups, a wooden spoon, a silicone spatula, a scarf, a clean empty tub with a lid, a ball, a metal bowl. Sit nearby and let your baby lead. Do not demonstrate unless they get stuck and frustrated.
*What you are looking for:* Longer engagement with one object than you expected. Repeated variations on a single action (banging on two surfaces, dropping from two heights, in and out of a container several times). Occasional glances up at you.
*What it means if nothing happens:* If your baby loses interest fast, that is about age and state, not about the objects. Very young babies do not sustain object play for long, and a tired or hungry baby will not engage with anything. Try again on a different day, at a different point in the wake window. It is also fine if your baby simply prefers one object and ignores the rest. That is a preference, not a failure of the activity.

**2. The gentle hide.**
*What to do:* With a baby somewhere past about six months who is sitting reasonably steadily, partly cover a toy they are interested in with a cloth, leaving a bit visible. See what happens. If they retrieve it, try covering it fully next time. If you like, and only in a light and unserious way, hide it under cloth A twice, then move it in full view to cloth B and see where they reach.
*What you are looking for:* Whether they pull the cloth off, and if you tried the A/B version, whether they reach to the old location.
*What it means if nothing happens:* A baby who does not search for a fully hidden object is showing you where their working memory currently is, which changes over months, not days. A baby who reaches back to A is doing the textbook thing, and per Diamond's account it reflects memory load and motor habit, not a missing understanding of objects. Neither result is a test outcome. Do not repeat this to get a better score, and stop the moment it stops being fun for them.

**3. Follow their attention, not yours.**
*What to do:* For a few minutes a day, deliberately reverse the usual direction. Instead of showing your baby things, watch what they are already looking at and comment on that. "You found the light." "That's the dog." If they look at something across the room, look at it too, then look back at them.
*What you are looking for:* Whether they check back to your face. Later in the year, whether they look where you look. Toward the end of the year, whether they start pointing, showing, or handing you things, and whether they check your reaction after doing it.
*What it means if nothing happens:* Gaze following and pointing arrive across a wide window, and plenty of babies are late to point and entirely typical. Nothing here is a screen and a single session shows you nothing. If, closer to fifteen to eighteen months, you are not seeing pointing, showing, or your baby checking your face when something surprising happens, that is a conversation to have with your pediatrician, calmly and early.

**4. One anchored sequence.**
*What to do:* Pick one part of the day and make its order reliable, without making its clock time reliable. Bedtime is the usual choice, but the pre-nap wind-down or the arrival-home sequence works just as well. Same steps, same order, same tone, whenever it happens.
*What you are looking for:* Over a week or two, signs of anticipation: settling earlier in the sequence, recognising the step that comes next, less resistance at the transition points.
*What it means if nothing happens:* Some babies show anticipation clearly, others do not show it in any way you can see, and a fortnight is a short window. Also, if the week involved illness, travel, teething, or a developmental leap, expect the pattern to vanish and return later. The sequence is not being graded and neither are you. If you miss it entirely for three nights, it is not undone.

**5. One honest ask.**
*What to do:* Ask one person for one specific, concrete thing this week. Not "let me know if you need anything," which is not an ask. Something with edges: two hours on Saturday morning, one shop run, one load of laundry, one phone call where you get to talk and they mostly listen. If your honest answer is that there is no one to ask, then make the ask a professional one: book the appointment you have been putting off, or raise it at your baby's next pediatric visit.
*What you are looking for:* Whether you can actually say it out loud. That is the entire exercise. The help is a bonus.
*What it means if nothing happens:* If you could not do it, that is worth noticing rather than criticising yourself for. Difficulty asking for help is extremely common in new parents and it is more common when someone is depressed or anxious, which is precisely the situation where it matters most. If this one was the hardest item on this list, please read Lesson 6.5 again and consider that a reason to talk to a clinician rather than a reason to try harder alone.

### What this module is not saying

- **Not saying that any screen exposure harms a baby.** The evidence supports a learning-efficiency gap (the video deficit effect) under about two years, and a displacement concern. It does not support the claim that occasional or moderate screen use damages a baby in an otherwise responsive household. Guilt about twenty minutes of television is not scientifically earned.

- **Not saying that "toxic stress" describes ordinary hard days.** In the Shonkoff/AAP framework, toxic stress specifically means strong, frequent, or prolonged stress-response activation *in the absence of adequate adult buffering*. Crying, frustration, separation, illness, vaccinations, daycare, and difficult days inside a caring relationship are not that. The term is widely misused and the misuse frightens parents for no reason.

- **Not saying that an ACE score predicts an individual child's future.** ACEs research describes population-level associations between cumulative adversity and average rates of later outcomes. As an individual-level predictor it performs poorly: most people with high scores do not develop the outcomes, and many with low scores do. No one should present a score to you as a forecast for your child.

- **Not saying that babies are born with innate moral judgment.** The famous helper/hinderer paradigm (Hamlin and colleagues), in which infants appear to prefer a character who helps a climber over one who hinders it, is frequently cited as evidence of innate morality. Lucca et al. (2025), a ManyBabies multi-lab replication with 1,018 infants, did not replicate the effect. This is listed here because "core knowledge" and "babies are born knowing" claims cluster around exactly the play and cognition content in Lesson 6.1, and this one specifically failed replication. `[failed-replication]`

- **Not saying that violation-of-expectation studies prove babies have adult-like object concepts in the first months.** The looking-time effects are widely observed, but their interpretation is contested and replication has been uneven. This course treats early object knowledge as an open question.

- **Not saying that expensive developmental toys outperform household objects.** There is no evidence that commercially marketed developmental toys produce better outcomes than a wooden spoon, a box, and a set of cups. Buy toys because they are pleasant, because they buy you a break, or because you want to. Not because a product claim implies your kitchen is developmentally insufficient.

- **Not saying that observational screen-time studies establish cause.** They establish that families differ. Household income, parental availability, family stress, and parental mental health confound the association and are independently linked to child outcomes.

- **Not saying that joint attention is a home screening tool.** Its predictive reputation comes substantially from autism-risk research in specialised samples. It is a thing to understand and support, not a checklist to administer to your baby.

- **Not saying that a depressed parent has damaged their child.** The literature linking parental depression to child outcomes is correlational, describes small to moderate group-level associations, and is heavily confounded. It is a reason to treat parental mental health seriously and quickly. It is not a reason for a struggling parent to feel they have already caused harm.

- **Not saying that this course can diagnose or treat anything.** It is educational. Clinical concerns belong with clinicians.

### Module summary

- Infant play across the first year moves roughly from mouthing, to banging, to container and dropping play, and the repetition you find tedious is the experimental method: same action, varied conditions.
- Object permanence develops gradually (Piaget, 1954), and the A-not-B error is best explained not as a missing concept but as immature working memory plus a strong motor habit (Diamond).
- Claims that infants show adult-like physical knowledge in the first months rest largely on violation-of-expectation looking-time studies, which are real observations under contested interpretation.
- The developmental value of a toy is roughly inverse to how much it does on its own. Objects that only do what the baby makes them do are the useful ones, and most of them are already in your kitchen.
- Joint attention is the shift from two-way (you and me) to three-way (you, me, and that thing) attention, and it emerges as gaze following, then point following, then, often around 9 to 12 months, the baby's own point.
- Twelve-month-olds point to share attention and interest, not only to request (Liszkowski et al., 2004), and they respond most when the adult shares the object *and* the feeling about it.
- Joint attention supports word learning because it solves the problem of working out which thing a speaker means. It is supported by following your baby's interest, not by drilling.
- Predictability regulates because it lets a baby generate expectations. Sequence and manner matter far more than clock times, and a late day in the usual order is still a predictable day.
- In the AAP/Shonkoff framework, the difference between tolerable and toxic stress is the presence or absence of a buffering adult relationship, not the severity of the event. Ordinary hard days are not toxic stress.
- Under about 18 months, screens teach poorly compared to a live person (the video deficit effect), which is why the AAP carves out video chat: it is contingent, and contingency is the missing ingredient.
- Observational screen-time findings such as Madigan et al. (2019) are small and confounded by family circumstances and cannot support a causal warning. If you need twenty minutes, take it.
- The caregiver is a developmental input. Perinatal depression and anxiety are common, are not a character failure, and are highly treatable, and asking for help is one of the highest-leverage caregiving acts available.
- Development is not a closing window. Responsive most of the time, with repair after ruptures, is the mechanism, and you are almost certainly already doing it.
