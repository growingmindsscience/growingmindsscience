## Module 2 · Reading Your Baby

Module 1 was about what is being built. This module is about how to see it happening. Your baby cannot tell you what they need in words, and will not be able to for a long time. But they are communicating constantly, from the first day, in a channel most of us were never taught to read: shifts in alertness, small movements of the hands and face, changes in breathing and color, the timing and texture of a cry. This module teaches you that channel. Not so you can decode your baby perfectly, because nobody does that, but so that the day feels less like weather happening to you and more like a conversation you are already part of.

### Lesson 2.1 · Behavioral states: the real schedule your baby is on

Let's start with the single most useful idea in newborn care, and one of the least talked about outside of clinical settings.

Your baby is not simply awake or asleep. Newborns move through a set of distinct, recognizable behavioral states, and which state your baby is in at any given moment determines almost everything about what they can do, what they need from you, and how they will respond to anything you try.

This is not a modern wellness idea. It comes out of careful observational work by Peter Wolff in the late 1950s and 1960s, who did something deceptively simple: he watched newborns for long stretches and recorded what he saw, rather than assuming that "awake" was one thing. What emerged were six states that show up reliably across babies, and that clinicians have used ever since. Wolff's descriptions were later built into the Brazelton Neonatal Behavioral Assessment Scale, which is the framework a lot of newborn nurses, pediatricians, and infant specialists are working from when they handle your baby in a way that looks almost like a conversation.

Here are the six.

**Quiet sleep.** Breathing is regular and deep. The face is still. There is very little movement apart from the occasional startle or sucking motion. This is the state that looks like the sleep in the parenting books. It is also the harder state to wake a baby from, and if you have ever tried to rouse a newborn for a feed and felt like you were trying to wake a stone, you were almost certainly working against quiet sleep.

**Active sleep.** Breathing is irregular. You will see eye movements under closed lids, small twitches, facial expressions that look like smiling or grimacing, brief fussing sounds. Many parents look at active sleep and conclude their baby is waking up, and pick them up, and are then confused when the baby is cranky. Newborns spend a much larger share of their sleep in active sleep than adults do in the equivalent state. A baby making noise is not automatically a baby who is done sleeping.

**Drowsy.** The in-between. Eyelids heavy or fluttering, gaze unfocused, movement slow and variable. This is a transition state, and it goes in both directions. A drowsy baby is on their way somewhere, and a fair amount of your influence happens here.

**Quiet alert.** Eyes open and bright. Body still. Breathing regular. Attention is available and pointed outward. This is the state where your baby looks at your face and actually looks, where they will track a slowly moving object, where they will turn toward your voice. It is the learning state, and I want to spend some time on it.

**Active alert.** Eyes open, but the body is busy. More movement, more vocalizing, less sustained attention. Fussing may be starting. This is a baby who is engaged but working harder to stay organized.

**Crying.** Its own state, with its own lesson. We will get there in 2.3.

Now, why does quiet alert matter so much?

Because attention, in a newborn, is expensive. Holding still, keeping the eyes open and focused, keeping breathing regular, and taking in the world all at once takes a great deal of physiological organization. Quiet alert is the state in which all of that is running smoothly at the same time. It is the state in which social interaction actually works: your baby can see your face, hold on to it, and respond to what you do. Everything Module 3 will say about early language, and everything Module 4 will say about connection, is happening in quiet alert.

Here is the part that surprises people. In the first weeks, quiet alert is not a large part of the day. A newborn may spend a relatively small fraction of a twenty-four hour period in it, and those windows may be a few minutes long. That is normal. It is not a sign of a low-engagement baby or a missed opportunity. The windows lengthen on their own over the first few months as the nervous system matures.

So the practical instruction is not "create more quiet alert time." It is: **notice it when it happens, and use it.** If your baby is lying there, still and wide-eyed, that is the moment to bring your face close, talk to them, let them look at you. Not the moment to run an errand while they are conveniently quiet. Nothing is lost if you do run the errand, to be clear. But if you have been wondering when the interacting is supposed to happen, this is when.

The second practical idea from state theory is about transitions, and this one reframes the whole first few months.

Most parenting advice talks about a schedule: feed at these times, nap at these times. Newborns do not have a schedule in that sense, and trying to impose one on a two-week-old mostly produces frustration in the adult. What newborns do have is a sequence. They move through states in a roughly ordered way, and the transitions are where your influence lives.

The transition from drowsy toward crying, for example, tends to run through active alert. Which means the moment to act is usually earlier than you think. A baby who is starting to move more, vocalize more, and lose their steady gaze is telling you that the alert window is closing. If you respond then, with a feed, a change of position, a quieter room, or sleep, you often skip crying entirely. If you wait until crying, you are now working with a much more disorganized system, and the intervention has to be bigger.

This is also why the same technique works brilliantly one hour and not at all the next. It is not that the technique stopped working. It is that the state changed. Bouncing a baby in active alert can help them organize. Bouncing a baby in quiet sleep will wake them up and make you both unhappy. Talking animatedly to a baby in quiet alert is delightful. Talking animatedly to a baby who is already overstimulated in active alert adds to the load.

So when someone tells you that a particular soothing method "doesn't work," the more accurate statement is usually that it did not match the state.

One more thing about states, and it matters for how you feel about all of this. State organization is a developmental achievement, not a personality trait. Very young babies, and especially babies born early, have less mature state control: they move between states abruptly, they have shorter alert windows, they are harder to rouse and harder to settle. This improves with time. If your baby is currently a chaotic mess of states, that is a nervous system that has not finished the work yet. It is not a preview of who they will be.

Learn the six states. Watch for quiet alert. Act at the transitions rather than at the crisis. That is most of the skill.

### Lesson 2.2 · Cues: how your baby says "more" and how they say "enough"

Once you can see states, the next layer is cues, and this is where a lot of parents have a genuinely useful experience of the world rearranging itself.

Babies signal, continuously, whether they want more of what is happening or less of it. The signals are small, they are physical rather than verbal, and once you know them you cannot unsee them. This vocabulary comes largely out of the same clinical tradition as the state model, particularly the Brazelton assessment framework and the newborn observation work that grew from it. It is a clinical framework rather than a single experiment, and I want to be honest about that: what follows is a well-established way of describing infant behavior used widely in newborn care, not a finding from one trial.

Broadly there are two families of cues: approach cues and stress cues. Some sources call them engagement and disengagement cues, which I like better, because "stress" makes it sound worse than it usually is.

**Approach cues** say: I am available, keep going. Watch for a smooth, steady gaze at your face. Eyes widening. Eyebrows lifting. The face softening or brightening. Hands coming toward the midline, or toward the mouth, in a relaxed way. Turning the head toward you or toward your voice. Reaching, once they are old enough. Smooth, unhurried movements of the arms and legs. Feeding rhythmically. Making soft sounds back at you when you speak.

**Disengagement cues** say: I need a break. This list is longer, and it is the one worth memorizing.

Gaze aversion is the headline one. Your baby, mid-interaction, turns their eyes or their whole head away from your face. Also: closing the eyes for a beat longer than a blink. Frowning. The face going still and blank. Hiccups. Yawning, which is often about arousal regulation rather than tiredness. Sneezing that is not about a tickle in the nose. Splayed, stiffened fingers, sometimes described as a stop sign made of a hand. Arms and legs going rigid, or arching the back away from you. Color change, a flush or a mottling or a paling around the mouth. Squirming and pushing away. Breathing that speeds up or turns irregular. Spitting up. Fussing, which is the last stop before crying.

Now the reframe, and it is the point of this lesson.

**Gaze aversion is not rejection. It is regulation.**

Interacting with an adult face is intense. Faces are high-information, high-arousal stimuli, and a baby's system responds to them strongly: heart rate rises, attention locks on, and arousal climbs. Babies do not yet have the ability to turn their arousal down internally the way you do. What they have instead is a body-based strategy, and it is a good one: they look away. Breaking the visual connection lowers the input, arousal comes back down, and then they look back.

This look away and look back pattern, repeated many times in a single interaction, is one of the earliest forms of self-regulation your baby has. It is a competence. It is your baby managing their own internal state with the tools available to them.

I emphasize this because the most common misread in early parenting is exactly here. A parent, often already worried they are not connecting, sees their baby turn away and reads it as disinterest or rejection, and the instinctive response is to work harder: move closer, get louder, get more animated, move into the baby's line of sight. Which raises the input at precisely the moment your baby was trying to lower it. Now the baby has to escalate to a bigger signal, arching or crying, to get the break they asked for politely thirty seconds ago.

The move instead is to back off, and to make backing off visible. Lean back a little. Go quiet. Stop the bouncing or the singing. Keep your hands where they are so your baby still feels supported, but stop adding. Then wait. In many cases you will get a few seconds of stillness and then your baby will turn back and re-engage on their own, and you can pick up right where you left off.

That whole loop, engage, disengage, break, re-engage, is what responsive interaction actually looks like at this age. It is not sustained eye contact for ten minutes. It is a series of short bursts with rests in between, and the rests are your baby's contribution to the conversation, not an interruption of it.

A few practical notes.

Cues cluster. One yawn is not much information. A yawn plus splayed fingers plus a turned head is a clear message. Look at the whole picture rather than hunting for a single tell.

Cues are individual. Some babies signal early, subtly, and consistently, and are easy to read once you know them. Others signal late and go from apparently fine to fully crying with very little warning in between. That difference is largely temperament and maturity, which is Lesson 2.4, and it is not a difference in how much your baby wants you.

Cues are also state-dependent. A baby in quiet alert who looks away is taking a breather. A baby in active alert who looks away is more likely finished. Same behavior, different meaning, depending on where the system already is.

And you will miss them. Constantly. Everyone does, including people who study this professionally. There is a body of work, which Module 4 will get into properly, suggesting that a substantial share of ordinary parent and infant interactions are out of sync at any given moment, and that what matters is not avoiding the mismatch but noticing and coming back. Reading cues well is not about a perfect hit rate. It is about having a second guess available when the first one is wrong.

One last thing. Feeding is where cues matter most concretely, because feeding is where adults are most tempted to override them. A baby who turns away from the bottle or breast, splays their hands, or arches is signalling a pause. Sometimes that pause means done, and sometimes it means give me a moment. Following the signal, offering again after a break, and stopping when the answer is consistently no is how a baby learns their own hunger and fullness are legible and respected. This is worth protecting even when a chart or a well-meaning relative says the bottle should be finished.

### Lesson 2.3 · Crying, and what it is actually for

Now the hard one.

Crying is your baby's most powerful signal, and it is designed to be. It is aversive on purpose. The acoustics of an infant cry are structured to reach into an adult nervous system and demand action. That is a feature. A newborn who could not compel a much larger, tired, distractible adult to attend to them would be in real trouble.

Which also means the distress you feel when your baby cries is not a defect in you. It is the system working. You are supposed to find it unbearable.

Let's start with the shape of it across the first months, because this single fact prevents an enormous amount of unnecessary suffering.

Crying in the first year is not flat. It follows a curve. It increases over the first weeks of life, peaks somewhere around five to eight weeks, and then declines. Ronald Barr's work is the main reference here, and it is the basis for the "period of PURPLE crying" framing you may have encountered in a hospital or pediatric office. This curve appears across very different cultures and caregiving arrangements, which is a strong hint that it is developmental rather than caused by anything a particular parent is doing.

For actual numbers, the best current source is a meta-analysis by Wolke, Bilgin and Samara, published in the Journal of Pediatrics in 2017, which pooled parent-diary data across a number of countries. Averaged across the sample, babies cried and fussed for roughly 110 to 118 minutes per day at one to two weeks of age, still around 110 minutes at six weeks, and around 70 minutes per day by ten to twelve weeks.

Two things about those numbers.

First, they include fussing, not just full crying, which is the right way to count it, because the fussing is what wears you down.

Second, and more important, they are averages, and the variation around them is enormous. The same meta-analysis found substantial differences between countries, and within any group there are babies far above and far below the mean. So the number to hold on to is not "two hours." It is: **two hours a day of crying and fussing in a six-week-old is within the ordinary range.** If you have been quietly assuming that a normal baby cries a little and yours cries a lot, that assumption may simply be wrong.

Now, the question underneath almost every crying question: does responding quickly make it worse?

You will hear that it does. You will hear that picking a baby up every time they cry teaches them to cry more, that you are reinforcing the behavior, that you are creating a baby who cannot settle alone.

The research most often cited against that goes back to Ainsworth and Bell's work in the early 1970s, which followed mother and infant pairs across the first year and reported that infants whose crying was responded to promptly in the early months cried less later in the first year, not more. Later work in that tradition has generally pointed the same direction.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** The Ainsworth and Bell "prompt responsiveness reduces later crying" finding has been directly challenged, including in re-analyses arguing the effect is weaker or absent, and the design is correlational: responsive parents differ from less responsive parents in many ways beyond response latency. This draft should present the finding as "the worry that responding creates more crying is not supported" rather than "responding reduces crying." Please confirm the current framing is adequately hedged.

So here is the honest version, and it is enough. **The claim that responding promptly to a young baby's cries makes them cry more, or makes them dependent, is not supported by the evidence we have.** You cannot spoil a baby in the first months. There is no mechanism for it. An infant this young does not have the cognitive machinery to form a manipulative strategy; they have a distress signal and no alternative. Respond as you see fit, on the timeline that works for your family, without carrying a fear of doing damage by comfort.

That is a different claim from saying you must reach every cry within seconds. You do not, and you cannot, and a baby who cries for two minutes while you finish something is not being harmed.

**A soothing repertoire.** No single technique works for every baby or for the same baby every time, so what you want is a list you can run through rather than a method you believe in.

Contact and containment: skin to skin, upright against your chest, or firm swaddling if your baby likes it and is not yet rolling. Motion: walking, slow rocking, a stroller, a car. Sound: shushing near the ear, a low hum, steady white noise or a fan, at a volume no louder than a shower. Sucking: the breast, a clean finger, a pacifier. Position: side-lying or across your forearm while awake and held, always returning to the back for sleep. Reduction: a dark quiet room, one adult, nothing added. Feeding, if it has been a while. Temperature and diaper, since it is embarrassing how often it is that.

And one that is not a technique: handing the baby to someone else. A fresh adult who is not carrying twenty minutes of accumulated failure often gets a different result, and this is not evidence that you were doing it wrong.

Try one thing at a time and give it a couple of minutes. A rapid cycle through six methods in ninety seconds is itself a source of stimulation.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Probiotics for colic, most commonly Lactobacillus reuteri, are widely marketed and were deliberately left out of the repertoire above. Trial results are genuinely mixed, with some positive findings in breastfed infants and null results in others, and formulations differ. This draft does not recommend them. Confirm that omission plus this flag is the right handling, and that no product-specific claim appears anywhere in the module.

**Now the part that matters most in this entire module.**

There will be nights when nothing works. Your baby will cry, you will have tried everything on the list twice, you will be running on almost no sleep, and you will feel something rise in you that frightens you. Rage, or panic, or a flash of an impulse you would never act on and cannot believe you had.

That feeling is common. It does not make you dangerous, and it does not make you a bad parent. What matters is what you do next.

**If you feel yourself getting close to losing control, put your baby down on their back in an empty crib or bassinet, walk out of the room, and close the door. Set a timer for five or ten minutes. Let your baby cry. Breathe, call someone, splash water on your face, sit on the floor. Then go back.**

A baby crying alone on their back in a safe, empty sleep space for ten minutes is not being harmed. Not at all. This is a safe outcome, and it is a good decision, and you should make it without guilt and without waiting until you are certain you need to.

**Never shake a baby. Not once, not gently, not to get their attention.** An infant's head is heavy relative to their neck strength, and their brain is fragile in a way that makes shaking capable of causing bleeding, permanent disability, and death, in seconds, with no visible injury on the outside. This is the mechanism behind abusive head trauma, and it is why the National Center on Shaken Baby Syndrome and the American Academy of Pediatrics build their prevention guidance around exactly the instruction above: the crying will stop on its own eventually, and your job in that moment is only to make sure everyone is safe until it does.

Tell every person who cares for your baby the same thing. Partners, grandparents, babysitters, anyone. The rule is: it is always fine to put the baby down and step away, and no one will be judged for it.

**When to call a clinician.** This course does not diagnose, and cannot. Please contact your pediatrician or seek urgent care if your baby's cry sounds unusually high-pitched, shrill, or weak and whimpery rather than robust; if crying comes with fever, poor feeding, vomiting, unusual floppiness or lethargy, difficulty waking, or a change in breathing or color; if there is a sudden dramatic change in the crying pattern; or simply if something feels wrong to you and you cannot say why. That last one is a legitimate reason to call. Parents notice things before they can articulate them, and a clinician would much rather see a well baby than miss a sick one.

Also, and separately: if you are experiencing persistent low mood, anxiety that will not switch off, intrusive frightening thoughts, or a sense that you are not yourself, tell your own doctor. Perinatal mood and anxiety conditions are common and treatable, and they are not a character failure.

### Lesson 2.4 · Temperament and goodness of fit

Last lesson in this module, and it is the one that tends to settle people.

Babies arrive different. Not slightly different. Meaningfully, observably different, from the first days, in ways that are not caused by anything you did.

The foundational work here is the New York Longitudinal Study, begun by Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess in the 1950s, which followed a cohort of children from infancy into adulthood. They were working against a strong assumption of their era, which was that a child's behavior was essentially a readout of parenting quality. What they found instead was that infants showed consistent individual behavioral styles very early, and that those styles shaped how the child met the world.

Thomas and Chess described a set of dimensions: activity level, rhythmicity or regularity of biological functions, approach or withdrawal to new things, adaptability to change, intensity of reaction, threshold of responsiveness, quality of mood, distractibility, and persistence or attention span. They also grouped children loosely as "easy," "difficult," and "slow to warm up," while noting that a large share of children did not fit neatly into any of the three.

I want to be careful with those labels, because they have aged badly in popular use. "Difficult" in the original sense meant irregular in biological rhythms, slow to adapt, intense in reaction, and prone to negative mood: a description of a behavioral profile, not a judgment about a child, and certainly not a prediction about the adult. A high-intensity, slow-to-adapt baby is a baby who feels things strongly and takes time to adjust. In a two-year-old that is exhausting. In a twenty-five-year-old it may read as passionate and thorough.

The most valuable idea Thomas and Chess produced is not the dimension list. It is **goodness of fit.**

Goodness of fit says that outcomes do not follow from temperament alone, and do not follow from the environment alone. They follow from the match between the two. The same baby thrives in one set of conditions and struggles in another, and the same set of conditions works for one baby and not their sibling.

A concrete version. A baby who is highly sensitive to sensory input and slow to adapt to novelty will do badly in a household with a lot of noise, frequent transitions, and many new faces per week. The same baby, in a predictable environment with advance warning before changes and time to observe before joining, may be entirely fine. Nothing about the baby changed. The fit changed.

That reframe does real work, in both directions. It gets you out of "what is wrong with my baby," because the answer is usually nothing. And it gets you out of "what am I doing wrong," because the answer is usually also nothing, and the actual question is what needs adjusting so these two particular people fit together better.

Practically, goodness of fit means adjusting your approach to the baby you actually have, not the one in the book. If your baby is intense and slow to adapt: fewer transitions, more warning, longer runway, more predictable rhythms. If your baby is low-intensity and slow to warm: more time to observe before joining in, and no pushing them into the middle of things. If your baby is high-activity and high-threshold, meaning it takes a lot to get a reaction: bigger, more vigorous input, more movement, and more physical play as they grow. If your baby is highly sensitive: dimmer, quieter, slower, one thing at a time.

Two other frameworks worth knowing.

**Jerome Kagan** studied what he called behavioral inhibition: a subset of infants, somewhere in the region of fifteen to twenty percent in his samples, who reacted strongly to novelty at around four months with vigorous motor activity and distress, and who were more likely to be shy and cautious in unfamiliar situations as toddlers and children. Kagan tied this to differences in how readily the threat-detection systems of the brain respond, and followed some participants for decades.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Temperament stability estimates, including Kagan's, are modest and are routinely overstated in popular writing. The honest version is that high-reactive infants are meaningfully more likely than low-reactive infants to be inhibited later, but most do not become inhibited adults, and the group-level association does not license individual prediction. Please check that this lesson never implies a given baby's outcome can be read from their infant temperament.

Which is exactly what I want to say plainly: behavioral inhibition in infancy shifts the odds. It does not set the outcome. Most highly reactive infants do not grow into anxious adults, and the trajectory is shaped substantially by what happens in between, including how much gentle, unforced exposure to new situations a child gets.

**Mary Rothbart's** framework is the one most current researchers use, and it is the most practically useful. Rothbart splits temperament into two components. **Reactivity** is how quickly and how strongly a baby's system responds, both to distress and to pleasure. **Regulation** is the developing capacity to modulate that response, which in infancy shows up as things like looking away, self-soothing, and orienting attention elsewhere. Her Infant Behavior Questionnaire, the measure most widely used in infant temperament research, resolves into broad factors usually described as negative affectivity, surgency or extraversion, and orienting or regulatory capacity.

The reason I like this framing for parents is that it separates two things that get confused. A baby can be highly reactive and well regulated, and look calm. A baby can be moderately reactive and poorly regulated, and look like a lot. Reactivity is largely constitutional and shows up early. Regulation develops, over years, and it develops in interaction with you. Which is where your influence actually sits: not in changing how strongly your baby feels things, but in being the external regulator while the internal one is being built. Module 4 is entirely about that.

**Differential susceptibility.** One more idea, offered as an orientation rather than a fact.

The traditional model of temperament is a vulnerability model: some children are more fragile, and adversity harms them more. Differential susceptibility, associated with Jay Belsky, Marian van IJzendoorn, and separately with Thomas Boyce and Bruce Ellis in the "orchid and dandelion" framing, proposes something different. It suggests that the same children who do worse in poor environments also do better than average in good ones, because what they are is more sensitive to environment in general, for better and for worse.

> **REVIEW FLAG:** Differential susceptibility is a contested framework. The candidate-gene versions of it in particular have had serious replication problems, and much of the supporting literature is observational with substantial risk of publication bias and analytic flexibility. It is included here as an orienting idea only and is explicitly not presented as established. Confirm this level of hedging is acceptable, or cut the subsection.

I include it because parents of sensitive, reactive babies are often told, in effect, that their child is fragile. Differential susceptibility at least raises the possibility that high sensitivity is not simply a liability. But it is a live scientific argument, not a settled finding, and I am not going to sell it to you as more than that.

**A closing point on where temperament comes from.** Temperament has a substantial constitutional and heritable component. It is not produced by your parenting, and it is not produced by anything that happened during pregnancy that you are currently lying awake reviewing. It is also not fixed: it interacts with experience across development, expressions of it change with age, and the same underlying tendency looks very different at six months, six years, and sixteen.

Your baby's temperament is information about how to parent them. It is not a verdict on them, and it is not a report card on you.

### Try this week

**1. Run a state watch.**
*What to do:* Once, for about twenty minutes, watch your baby without trying to change anything. Note which of the six states they are in and when it shifts. Do it during a normally busy part of the day rather than a calm one.
*What you are looking for:* The transitions. Specifically, what happens right before a shift: the change in breathing, the movement picking up, the gaze going unfocused.
*If nothing happens:* If your baby stays in one state the entire time, you have learned something useful about their rhythms and you can try again at a different hour. If you cannot tell the states apart yet, watch breathing regularity first. It is the easiest single indicator and it separates quiet sleep from active sleep immediately.

**2. Find quiet alert, and use it.**
*What to do:* When you catch your baby eyes open, body still, breathing steady, drop what you are doing. Bring your face about eight to twelve inches from theirs and just look at them. Talk quietly, or say nothing. Let them look back.
*What you are looking for:* Sustained gaze at your face, eyebrows lifting, a stilling of the body, maybe a small sound. Note roughly how long it lasts before they look away.
*If nothing happens:* In the early weeks these windows can be very brief and may not appear at a convenient time for days. A baby who does not lock on is often just not in that state, or is younger than the visual system needs. Try again tomorrow. There is nothing to make up for.

**3. Practice the back off and wait.**
*What to do:* During any face-to-face interaction, the moment your baby looks away, stop. Stop talking, stop moving, lean back slightly, keep your hands supporting them. Say nothing for ten to twenty seconds.
*What you are looking for:* Whether they turn back to you on their own, and how long it takes. Most parents are surprised how short the break needs to be.
*If nothing happens:* If they do not turn back within thirty seconds or so, the interaction is over for now rather than paused, and ending it is the correct read. If they escalate to crying, you were later than you thought, and the cue you missed came earlier. Look for it next time.

**4. Build your two-column cue list.**
*What to do:* On your phone, keep a running note with two headings: "more" and "enough." Over several days, add the specific things your baby actually does, in your words. Not the textbook list. Theirs.
*What you are looking for:* Repeats. After a few days you will notice that your baby has two or three signature disengagement cues they use consistently, and knowing those is worth more than knowing all twelve generic ones.
*If nothing happens:* Some babies genuinely signal late and subtly, and a thin list after a week is real information, not a failure of observation. If that is your baby, shift your attention to timing and context instead: how long they usually last before they are done, and which situations use them up fastest.

**5. Have the step-away conversation before you need it.**
*What to do:* Today, while nobody is crying, say out loud to your partner or whoever else cares for your baby: if either of us feels close to losing it, we put the baby down on their back in the empty crib and walk out for a few minutes, and nobody gets judged for it. Decide now where the safe spot is.
*What you are looking for:* Agreement, spoken, in advance. That is the entire goal. Plans made calmly are the ones that get used at three in the morning.
*If nothing happens:* If you are doing this alone with no one to have the conversation with, say it to yourself and write it down somewhere you will see it. Then, separately, find one person you can call at a bad hour, and ask them this week whether you can. Having the number matters more than using it.

### What this module is not saying

- **Not saying that how much your baby cries reflects your parenting.** Crying volume in the first months is driven largely by development and by individual differences the baby brought with them. The curve peaks around six weeks in babies with very different parents in very different countries. A baby who cries a lot is not evidence of a parent doing it wrong.
- **Not saying that a "difficult" temperament predicts a difficult person.** The Thomas and Chess labels describe a behavioral style in childhood, not a destiny. Intensity, persistence, and slow adaptation are not defects, and they read completely differently at twenty-five than at two.
- **Not saying there is a schedule every baby should be on.** Newborns have state sequences, not clock schedules. Published routines describe averages and cannot describe your baby. Being off any given schedule is not a problem to be corrected.
- **Not saying that responding promptly creates dependence.** There is no evidence that comforting a young baby quickly produces more crying, more neediness, or a child who cannot settle. You cannot spoil an infant with responsiveness.
- **Not saying you must catch every cue.** Missing cues is the normal condition of parenting. Repair matters more than accuracy, which Module 4 covers.
- **Not saying that a baby who looks away is rejecting you.** It is regulation, and it is a skill.
- **Not saying sensitive babies are fragile.** And also not asserting the reverse. Differential susceptibility is a contested idea, flagged as such above.
- **Not saying probiotics, gripe water, or any product treats colic.** The trial evidence for probiotics is mixed and this course does not recommend them. Ask your pediatrician.
- **Not saying this module can tell you whether your baby is unwell.** It cannot. Lesson 2.3 lists the signs that warrant a call, and "something feels wrong" is on that list for a reason.

### Module summary

- Your baby is not just awake or asleep. Six behavioral states, described by Wolff and built into the Brazelton framework, determine what your baby can do at any moment.
- Quiet alert is the learning state: eyes open, body still, attention available. In early weeks it is brief and infrequent, and that is normal.
- Notice quiet alert and use it. Do not try to manufacture more of it.
- Act at state transitions, not at the crisis. The window to prevent crying usually opens before crying starts.
- A technique that worked yesterday and fails today usually failed because the state changed, not because the technique is wrong.
- Babies signal continuously with approach and disengagement cues. Learn your baby's two or three signature ones.
- Gaze aversion is arousal regulation, not rejection. The correct response is to reduce input and wait, not to work harder.
- Crying follows a developmental curve that rises to a peak around five to eight weeks and then falls, across cultures.
- Wolke, Bilgin and Samara (2017) found average crying and fussing of roughly 110 to 118 minutes a day at one to two weeks, falling to about 70 minutes by twelve weeks, with very wide variation.
- Responding promptly to a young baby does not create more crying or more dependence. You cannot spoil a baby in the first months.
- If you feel close to losing control, put your baby on their back in an empty crib, leave the room, and take a few minutes. Never shake a baby, ever, for any reason.
- Temperament is real, arrives early, and is not your doing. What matters is goodness of fit: adjusting the environment and your approach to the baby you actually have.

### Citations

1. Wolff, P. H. (1959). Observations on newborn infants. *Psychosomatic Medicine*. And Wolff, P. H. (1966). The causes, controls, and organization of behavior in the neonate. *Psychological Issues*. Original description of infant behavioral states. `[supported]`
2. Brazelton, T. B., & Nugent, J. K. *Neonatal Behavioral Assessment Scale* (multiple editions). Clinical framework for infant state and cue observation. `[supported]` as a clinical framework; not a single experimental finding.
3. Wolke, D., Bilgin, A., & Samara, M. (2017). Systematic review and meta-analysis: Fussing and crying durations and prevalence of colic in infants. *The Journal of Pediatrics*. Pooled parent-diary data across countries; roughly 110 to 118 minutes per day at 1 to 2 weeks, declining to about 70 minutes by 12 weeks. `[supported]`
4. Barr, R. G. Work on the normal crying curve and the "period of PURPLE crying" framing (multiple papers and the associated prevention program). Early-infancy crying rises to a peak around 5 to 8 weeks and declines, across cultures. `[supported]`
5. Ainsworth, M. D. S., & Bell, S. M. (1972), and related Ainsworth-tradition work. Reported that prompt maternal response to crying in early months was associated with less crying later in the first year. `[correlational]` and actively debated; see the review flag in Lesson 2.3.
6. Thomas, A., & Chess, S. New York Longitudinal Study; *Temperament and Development* (1977). Temperament dimensions and the goodness-of-fit concept. `[supported]` as a framework.
7. Kagan, J. Work on behavioral inhibition, including longitudinal follow-up of high-reactive and low-reactive infants. Early reactivity to novelty is associated with later inhibited behavior; the association is real but modest and does not support individual prediction. `[attenuated]`
8. Rothbart, M. K. Infant Behavior Questionnaire and the reactivity and regulation framework. The dominant current model of infant temperament. `[supported]`
9. Belsky, J., & van IJzendoorn, M. H.; Ellis, B. J., & Boyce, W. T. Differential susceptibility and biological sensitivity to context ("orchid and dandelion"). Proposes that more susceptible children fare worse in poor environments and better in good ones. `[contested]`
10. National Center on Shaken Baby Syndrome; American Academy of Pediatrics guidance on abusive head trauma prevention. Never shake an infant; if overwhelmed, place the baby safely on their back and step away. `[supported]` and included here as safety guidance.
