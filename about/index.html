Science, for the
people living it.

Growing Minds Science exists so parents can raise their children with more clarity and less shame, using developmental research in language that holds up in real family life.

Why it exists

Parents don’t need more pressure. They need better explanations.

Most parenting content is either rigid scripts, vague slogans, or a single personal philosophy with very strong opinions.

Meanwhile, the actual research on early development is rich, careful, and full of things that would help parents, but it sits inside academic papers most parents never see.

Growing Minds Science is the bridge. It translates developmental science into classes and tools that respect parents as adults, recognize how exhausting early childhood is, and stay calm about uncertainty when uncertainty is honest. That includes the classes, the free milestone tracker, and Number Path, a ten-minute at-home check-in that places a child on the counting ladder and turns the research on how children learn to count into games a parent can actually play.

Matthew McArthur

Child Development Specialist and parent coach, Los Angeles. Alongside Growing Minds Science, he teaches an Infant and Toddler Socialization class.

At San Diego State University, Matthew studied how parent stress, changes to the home, screen time, and back-and-forth interaction shape language and cognitive development from 6 months to 5 years, after working with developmental researchers at UCLA during the pandemic. With Margaret Friend he wrote “Why Early Chatter Matters: The Role of Language in Shaping Futures” for Frontiers for Young Minds, a journal written for families and reviewed by young readers.

That work includes two years in state-funded early intervention, coaching families with children under 3 who were experiencing delays, more than 100 families in Los Angeles in all, and six years studying the developmental science behind them. The throughline is the same as everything else here: take what the research actually says, then translate it into something a parent can use on a Tuesday.

How it teaches

  • Translate the research, don’t replace itPlain language, with honesty about what the evidence is and isn’t. No invented certainty, no oversimplified hooks.
  • Respect the parentParents are adults. The work assumes you’re already trying hard, already paying attention, and already capable of nuance.
  • Mental models over scriptsThe goal is a clear way to understand what’s happening, not memorized phrases for every situation. Mental models travel; scripts don’t.
  • Calm by defaultNo urgency manufactured for engagement. No shame. The tone is what a thoughtful friend who happens to know the research would sound like.
  • Built for ordinary daysEvery recommendation has to work on a normal weekday, between transitions, meals, work, and the actual capacity of tired adults.
